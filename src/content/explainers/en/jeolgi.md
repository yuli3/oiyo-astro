If you think of the solar terms as an old calendar, you have half of it right. The 24 solar terms were not built by watching the moon. They **measure the position of the sun as an angle**. That is why they sit inside a lunisolar calendar and yet track the solar year almost exactly.

This article does not introduce the terms as folklore. It follows the definition: what a solar term actually is, why there are 24, and why in Saju a month turns on a solar term rather than on a birthday.

## 1. A solar term is an angle, not a date

Seen from Earth, the sun traces one full circuit against the stars over a year. That apparent path is the **ecliptic**, and how far along it the sun has travelled, measured as an angle, is the **solar longitude**.

The 24 solar terms divide that longitude into **24 steps of 15°**. Fifteen times twenty-four is 360 — exactly one circuit.

- Spring equinox = 0°
- Summer solstice = 90°
- Autumn equinox = 180°
- Winter solstice = 270°

That the four cardinal points land exactly on 0°, 90°, 180° and 270° is not a coincidence. The vernal equinox is the origin of the coordinate.

One common misunderstanding dissolves here. **A solar term's date shifting by a day from year to year is not calendrical sloppiness.** Earth's orbital period is about 365.2422 days, which does not divide evenly into whole days. A term is defined as *the moment the sun reaches that angle*, so that moment drifts about six hours later each year against the civil calendar until a leap day pulls it back. It is the calendar that wobbles, not the term.

For that reason solar terms are published as **times**, not dates. The Korea Astronomy and Space Science Institute (KASI) announces the entry moment to the minute — the start of spring in 2000, for instance, fell at 21:40 KST on 4 February. The Saju engine on this site uses that entry moment, not the date.

## 2. Jeol and junggi — the 24 are really 12 + 12

The 24 terms are not a uniform run of 24. **Jeol (節) and junggi (中氣) alternate.**

| Type | Count | Role | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Jeol (節) | 12 | Opens a **month boundary** | Start of Spring, Awakening of Insects, Pure Brightness … |
| Junggi (中氣) | 12 | Marks the **middle** of a month | Rain Water, Spring Equinox, Grain Rain, Grain Full … |

The distinction is structural, not decorative.

**First, a Saju month pillar changes on a jeol.** In Korean four-pillars astrology the month branch turns at the entry moment of a solar term, not on the first day of a calendar month. Start of Spring (315°) begins the Tiger month, and the branch advances once every 30° from there. That is why someone born on 3 February and someone born on 5 February carry different month pillars — and why two people born on the same 4 February can differ, depending on which side of the entry moment they fall.

**Second, junggi decides leap months.** A lunar month runs about 29.53 days while the interval between solar terms is about 30.44 days. Because of that mismatch, a lunar month occasionally contains **no junggi at all**, and that month becomes the leap month. The rule has a name: **無中置閏 (no-junggi leap placement)**. Leap months are not inserted arbitrarily.

## 3. Why Start of Spring falls in early February

"If Start of Spring opens the season, why does it land in the coldest part of the year?" It is the first question almost everyone asks.

The 24 terms divide the year by the **structure of daylight**, not by temperature. The winter solstice (270°) is the shortest day; the spring equinox (0°) is when day and night are equal. The **midpoint between them** is 315° — Start of Spring. So it does not mark the day things get warm. It marks the point where the lengthening of daylight has passed the halfway mark.

Temperature lags because land and ocean release heat slowly (seasonal lag). The coldest stretch is therefore not the solstice but Minor and Major Cold, and the hottest is not the summer solstice but Major Heat. The system already knew this and wrote the lag into its names: Minor and Major Heat follow the summer solstice, Minor and Major Cold follow the winter one.

## 5. Whose climate is this?

The names reflect the climate of the **North China Plain** — the farming calendar of the middle and lower Yellow River. On the Korean peninsula some names sit oddly against lived experience: the timing implied by Awakening of Insects, or the fieldwork around Pure Brightness, shifts with latitude and terrain.

Climate change adds to the gap. For a growing number of terms, the natural event in the name and the actual timing have come apart. **Read as a weather forecast, the terms are drifting; read as a scale of solar position, they remain exact.** Fifteen degrees of longitude does not move when the climate does.

Knowledge of the solar terms was inscribed on the UNESCO Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2016. The inscribed title — "The Twenty-Four Solar Terms, knowledge in China of time and practices developed through observation of the sun's annual motion" — names **astronomical observation** as the basis of the listing itself.

## 6. How this site computes them

The Saju engine here does not look up a table of solar term dates. It **computes the solar longitude directly**, using the standard algorithm for the sun's apparent longitude (Meeus), and cuts the month branch every 30° starting from 315°.

The computation is checked against the entry moments published by KASI — 168 measured values across 2000–2028 serve as the reference. The advantage is that years outside any table still compute; the limit is that precision can only be confirmed inside the range a table covers.
