Plenty of writing uses circadian rhythm as **another name for the sleep cycle**. That is half right. Sleep is simply the most visible of the many things riding on this rhythm.

## 1. Sleep is not the only thing on the rhythm

Things that rise and fall over a day:

- **Core body temperature** — lowest in the small hours, highest toward evening.
- **Melatonin** — starts rising as it gets dark, peaks in the middle of the night.
- **Cortisol** — climbs sharply just before waking (the cortisol awakening response).
- **Cognitive performance** — alertness, reaction speed and working memory differ by hour.

So "I can't work at night" may not be about willpower. It may mean **your body is in a different state at that hour.**

## 2. There is more than one clock

The master clock is the **suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN)** in the hypothalamus. It takes light information from the eye and sets the time across the body.

But clocks are not only there. **Peripheral tissues — liver, heart, muscle, fat — each carry their own clock.** The work uncovering how these molecular clocks operate was awarded the 2017 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

A practical conclusion follows. **The master clock and the peripheral clocks are set by different signals.** The SCN responds to light; the liver and metabolic tissues respond strongly to **meal timing**. That is why eating late matters — a master clock set by light and peripheral clocks set by food fall out of step.

## 3. The intrinsic period is not 24 hours

The human endogenous circadian period is not exactly 24 hours but **about 24.2 hours on average**, measured in environments stripped of time cues.

So it drifts a little later each day, and **needs re-entraining daily.** Light does that work.

- **Morning light** pulls the clock earlier.
- **Evening and night light** pushes it later.
- The same brightness has **opposite effects depending on when** you get it.

"Blue light" gets the attention, but strictly it is not only a matter of wavelength. **Timing, intensity and duration** decide together. A bright screen in a dark room before bed is the problem; screens during the day actually help.

## 4. What happens when it falls out of step

A rhythm out of step with external time is **circadian misalignment**. Jet lag, shift work and social jetlag are the familiar cases.

Unfavourable metabolic and cardiovascular figures have been reported repeatedly in shift workers. But **causation is hard to pin down here** — shift work comes tangled with sleep loss, meal timing and social conditions.

## 5. What you can actually do

- **Get light after waking.** A window or outdoors is far stronger than indoor lighting.
- **Fix the wake time.** Easier to control than bedtime, and more effective.
- **Reduce bright light at night.** Intensity and timing come before wavelength.
- **Keep meal times regular.** That is the signal to the peripheral clocks.
- If daytime sleepiness is unbearable or you cannot fall asleep, **have a professional look at sleep disorders.**
