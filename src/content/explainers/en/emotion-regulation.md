"Good at regulating emotion" is usually taken to mean **good at holding it in**. What the research says is close to the opposite. Holding it in is one strategy among several, and **not a well-performing one**.

## 1. Regulation is not only something you do after the feeling arrives

James Gross's **process model** follows the order in which an emotion is built and marks five points where regulation can enter. Its usefulness is in showing that **regulation is not restricted to the moment after a feeling has grown**.

| Point | What you do | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Situation selection | Decide whether to enter the situation at all | Not taking the meeting that drains you |
| Situation modification | Change the situation itself | Having the conversation with a third person present |
| Attentional deployment | Move what you attend to | Focusing on the content rather than the tone |
| Cognitive change | Reinterpret what the situation means | "I was dismissed" → "they were in a hurry" |
| Response modulation | Handle the response already produced | Keeping your face still, slowing your breath |

The first four operate **before the feeling grows**. Only the last handles a feeling that already exists — and that last box is what most people mean by "emotion regulation". It is the latest and most expensive point in the chain.

## 2. Reappraisal and suppression are not the same move

Gross and John measured two strategies separately.

- **Cognitive reappraisal**: reinterpret what the situation means — the "cognitive change" point above.
- **Expressive suppression**: leave the feeling as it is and keep it off your face — "response modulation".

Their results diverged. **Suppression reduces expression but does little to the felt emotion.** It also carries costs that have shown up repeatedly: weaker memory for the moment, and greater distance from the person you are with, because resources go into the concealing.

Reappraisal changes the felt emotion itself, and those side costs are not observed in the same way.

One common belief dissolves here. **The person who holds it in is not the person regulating well.** Quiet on the outside and settled on the inside are different states, and the one who holds it in longer may end up further from the people around them.

## 3. But which strategy is good depends on the situation

None of this makes reappraisal always better. What regulation research has emphasised since is **context fit**.

Where a situation can be changed, changing it beats reappraising it. Reframing something unjust as "they must have had their reasons" is less regulation than **a way of leaving the problem in place**. Where a situation cannot be changed — something already past, something outside your remit — reappraisal earns its keep.

The same holds for suppression. Briefly holding a feeling back when showing it here would do harm is reasonable. The problem is **when it becomes the default**.

## 4. Suppression versus regulation

Suppression, in the broader sense, denies the feeling itself. Regulation acknowledges the feeling and works on what it does to your behaviour and your relationships.

Where that difference becomes concrete is **naming**. If you can tell whether what you feel is anger, or hurt, or fear, more of it becomes workable. Left lumped together as "in a bad mood", there is no place to put your hand.

## 5. Something to try

- Take a recent moment when feeling ran high, and mark **which of the five points** you could have acted at.
- Was what you used reappraisal, or suppression?
- Was that situation **changeable**? If it was, modifying it may have beaten reappraising it.

If swings in feeling are disrupting daily functioning, talking to a professional beats collecting more strategies.
