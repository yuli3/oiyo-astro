> **Summary**
>
> Attachment theory developed through the work of John Bowlby and Mary Ainsworth. Early caregiving experiences may relate to later relationships, but they are not a destiny chart for adult romance. Alongside categorical “styles,” researchers use the continuous dimensions of attachment anxiety and avoidance.

## Key concepts

- **Security:** expecting the other person to respond when needed, while maintaining both closeness and autonomy.
- **Attachment anxiety:** concern about rejection or abandonment and heightened vigilance to relationship signals.
- **Attachment avoidance:** discomfort with closeness or depending on other people.

Categories such as secure, anxious, avoidant and fearful-avoidant make explanations easier, but scores vary by degree and may differ across relationships and situations. None is a label for a person's worth or courage.

[Fraley's research overview](https://labs.psychology.illinois.edu/~rcfraley/attachment.htm) explains adult anxiety and avoidance dimensions and developmental research. Observational classifications of infants and adults' self-reports about relationships differ in what and how they measure. Do not treat adult fearful-avoidant descriptions as the same test result as infant disorganization, or infer childhood abuse from current answers. The [researcher consensus paper on infant disorganization](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5600694/) also cautions against reading that classification as a fixed trait, clinical diagnosis or definitive indicator of abuse.

## Childhood and adult relationships

Relationships with early caregivers may contribute to attachment representations, but later relationship experiences and living conditions also need consideration. Longitudinal research examines stability and change in attachment-related tendencies and associations between early experiences and later relationships.

A [longitudinal study of early experiences and adult attachment](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3885143/) examines associations involving caregiving environments and later social experiences. Do not turn an observational association into an established cause in one person's life or a verdict assigning responsibility to their caregiver.

Childhood experiences alone therefore cannot diagnose current conflict, abuse history or mental-health status, or predict a particular romantic outcome. Neither unsupported population percentages for styles nor a fixed deadline for “becoming secure” can be generalized.

## The possibility of change

Expectations and responses in relationships may change through new relationship experiences, self-understanding, counseling or therapy. The pace and direction differ between individuals; this should not be explained by requiring one partner to act as a therapist. If recurring anxiety or avoidance involves substantial distress or safety concerns, consider seeking help from a qualified professional rather than drawing conclusions from a self-administered style quiz.

An [open manuscript of a longitudinal study on life events and attachment change](https://psycnet.apa.org/manuscript/2020-59857-001.pdf) discusses individual differences in the size and persistence of changes after events. Its observation period is not a treatment duration, and the study is not a treatment experiment testing mindfulness combined with a particular kind of partner.

## What does the current OIYO result represent?

The current OIYO activity uses 12 OIYO-authored items, six each for attachment anxiety and avoidance. It is not an official ECR, ECR-R or ECR-RS instrument. It is a draft awaiting independent review of item content validity, similarity and translation equivalence. Referring to a research model does not certify the accuracy of these items.

Responses run from 1 to 5. Items designated for reverse scoring use `6 − response`. If S is the sum for one axis, the displayed value is `(S − 6) ÷ 24 × 100`, rounded. The examples below show values after any required reverse scoring.

| Example responses for one axis | Sum of six items | Displayed value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| All scored 1 | 6 | 0% |
| All scored 3 | 18 | 50% |
| All scored 5 | 30 | 100% |

50% is not the 50th population percentile or the probability of an unstable relationship. It is the midpoint of this activity's response scale. The current calculation does not assign four styles or apply clinical cutoffs. Do not combine answers about different relationships or times into a fixed identity for one person.

| What an OIYO result can help you examine | What this result alone cannot establish |
| --- | --- |
| How concerns about closeness or distance appeared in these answers | Specific childhood caregiving experiences or whether abuse occurred |
| Which relationship and situation you pictured while answering | Someone else's hidden intentions or a couple's compatibility |
| Where the description does not fit actual experience | Diagnosis, treatment selection or relationship safety |

These tables explain the current code and the editorial limits on interpretation. They are not norms or findings from a validation study. Do not attribute violence, control or threats to an attachment style, or assume that conversation aimed at changing the other person must solve the situation.

### Frequently asked questions

**Q: Must anxious and avoidant people make each other unhappy?**

A: The two tendencies can produce a pursuit-and-distancing pattern during conflict, but that is not inevitable. Consider communication, relationship context, safety and mutual respect together.

**Q: Can attachment styles change?**

A: Attachment-related tendencies show some stability, but are not immutable. There is no single method or deadline for change that applies to everyone.
