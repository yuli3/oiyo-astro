Talk about hormones tends to **begin and end with nicknames**: dopamine is pleasure, serotonin is happiness, oxytocin is love, cortisol is stress. This article starts by asking how far those hold.

## 1. First, hormones and neurotransmitters are different

- A **hormone** is released by a gland and travels **in the bloodstream** to a distant target. Cortisol, insulin and oestrogen work this way.
- A **neurotransmitter** acts in the **synaptic gap** between neurons. Dopamine and serotonin belong mainly here.

The boundary is not absolute — both also act hormonally elsewhere in the body. But **"happiness hormones" is a convenience phrase**, not a physiological category.

## 2. Dopamine is not the pleasure chemical

The most widespread misunderstanding. Dopamine relates to **wanting more than liking**, and to **prediction error**.

The key observation: the dopamine signal rises when a reward is **better than expected**, and does **not** rise when it is exactly as expected. When it is worse, the signal drops. What dopamine carries is not the size of the pleasure but **how far things departed from expectation**.

So dopamine is less the substance that makes you feel good than the one that **makes you move toward something**. Blocking dopamine in rats leaves the reaction to sweetness intact while reducing the effort to obtain it — an observation that supports the distinction.

## 3. "Depression is caused by low serotonin" is poorly supported

A long-standing account holds that depression arises from a shortage of serotonin in the brain, and that this is why SSRIs work. **That chemical-imbalance theory is judged to rest on weak evidence.**

An umbrella review published in 2022 synthesised the several lines of evidence linking serotonin to depression and reported **no consistent evidence that lowered serotonin activity or concentration causes depression**.

One caution. This is **not the claim that antidepressants do not work.** How a drug works and whether it works are different questions. Do not use this paragraph as grounds to stop treatment — changes to medication belong with the prescribing clinician.

## 4. Oxytocin is not simply the love hormone either

Oxytocin relates to bonding and trust, but **whom the bond is with moves along with it.** Studies have found it heightening attachment to an in-group while **also strengthening wariness of, or favouritism against, an out-group**.

Oxytocin is thus less a substance that increases love than one that **sharpens social boundaries**.

## 5. A "dopamine detox" does not lower dopamine

A popular practice: cut off stimulation for a few days to "reset" dopamine receptors.

The phrase borrows neuroscience vocabulary, but **there is no evidence it lowers dopamine levels or resets receptors.** Dopamine is constantly needed for movement, motivation and learning; it is not something to abstain from.

That said, **the practice is not meaningless.** Reducing short, intense stimulation — infinite scroll, notifications — does restore attention and the tolerance for boredom. The effect is real; what explains it is not a dopamine reset but **a rearrangement of habit and attention**.

## 6. Principles for reading

- **Accounts that pair one molecule with one emotion are almost always wrong.** The same molecule does different work by region and context.
- **Be wary of claims that a blood level explains your mood.** What happens inside the brain is not well captured by a blood test.
- If changes in mood, sleep or appetite persist, **see a clinician before reaching for a hormonal explanation.**
