Burnout is not another word for being very tired. It is a concept with **a definition, an instrument that measures it, and a place in official classification** — and all three often differ from what is popularly assumed.

## 1. The WHO does not classify burnout as an illness

Start with the most misunderstood point. In 2019 the WHO included burnout in the eleventh revision of the International Classification of Diseases, but classified it as an **occupational phenomenon, not a medical condition**. It sits under code QD85, in the chapter on factors influencing health status — not in a disease chapter.

The definition is narrow too. ICD-11 describes burnout as **a syndrome resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed**, and states that it should be applied **only in the occupational context** and not to describe experiences in other areas of life.

What that means in practice is clear:

- Burnout is **not a diagnosis.** "I was diagnosed with burnout" does not hold.
- "Parental burnout" or "relationship burnout" are metaphors. Not wrong as language, but not the ICD-11 definition.
- If symptoms run deep, the priority is not the label but **distinguishing it from depression, anxiety and sleep disorders**, which are real diagnoses with different treatment paths.

## 2. It is defined by three axes

The standard definition comes from the instrument Christina Maslach built in 1981 — the Maslach Burnout Inventory. Three axes:

| Axis | What it is | Common misreading |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Emotional exhaustion | Emotional energy run down | Often taken on its own as "burnout" |
| Cynicism / depersonalisation | Pulling away from work and people | Mistaken for a change of character |
| Reduced efficacy | Feeling unable to do what you used to | Mistaken for a question of ability |

The point is that **the three move together**. Being tired is exhaustion, not burnout. What marks burnout is that the tired person **simultaneously turns cynical and loses their sense of efficacy**.

The second axis matters most for how burnout looks from outside. Someone in burnout becoming distant and sharp is not a change of personality but **a defence against depletion**: with no emotional capacity left, they build the distance that lets them spend none.

## 3. How it differs from stress

Stress and burnout differ in direction, not in degree.

- **Stress is excess.** Demands are too many, feelings over-respond, everything is urgent.
- **Burnout is depletion.** Feeling flattens, motivation empties, distance opens.

That is why "reduce the load" works for stress but works poorly on its own for burnout. The tank is already empty.

## 4. Why a holiday rarely fixes it

The dominant model in burnout research is the **Job Demands–Resources model**. Exhaustion arises when demands — workload, time pressure, emotional labour — persistently outrun resources such as autonomy, support, feedback and role clarity.

That model explains something specific: **why people go back after a holiday and are flat again within days.** A holiday sets demands briefly to zero but changes nothing on the resource side. On return, the ratio is exactly what it was.

Maslach and Leiter have made the same point repeatedly. Burnout is **least well solved when it is treated as a problem of personal resilience**, because much of its cause sits in conditions of work design, role clarity, control and fairness.

This does not mean an individual can do nothing. It changes **where to push**: rather than hunting for more recovery techniques, look first at where demands can be cut or resources added — scope, support you could ask for, decisions you could be given.

## 5. What to look at now

- **How many** of the three axes apply? If it is only fatigue, it is early to call it burnout.
- Is the depletion **confined to one job**, or general to your life? If general, depression needs ruling out.
- Can demands be reduced, or resources increased? If neither, that is itself information.
- If sleep, eating or relationships are breaking down, **getting help comes before interpretation**.
