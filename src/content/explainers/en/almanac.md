Introductions to the almanac usually start with Benjamin Franklin's *Poor Richard's Almanack* (1732). It was widely read — but **the almanac is a much older form than that.**

## 1. An almanac has two layers

The key to the form is that **one book holds two layers of different character.**

| Layer | Contents | Kind of evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Calculation | Solar terms, sunrise and sunset, lunar phase, tides, eclipses | Astronomical computation; verifiable, with measurable error |
| Guidance | Sowing and harvest timing, weather lore, proverbs, conduct | Experience and custom; how well tested varies item by item |

Sitting in the same book does not make the two layers equally reliable. Separating them is the first thing to do when reading an almanac.

## 2. Before Franklin

In Europe, almanacs circulated in manuscript from the medieval period and became, after printing, among the most widely sold printed matter there was. Franklin's was **a success built on that tradition**, adding aphorism and humour.

East Asia has a separate lineage. In Joseon the royal observatory produced and promulgated the **calendar book (yeokseo)**, carrying solar terms and eclipse predictions. *Chiljeongsan Naepyeon* (1444), from the reign of Sejong, is the treatise that carried out those computations for the latitude and longitude of Hanyang.

Farming guidance came down a separate line. Jeong Hak-yu's *Nongga Wollyeongga* (1816), setting month-by-month farm work in verse, is the representative example.

## 3. Phenology — the part that became a science

One strand of almanac guidance **grew into an observational science**: phenology.

Guidance of the form "when the forsythia blooms, plant such-and-such" uses **the state of living things**, not a date, as its signal. A date is fixed even when the year's temperatures are not; the moment of flowering reflects the actual warmth of that year. So the method can be a better signal than a date.

Today, long records of flowering dates and first frosts are used as **data for measuring climate change**. It is a rare case of old farming guidance turning into science as it stood.

## 4. Lunar gardening — the part that is thin

At the other end is the guidance to **sow and harvest according to the phase of the moon**. It is a staple of almanacs and is still widely repeated.

Here the wording has to be exact. **The moon's tidal force on the Earth is real** — it is what raises the tides. But **evidence that this force meaningfully affects soil moisture in a field or the germination of a seed has not been well observed.** Controlled trials have reported no difference by lunar phase.

That moonlight influences the behaviour of some organisms is a separate matter, and it does not underwrite guidance about sowing dates.

## 5. So how to read one

- **The calculated layer is still exact.** Solar terms, sunrise and tides are computed more precisely today; KASI publishes solar term entry moments to the minute.
- **The biological-signal layer is useful.** It reads the actual state of the year rather than a date.
- **Enjoy lunar-phase guidance as tradition, but do not treat it as grounds.**
- The value of an almanac lies not in prophecy but in **arranging life against the sky and the season**.
