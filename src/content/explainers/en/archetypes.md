Search for "the 12 archetypes" and you get Hero, Sage, Explorer, Magician — usually introduced as **Carl Jung's theory**. This article starts from the fact that the attribution is inaccurate.

## 1. Jung never sorted archetypes into twelve

The record first. **Jung did not fix a number of archetypes.** He held them to be innumerable in principle, and the ones he named and worked with in his writing are different from that list.

| Archetypes Jung worked with | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| Persona | The face turned outward; a social role hardened |
| Shadow | The part of yourself you do not acknowledge |
| Anima / Animus | The contrasexual image within |
| Self | The centre of the whole, conscious and unconscious together |
| Mother, Wise Old Man, Trickster … | Figures recurring in myth and dream |

Jung's concern was **individuation** — acknowledging the shadow, loosening the grip of the persona, moving toward the Self. It was not sorting people into twelve boxes.

## 2. So where do the twelve come from

The list in circulation was set out by **Margaret Mark and Carol S. Pearson in a 2001 book on branding**. It is a practitioner's tool for giving a brand a consistent character, borrowing Jung's vocabulary.

Different purposes, different objects:

| | Jung's archetypes | The 12 brand archetypes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Purpose | Describe the structure of the unconscious | Design a consistent brand character |
| Number | Not fixed | Fixed at twelve |
| Use | Interpreting the process of individuation | Marketing, copy, identity |
| Basis | Clinical observation and mythological comparison | A typology distilled from practice |

None of this makes the twelve useless. **For working on a brand, it works well.** But presenting it as Jung's psychology produces two misunderstandings at once: Jung is credited with something he did not say, and a branding tool gets used as a personality assessment.

## 3. The problem of testing

Archetype theory has long been criticised as **hard to falsify**. If an image appearing counts as an archetype expressing itself, and its absence counts as repression, then no observation can contradict the theory.

Jung's claim that the collective unconscious is inherited also lacks support in modern genetics and cognitive science. Similarities across myths are explained **without inherited memory** — by the bodily and social conditions humans share, and by cultural transmission.

So archetypes are more accurately treated as **a language of interpretation** than as a tested theory. A vocabulary useful for reading stories and organising your own experience — not a measuring instrument for judging a person's character.

## 4. What remains useful anyway

The concept has survived for reasons.

- **A way of seeing story structure.** Joseph Campbell's analysis of the hero narrative was built on this vocabulary, and that in turn shaped how popular stories are written.
- **A tool for self-description.** "Which part am I playing right now?" is not a question with a fixed answer; it is one that makes you look again.
- **The idea of the shadow.** The observation that what you refuse in yourself gets projected onto others is a starting point for conversation, even without being a tested theory.

One thing to keep while reading. **An archetype is a lens for reading a story, not a box for sorting people.**

## References

> **C. G. Jung** (1959) *The Archetypes and the Collective Unconscious* (Collected Works 9i) — the source for archetype and collective unconscious.

> **C. G. Jung** (1964) *Man and His Symbols* — the popular account Jung himself took part in.

> **Margaret Mark & Carol S. Pearson** (2001) *The Hero and the Outlaw* — the source of the twelve-archetype list in circulation. A book on brand strategy, not a psychology text.

> **Joseph Campbell** (1949) *The Hero with a Thousand Faces* — applying archetypal vocabulary to comparative myth; hugely influential on popular narrative.
