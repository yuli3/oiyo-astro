## Separate what is gone from what can still change

A **sunk cost** is a past expense that cannot be recovered whichever option you choose now. The sunk cost fallacy is letting that expense alone change your next choice. Continuing is not itself an error: future benefits may justify the additional costs.

A past payment is not fully sunk if it can be refunded. Equipment resale proceeds, cancellation charges and time available for another activity differ between options and belong in the comparison. A non-refundable past course fee that is identical under both options does not distinguish them.

## An imagined project: continue or stop?

This is a **fictional example** in arbitrary units of one currency. The 100 already paid cannot be recovered. Continuing requires another 60 and yields a future benefit of 90. Stopping brings 20 from equipment resale and costs 5 to cancel. Future amounts occur at the same time; uncertainty and taxes are omitted.

Recording the 100 only as a loss from stopping distorts the comparison: continuing does not bring that money back either. Past spending can nevertheless provide evidence about how well you forecast future costs. Learning from past information is different from trying to vindicate a past expense.

## What the research does and does not establish

[Arkes and Blumer’s 1985 paper](https://doi.org/10.1016/0749-5978%2885%2990049-4) examines how previous investment affects continuation, including a theatre subscription field study and questionnaire experiments. It does not establish that every decision to continue is mistaken.

The label “Concorde fallacy” is common, but a major public project’s motives cannot be reduced to past spending alone. This article does not adjudicate the historical causes of the Concorde programme.

## Some decisions cannot be reduced to money

Jobs, courses and relationships also involve safety, care, commitments, prospects for repair and real switching costs. Feeling attached does not by itself make a choice irrational. This article is not a prescription to trade an investment or end a relationship.

Ask: “Would I choose this without knowing the past bill?”, “Which portion is genuinely unrecoverable?”, and “What new evidence would change the outlook for continuing or stopping?” These questions make changeable conditions visible instead of treating an unchangeable past as the answer.
