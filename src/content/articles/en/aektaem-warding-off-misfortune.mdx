## People Who Smile at a Broken Dish

When something breaks in a Korean household, or a small accident happens, someone nearby is likely to say: *"Aektaem haetne."* You've done your aektaem.

Looked at plainly, it is a strange sort of comfort. You have just lost something, and you are being told it went well. But the phrase carries a fairly precise structure. **Aek (厄), misfortune, has been patched.** A larger disaster was on its way, and the small loss you just took has filled that hole — the way a hole in a kettle gets plugged with a scrap of metal before it leaks.

Two assumptions sit underneath. First, that **the total amount of misfortune is fixed.** Second, that **the total can be paid in pieces of different sizes.** The first assumption alone produces resignation. The moment the second is added, the posture changes: instead of waiting to be struck, you can step forward and pay the debt small.

Nearly every aektaem custom rests on that second assumption. The aim is not to abolish misfortune. It is **to choose the form and the size in which you pay it.**

## When Misfortune Is Thought to Arrive

Aektaem is not performed at random. Certain periods are held to concentrate misfortune, and the customs cluster there.

### Samjae, the Three Calamities

The best known is *samjae*. Once every twelve years, misfortune is said to settle in for three years. Birth-year animals are grouped into four sets, and each set has its own samjae years.

| Animal group | Samjae years |
| --- | --- |
| Monkey, Rat, Dragon | Tiger, Rabbit, Dragon years |
| Pig, Rabbit, Goat | Snake, Horse, Goat years |
| Tiger, Horse, Dog | Monkey, Rooster, Dog years |
| Snake, Rooster, Ox | Pig, Rat, Ox years |

Each of the three years has its own name: the year misfortune **enters**, the year it **stays**, and the year it **leaves**. Folk practice treats the entering year as the heaviest, and often reads the leaving year as a loosening rather than a threat.

The arithmetic itself comes from Five-Element theory. The animal groups are Earthly Branches bound together in a *samhap* triad, and samjae falls where that combined energy exhausts itself and is buried. Strip away the divinatory reading and what remains is closer to a calendrical device: **three years flagged for caution inside a twelve-year cycle.**

### Ages Ending in Nine

Avoiding ages that end in nine — 19, 29, 39 — belongs to the same family. The year just before a threshold, the year spent **standing on the boundary**, is treated as dangerous. It overlaps precisely with where many cultures place rites of passage.

### Solar Terms and Festivals

Customs gather at the joints of the year: the winter solstice, the first day of the first lunar month, the first full moon. These are points where something **turns over** — where the longest night begins to shorten. Misfortune was thought to move in and out at exactly such moments.

## What People Actually Did

Aektaem was an action, not an attitude. These are the practices documented in the record and still partly alive.

### Red Bean Porridge at the Solstice

On the winter solstice, households cooked red bean porridge, ate it, and splashed it on gates and storage jars. Red was held to repel malign influence. Eating and smearing sit inside one act, which treats the inside and the outside of the household together.

### Cracking Nuts at the First Full Moon

Peanuts, walnuts and chestnuts were cracked between the teeth. The sound drove off spirits, and the practice was said to prevent boils for the coming year. Here again the basic structure of aektaem is visible: **a small breakage standing in for a large one.**

### The Misfortune-Banishing Kite

Kites flown during the first lunar month were cut loose around the full moon and allowed to fly away. People commonly wrote `送厄迎福` on them — *send off misfortune, welcome fortune* — along with their name and birth year. Misfortune is detached from the body, attached to an object, and the object is discarded.

### The Straw Effigy

On the eve of the first full moon, a human figure was woven from straw. Inside went coins matching the person's age and a slip of paper bearing their name and birth year, and the figure was thrown into the street. Children picked it up, took the coins, and tore the straw apart — and that tearing was the misfortune dispersing.

The effigy pushes the kite's logic further. A **stand-in** is made to carry the misfortune, and the stand-in is destroyed in your place.

### Talismans

And then there are talismans. Where the practices above are tied to particular dates, a talisman can be made at any time and carried on the body, which made its range far wider.

## A Talisman Is Writing, Not a Picture

Most people looking closely at a Korean talisman for the first time conclude they cannot tell what it depicts. That impression comes from a misunderstanding. **A talisman is fundamentally writing, not drawing.** It consists of legible Chinese characters, deliberately distorted ones, and symbols such as constellations.

### Materials

Traditionally it was written on yellowed *hanji* paper in **cinnabar** — a red mineral pigment, tied to the belief that red repels misfortune. One point matters here: the strokes are red **regardless of purpose or element.** A talisman replenishing Water is not drawn in blue. Redness is a fact about the material; the content is carried by the characters.

### Structure

A talisman reads top to bottom, in three parts.

- **Head (頭)** — marks at the top that summon energy inward. Three dots is a common form.
- **Body** — the largest character sits at the centre. What the talisman is for lives here.
- **Tail (尾)** — a short phrase runs down the bottom to close it.

The **Big Dipper** appears frequently: seven dots joined in a ladle. In Korean folk belief the Dipper governs lifespan and fortune, and Seven-Star worship grew into a distinct strand of practice.

### Why It Is Hard to Read

The characters are distorted on purpose. They are overlapped, reduced to stacked radicals, or stretched into long strokes. Being hard to read is part of the point: something anyone could write would not be a special object.

That same quality makes it easy to **scribble arbitrary lines and call the result a talisman.** The test separating the real thing from a scribble is simple: does every mark on the sheet have a source — can you say which script it belongs to and where it came from?

## Why Aektaem Feels Like It Works

From here the question is observational, not devotional. There is no evidence that aektaem transfers disaster. Yet the fact that people feel steadier after performing it shows up fairly consistently. Several explanations apply.

### Ritual Lowers Anxiety

Experimental work links ritual behaviour to reduced performance anxiety. Brooks and colleagues (2016) had participants carry out a fixed sequence of actions before tasks such as singing or mental arithmetic; that group reported less anxiety and performed better. The striking part is that the effect appeared even when the actions bore no relation to the task.

Norton and Gino (2014) observed something similar among people who had suffered loss. Even rituals the participants invented themselves reduced grief, and the mediating factor was **a restored sense of control.**

Aektaem has exactly this shape. In front of something you cannot prevent, it manufactures one thing you can do.

### Room for After-the-Fact Reading

"You've done your aektaem" is difficult to falsify. If nothing bad follows, the aektaem worked; if something bad follows, the misfortune was simply larger. That structure keeps the belief stable. It is a criticism, but it is also an explanation for **how the custom survived for centuries.**

### The Illusion of Control

The illusion of control, set out by Langer (1975), is the tendency to overestimate one's influence over chance outcomes. The overestimate is not always a loss, though. A prolonged sense that nothing can be done hardens into helplessness, and aektaem puts one small action back in the hand.

## What to Do With the Custom Today

There are two ways to hold it.

One treats aektaem as a **predictive device**: samjae has arrived, therefore accidents will happen this year. This reading has no basis and can work actively against the person holding it, because it invites them to locate the cause of any misfortune in their own birth year.

The other treats it as a **marker**: every place aektaem attaches — samjae, the nines, the solstice, the new year — is a joint where something changes. Read as a signal to stop and take stock at those joints, the custom becomes an old form of **periodic self-review.**

OIYO takes the second position. We will write you a talisman, but we will not tell you it prevents disaster. Instead we disclose what every mark on it is and where it came from.
