Cal Newport writes in *Deep Work* (2016) that the ability to concentrate deeply becomes more valuable at work at the very time that ability is growing rare. On a day full of alerts, mail and messages cut work into small pieces. **Deep work** in this article is not a slogan. It is the way of working he described.

## Deep work and shallow work

In Newport's definition, **deep work** is cognitively demanding work done with uninterrupted concentration. It aims at a result that creates new value, raises a skill, or is hard to copy by the same procedure.

**Shallow work** sits near the other side.

- Replying to mail, sorting a schedule, simple administration
- Work with a low cognitive load that moves ahead without a long immersion
- Work another person can take over by the same procedure

This does not mean shallow work is useless. If you do not first keep a block for deep work, shallow work easily fills the day.

## Switching has a cost

It is hard to say that two complex tasks are handled at the same time, at the same quality. Much of what people call multitasking is closer to moving quickly between tasks.

Rubinstein, Meyer, and Evans (2001) report an experiment: task switching has a cost in executive control. That paper did not measure the 23 minutes below.

### The figure of 23 minutes

Mark, Gonzalez, and Harris (2005) is a conference paper that observed information workers. The sample is a small number of people at one company. The site is earlier than the time when smartphone alerts are an ordinary part of the day. When people returned to the original work on the same day, the average delay was roughly around 25 minutes. When the break came from outside, it was close to about 23 minutes. The widely repeated 23 minutes and 15 seconds are close to a rounding Mark later used in a public explanation.

A large part of that interval was time spent on other work, not time spent blank. The spread from person to person is also large. It is better not to read this as a law that every interruption takes 23 minutes. At the related site, activity also changed in stretches of a few minutes. The time it takes to return, and the time spent staying on one activity, are different observations.

### Attention residue

Sophie Leroy (2009) reports that after you change tasks, thoughts of the previous task can remain and get in the way of the next performance. The paper is often cited as a reason why, when short switches pile up, the quality of each piece of work tends to fall together.

### It overlaps with flow, but it is not the same theory

Csikszentmihalyi explains that flow appears when challenge and skill match. Deep work is not a device that guarantees that state. It is a design of work that reduces interruption and gives time to difficult work. There is a stretch where it overlaps with flow.

## Four schedules

Newport divides ways of placing deep work into four. This does not mean that any one of them produces the best measured result.

### 1. Monastic

Cut shallow duties sharply, and make deep work the default.

- An example close to the book is Donald Knuth. He has said that he does not use email from 1990-01-01, and he receives letters by postal mail.
- It is an extreme that fits a person who can give a long time to one piece of work. It is unrealistic for most jobs.

### 2. Bimodal

Spend a long stretch, at least on the scale of a day, on deep work. Use the remaining time for ordinary work.

- In a picture Newport gives, there is the retreat time Carl Jung used at Bollingen.
- It fits more easily work that can set aside a long block, such as research or writing.

### 3. Rhythmic

Place a deep-work block at the same time every day.

- For example, the same two or three hours on a weekday morning.
- On an employed schedule, it is presented as the easiest to keep going.

### 4. Journalistic

When an open stretch appears, go straight into deep work.

- It needs a lot of practice. It is a way to consider when the schedule is irregular.
- It is not set as the default prescription for someone who is just starting.

## The setting

### Place

- If you can, choose a place used only for deep work.
- Leave the phone in another room, and turn alerts off.
- You can treat sitting there as a start signal. That does not mean focus turns on the moment you sit down.

### Screen

- Some people use a tool that blocks the internet during the block. Cold Turkey and Freedom are names of that kind. The tool does not create focus.
- Set times for looking at mail. For example, morning, after lunch, and before you finish work.
- There is also the trial of taking social apps off the phone, or limiting their time.

### Time blocks

Divide the day into blocks, and mark the deep-work block first. What follows is an example. It is not a correct timetable.

```
8:00–12:00  Deep work
12:00–13:00 Lunch
13:00–14:00 Mail, meeting preparation
14:00–17:00 Deep work or collaboration
17:00–17:30 Mail, closing
```

## Practice

### Productive meditation

While walking or running, hold one problem that needs deep work and think it through. Newport introduces this as training.

### Memory palace

This is a memory art that ties place and image together. Newport mentions it as one way to practice concentration. This article has no measurement that concentration time grows from that skill alone.

### A timer

Pomodoro is commonly 25 minutes of work and 5 minutes of rest. Ninety minutes of work and 15 minutes of rest is one schedule people try for deep work. It is not an experimental conclusion that 90 minutes is better than 25.

### Length

Concentration can grow with practice. It does not become several hours from the start. Newport speaks of the upper bound of deep work as about a few hours a day. That is not a promise that two to four hours opens for everyone.

## Social media

Calling social media the enemy of deep work is a slogan. Three reasons are often given.

1. The account is that the hand goes there easily, because the structure does not say when a reward will arrive. It is a story that likens variable-ratio reinforcement to social media. This article did not measure that addiction.
2. A feeling of comparison, and of falling behind.
3. Information with a short context splits attention into small pieces.

A 30-day pause, keeping the app only on the computer on the desk, and looking only twice a day are trials in cutting back. They are not a treatment effect.
