# Foundations of Psychology

Psychology is the scientific study of mind and behavior. Researchers examine explanations through observation and experimentation, then assess the conditions and populations to which the findings can generalize. This chapter introduces major research perspectives and methods, with an exercise that distinguishes correlation from causation.

---

## 1. From philosophical questions to experimental research

Questions about mind and behavior have a philosophical history. An institutional milestone was Wilhelm Wundt's founding of the Institute of Psychology in Leipzig in 1879. [Leipzig University's history](https://www.lw.uni-leipzig.de/en/wilhelm-wundt-institute-for-psychology/institute-history-1/history-of-the-institute) describes the laboratory's international role. This date marks an institutional development, not the instant when every psychological question acquired a scientific answer.

## 2. Major schools and perspectives in psychology

Psychology has developed various perspectives for observing humans over time.

| Perspective and researchers | Concepts and emphasis |
| --- | --- |
| **Psychoanalysis**<br />Freud | <mark>Unconscious</mark>, Childhood<br />Internal conflict and instinctual drives |
| **Behaviorism**<br />Watson, Skinner | <mark>Stimulus-Response (S-R)</mark><br />Observable behavior and learning |
| **Cognitivism**<br />Piaget, Neisser | <mark>Information Processing</mark><br />Humans as "thinking beings" |
| **Humanism**<br />Rogers, Maslow | <mark>Self-actualization</mark><br />Human potential and free will |
| Biological perspective<br />Multiple research traditions | Nervous system, genetics, evolution<br />Biological contributions to behavior |

These are introductory categories, not five mutually exclusive explanations. A researcher can investigate learning, cognition and neural activity in the same question. A biological association does not imply that genes or one brain region determine a person's behavior.

## 3. Core concepts

### (1) Metacognition

Often described as "thinking about thinking," it is the awareness of one's own cognitive processes. The ability to distinguish between what you know and what you don't is key to learning and problem-solving.

### (2) Mind-Body Connection

Studying the mind-body relationship requires attention to biological, psychological and environmental factors. A brain state alone does not explain depression; an association is not proof of causation. [NIMH's depression overview](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/depression) describes multiple contributing factors.

## 4. Research methods and the research cycle

- **Experiment**: Manipulate an independent variable and measure an outcome. Control conditions and assignment procedures help evaluate competing explanations; merely calling a study an experiment does not remove confounding.
- **Correlational study**: Examine how variables vary together without manipulating them. An association alone does not establish direction or rule out a third variable.
- **Observation Method**: Recording behavior in natural settings.
- **Case Study**: Deeply analyzing unique cases (e.g., patients with specific brain damage).

A research cycle starts with a question and a testable prediction. The researcher defines what the variables mean operationally, selects a design and sample, collects and analyzes data, then reports the result and its limits. Follow-up studies can test replication and whether the result extends beyond the original task or population.

For example, students who sleep longer might obtain higher exam scores. That association does not by itself prove that changing sleep duration changes scores: prior preparation or other differences may also matter. A design that addresses those explanations provides stronger causal evidence. A narrowly selected sample still limits generalization.

## Practice: interpreting a research result

### Question

In a hypothetical observational study, university students' sleep duration is positively associated with exam scores. The researcher concludes, "More sleep improves everyone's scores." What is wrong with the causal claim and its generalization? Suggest what a follow-up study should examine.

### Explanation

Sleep was not manipulated, so the association alone does not establish causal direction. Prior preparation or other differences could relate to both sleep and scores. A university-student sample also does not justify a claim about everyone. Examine how sleep and scores were measured, how the sample was recruited, how alternative explanations could be tested, and whether the finding replicates in other populations. Even an experiment needs scrutiny for confounding and limits of application; it cannot promise a particular improvement in scores.
