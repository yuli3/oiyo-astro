## Myths are old mirrors

"Narcissist," "Oedipus complex," "a Cassandra-like warning" — names from myth are still used to describe the mind. This article picks seven frequently cited figures and story patterns and sets out **what the original story was**, **how it is read today**, and **what to be careful about** when taking up those readings.

## The hero's journey: leave, be tested, return

In *The Hero with a Thousand Faces* (1949), Joseph Campbell argued that hero stories across the world share a common structure — **departure → initiation (trials) → return** — which he called the "monomyth." The hero is called away from the ordinary world, crosses a threshold, faces trials and returns with something gained. The pattern has since shaped a great deal of film and fiction writing.

**Modern reading**: seeing a major life change — a new job, study abroad, recovering from illness — as a journey can help you understand present hardship as "crossing a threshold."

**Be careful**: folklorists have long criticised Campbell for **trimming away what did not fit** in order to press different traditions into one mould. Not every story follows this structure.

## Icarus: too high, and too low

In Ovid's *Metamorphoses* (Book 8), the craftsman Daedalus builds wings of wax and feathers so he and his son Icarus can escape. He warns: fly too high and the sun will melt the wax; fly too low and the sea's damp will weigh the wings down. Thrilled by flight, Icarus soars upward and falls into the sea.

**Modern reading**: the story is usually read only as "the price of overreaching," but the original warning was to **avoid both extremes**. It is a tale of balance: excessive caution ruins the wings just as excessive ambition does.

## Narcissus and Echo: two people who cannot reach each other

Also in the *Metamorphoses* (Book 3), the nymph Echo is punished so that she can only repeat the ends of others' words; rejected by Narcissus, whom she loves, she fades until only her voice remains. Narcissus becomes captivated by his own reflection in a pool and wastes away, unable to leave it.

**Modern reading**: the word "narcissism" comes from here. The story can also be read as a relationship in which someone trapped in themselves and someone who **only repeats others without a voice of their own** can never meet — a reminder, too, of the "echo chamber" where only similar opinions bounce back.

**Be careful**: people casually call others "narcissists," but narcissistic personality disorder is a clinical concept diagnosed by professionals.

## Oedipus: the fate he tried to escape

In Sophocles' tragedy *Oedipus Rex*, Oedipus leaves home to escape the oracle that he will kill his father and marry his mother — and that very choice fulfils the prophecy. Having pursued the truth to the end, he blinds himself.

**Modern reading**: Freud borrowed the story in *The Interpretation of Dreams* (1900) to propose the "Oedipus complex," a concept that psychology today regards as **weakly supported by evidence**. The weight of the original lies rather in **the relentless pursuit of truth and its cost**, and in how much humans can know about their own fate.

## Cassandra: the truth no one believes

Cassandra, a princess of Troy, received the gift of prophecy from Apollo, but as punishment for rejecting him, **no one would ever believe her**. In Aeschylus's *Agamemnon*, she accurately foretells her own death and Agamemnon's, and no one listens.

**Modern reading**: the name is used for people who give correct warnings and are ignored — the "Cassandra complex." It is read as a lesson in why people raising uncomfortable risks in organisations get brushed aside, and in listening to **what is said rather than who says it**.

## Medusa: from monster to symbol

Ovid tells that Medusa was once a beautiful woman who, after being raped by Neptune in a temple, was turned by Minerva (Athena) into a monster with snakes for hair (*Metamorphoses*, Book 4). The hero Perseus cuts off her head.

**Modern reading**: in the twentieth century, Hélène Cixous's "The Laugh of the Medusa" (1975) reclaimed Medusa as a symbol of **women's suppressed voice and anger**. She is often cited as a symbol critiquing how victims are branded as monsters.

## The trickster: the one who shakes the rules

Loki in Norse myth, Hermes in Greek myth, Coyote and Raven in Native North American myths — many mythologies feature a **trickster** who deceives, plays pranks and upsets order. Anthropologist Paul Radin (1956) compared such figures, and Lewis Hyde (1998) argued that the trickster, crossing boundaries, is also **a creator of new things**.

**Modern reading**: a symbol of people who crack rigid rules and of game-changing ideas. Yet the trickster's pranks also carry a price; the key is that destruction and creation come together.

## Summary at a glance

| Figure / pattern | Source | Common modern use | Be careful |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hero's journey | Campbell (1949) | Seeing life changes as a journey | Not every story fits |
| Icarus | Ovid, Book 8 | The price of overreaching | Originally warned against both extremes |
| Narcissus & Echo | Ovid, Book 3 | Narcissism, echo chambers | Do not use diagnoses lightly |
| Oedipus | Sophocles | Oedipus complex | Weak empirical support |
| Cassandra | Aeschylus | Ignored warnings | Listen to what, not who |
| Medusa | Ovid, Book 4 | Women's anger and voice | Readings change with the times |
| Trickster | Many myths | Rule-breaking creativity | Destruction and creation together |

Myths are not textbooks that hand out answers; they are mirrors for the mind. Knowing the original stories shows both how much the familiar metaphors have been simplified and how much insight they still hold.
