## Separate observation, metrics and policy hypotheses

These five labels are not the same kind of “psychological law.” The streetlight effect is a metaphor about observation; survivorship bias concerns inference from a selected sample. The cobra effect names an incentive problem, broken windows is a debated social and policy hypothesis, and the law of triviality is an organisational satire.

Before asking whether a calculation is correct, ask **who was counted, and which number stands in for which purpose**. Naming an effect does not establish a cause or a remedy.

## Streetlight effect: easy to search versus likely to answer

The story of searching for keys only under a streetlight distinguishes “easy to see” from “likely to contain the answer.” Here it is an illustration, not a verified event or a documented quotation by a historical person.

If sales decline, clicks already on a dashboard may omit refund reasons or the experience of customers who abandoned a purchase. That does not make the current metric useless. It means specifying the questions it cannot answer.

**Check question**: “Where might the answer be, and does this evidence include that place?”

## Survivorship bias: investigate unobserved cases

Survivorship bias arises when visible or surviving cases are used to infer outcomes for a group that also contains unobserved cases. Stories of successful founders omit failures, withdrawals and nonresponse when used to estimate success probabilities.

Wald’s aircraft survivability work is a familiar illustration ([Mangel and Samaniego, 1984](https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.1984.10478038)). Treat the popular “armour where there are no holes” diagram as a compressed explanation of selection, not a reconstruction here of an exact wartime conversation or every aircraft’s fate.

**Check question**: “Who entered the sample, why, and how could missing outcomes be investigated?” Missing cases increase uncertainty; they do not simply reverse the observed success rate.

## Cobra effect: when the metric and the goal diverge

The cobra effect names a possible mismatch between a rewarded metric and the actual objective. It is not a law that all rewards fail or everyone manipulates metrics.

The Hanoi rat hunt is a case in Vann’s historical research; see an [interview with the researcher](https://madeinchinajournal.com/2020/08/20/the-great-hanoi-rat-hunt/). Do not reduce every participant’s motive or all colonial policy outcomes to money alone. The Indian cobra bounty story is not presented here as a verified event.

Rewarding only closed tickets could encourage postponing difficult cases or closing easy ones prematurely. This is a **hypothetical design risk**, not an allegation of observed manipulation. Examine speed alongside repeat contacts and actual resolution.

**Check question**: “Does the easiest route to a higher number also advance the real objective?”

## Broken windows: environmental cues are not policy effects

Broken windows proposes that signals of minor disorder can influence behaviour and local order. Experiments about environmental cues, changes in neighbourhood crime and evaluations of enforcement are different questions. Evidence for one does not automatically establish the other two.

[Braga and colleagues’ 2015 systematic review and meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022427815576576) reports differences across interventions. Community problem-solving at places should not be treated as interchangeable with aggressive enforcement against individual minor offences.

Compare controls, other changes over the same period, crime outcomes, implementation and adverse effects. This article does not recommend expanding enforcement or punishment in any country, and is not current local legal guidance.

**Check question**: “What intervention changed, what outcome was measured, and how far were alternative explanations ruled out?”

## Law of triviality: satire versus meeting evaluation

Parkinson’s bicycle-shed story **satirises** a committee discussing an accessible roofing decision longer than a complex reactor budget. It is not an experimentally established law of all meetings, or evidence that small agenda items never matter.

Long discussion may be necessary when safety, responsibility or interests are complex. Identify impact and irreversibility before judging the time spent. Do not cut discussion merely because it is long.

**Check question**: “Does discussion time fit the impact, risk and uncertainty that need resolving?”

## Checklist at a glance

| Trap | Check question |
|---|---|
| Streetlight effect | Am I looking where the answer is likely to be? |
| Survivorship bias | Where are the failures that vanished? |
| Cobra effect | Does the easy way to raise the number harm the goal? |
| Broken windows | Which approach actually worked? |
| Law of triviality | Is the longest discussion the most important item? |

This checklist is a thinking aid, not a proven guarantee of fewer errors. Compare actual evidence and alternatives, and record what would change your judgement.
