## 1. Your circle is not a formula for your future

The saying that you are the average of your five closest people cannot calculate an individual's future. A metaphor for reviewing relationships is not a validated prediction rule. Rather than ranking people as successful or unsuccessful, look at shared activities and repeated conduct.

## 2. Similarity does not establish influence

Shalizi and Thomas's [study of causal inference in social networks](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3328971/) explains why observational data can struggle to separate social influence from ties formed between similar people. Similar outcomes alone do not establish that one person changed another.

| Possible explanation | Question for a study group |
| --- | --- |
| Social influence | Did you actually use a solution method learned in the group? |
| Selection and homophily | Were similarly motivated students already choosing to meet? |
| Shared environment | Were all members responding to the same exam date or deadline? |

This does not establish that friends have no influence. Claims about its presence and size need evidence addressing alternative explanations. Mirror neurons or “energy waves” cannot serve as scientific proof for choosing relationships.

## 3. Review agreements, not people's rank

These prompts prepare a conversation; they are not a diagnostic scale.

- When plans change, do you give each other notice?
- Can a request be declined and its scope renegotiated?
- During disagreement, do you discuss conduct and evidence or attack the person?
- If support falls mainly on one person, can you revisit its timing and limits?

Feeling tired once, or hearing a friend's hardship, does not establish exploitation. Separate the specific situation, whether it repeats, and what you can reasonably provide. Low mood or a setback is not a reason to label someone a bad environment.

## 4. Try a small change and review it

<details>
<summary>Fictional case: did the study group improve the grade?</summary>

After joining a group, Mina spends more time studying. The weekly commitment may help, but an approaching exam and her choice to join already motivated students are alternative explanations.

Mina proposes taking turns explaining solutions and keeping comparable notes on days she studies alone. Those notes support a conversation about what was useful; they are not an experiment establishing the group's causal effect. Study time, understanding, and grades also need separate attention.

</details>

Before ending all contact, you might adjust meeting time, activities, or responsibilities. State a limit you can keep, such as attending once a week this month, and set a time to review it. The other person may decline or suggest a different arrangement.

## 5. Apply the same standards to yourself

If you review someone else's reliability, also review your own cancellations and communication. Respect their boundaries and be clear about help you cannot provide.

Choose one relationship this week and write down one shared activity and one agreement you can keep. Identify conditions both people can accept, rather than someone who guarantees your future.
