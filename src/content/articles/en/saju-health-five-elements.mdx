## The Traditional Correspondence Between the Five Elements and the Body

East Asian traditional thought has long described the Five Elements (五行) not only in nature but also **in pairing with the body's organs, senses and emotions**. This system of correspondences is set out in classical medical texts such as the *Huangdi Neijing* (黃帝內經), and Saju borrows it. The table below shows that **traditional correspondence (a symbol)**; it does not describe anatomical or modern medical fact.

| Element | Traditionally paired organs | Sense organ | Emotion |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wood (木) | Liver, gallbladder | Eyes | Anger |
| Fire (火) | Heart, small intestine | Tongue | Joy |
| Earth (土) | Spleen, stomach | Mouth | Pensiveness, worry |
| Metal (金) | Lungs, large intestine | Nose | Grief |
| Water (水) | Kidneys, bladder | Ears | Fear |

Saying that a chart has a lot or a little of some element is part of Saju's **interpretive language**. It does not mean a particular organ is weak or that you will develop a particular illness.

## Traditional Readings and Daily Rhythms by Element

Below are the images tradition has attached to each element, paired with ordinary everyday habits that suit almost anyone. They are not criteria for judging or treating any health condition.

### Wood (木) — paired with the liver, gallbladder and eyes

- **Temperament it symbolizes**: reaching outward, planning, growth. Traditional readings use it as a **metaphor for mood**: too much Wood means becoming impatient or quick to anger; too little means losing drive.
- **Season**: spring
- **Daily rhythm ideas**: walks in nature, stretching, pausing when you feel angry and putting the feeling into words

---

### Fire (火) — paired with the heart, small intestine and tongue

- **Temperament it symbolizes**: passion, expression, brightness. Traditional readings use it as a **metaphor for mood**: too much Fire means feeling restless and scattered; too little means motivation sinks.
- **Season**: summer
- **Daily rhythm ideas**: meditation, listening to music, a quiet rest after an overstimulating day

---

### Earth (土) — paired with the spleen, stomach and mouth

- **Temperament it symbolizes**: stability, mediation, trust. Traditional readings use it as a **metaphor for mood**: too much Earth means overthinking and worry; too little means finding it hard to stay centered.
- **Period**: the transitions between seasons
- **Daily rhythm ideas**: a regular daily routine, writing your worries down so you can set them aside

---

### Metal (金) — paired with the lungs, large intestine and nose

- **Temperament it symbolizes**: decisiveness, principle, order. Traditional readings use it as a **metaphor for mood**: too much Metal means becoming overly strict; too little means putting off decisions.
- **Season**: autumn
- **Daily rhythm ideas**: time for slow, deep breaths, sharing sadness with someone you trust rather than holding it in

---

### Water (水) — paired with the kidneys, bladder and ears

- **Temperament it symbolizes**: wisdom, flexibility, depth. Traditional readings use it as a **metaphor for mood**: too much Water means anxiety and worry grow; too little means forgetting how to rest.
- **Season**: winter
- **Daily rhythm ideas**: enough sleep, resting before fatigue builds up

<Callout type="note">
**Physical symptoms need a doctor**: Changes in your body such as fatigue, palpitations, indigestion, breathing problems or swelling are not something to explain with the Five Elements. If you have symptoms, please get checked at a medical facility.
</Callout>

## Traditional Correspondences by Day Stem

Here are the organs that the traditional table pairs with the element of each day stem (日干). This is **only a symbolic link**; it does not mean people with that day stem are prone to illness in those organs.

| Day stem | Element | Traditionally paired organs (symbolic) | Reflection keywords |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gap, Eul | Wood (木) | Liver, gallbladder | Growth, handling anger |
| Byeong, Jeong | Fire (火) | Heart, small intestine | Expression, calming restlessness |
| Mu, Gi | Earth (土) | Spleen, stomach | Stability, letting go of worry |
| Gyeong, Sin | Metal (金) | Lungs, large intestine | Decisiveness, sharing grief |
| Im, Gye | Water (水) | Kidneys, bladder | Wisdom, facing fear |

## The Traditional Correspondence of Elements and Seasons

| Season | Ruling element |
|---|---|
| Spring | Wood (木) |
| Summer | Fire (火) |
| Transitions between seasons (Jin, Mi, Sul, Chuk months) | Earth (土) |
| Autumn | Metal (金) |
| Winter | Water (水) |

Tradition recommends adjusting your daily rhythm to the flow of the seasons. You can take that as a philosophy of living, but it is not a prediction that certain people fall ill in certain seasons.

## The Traditional Correspondence of Elements, Colors and Tastes

The traditional table also paired the elements with colors and tastes. The foods below are just **examples** that bring each color or taste to mind.

| Element | Color | Taste | Example foods that evoke it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wood (木) | Green | Sour | Spinach, broccoli, vinegar |
| Fire (火) | Red | Bitter | Tomato, strawberry, beet |
| Earth (土) | Yellow | Sweet | Sweet potato, pumpkin, kabocha squash |
| Metal (金) | White | Pungent | Radish, Asian pear, lotus root, bellflower root |
| Water (水) | Black | Salty | Black beans, black sesame, seaweed |

<Callout type="note">
**Food is not treatment**: There is no evidence that eating foods of a particular color or taste to "balance" your elements improves health. The usual recommendation is a varied diet, and if you think you need to change your diet or take supplements, please talk with a doctor or a registered dietitian.
</Callout>
