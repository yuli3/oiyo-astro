Korean culture has fortune-telling traditions with a very long history. At Lunar New Year many people look up Tojeong Bigyeol. When they set a day to move or to marry, they look for a day without the Son. Before a large decision they may read a saju chart. This guide explains the main pieces in plain language.

## Tojeong Bigyeol (토정비결)

### History

Tojeong Bigyeol is a year-fortune book traditionally attributed to the mid-Joseon scholar Yi Ji-ham (Yi Tojeong, 1517–1578). The Encyclopedia of Korean Culture does not confirm that he wrote the text that survives. What is clear is that the book circulated as a simple way for ordinary people to look at the year ahead.

### How the calculation works

It uses only the lunar year, month, and day of birth. It does not use the birth hour. That is a different calculation from the Four Pillars (saju palja).

**Basic structure** (the casting method in the Encyclopedia of Korean Culture entry on Tojeong Bigyeol):

- Upper figure: add the age and that year's taese number (태세수), divide by 8, and take a remainder from 1 to 8.
- Middle figure: add the number of days in the lunar birth month (30 in a long month, 29 in a short month) and the wolgeon number (월건수), divide by 6, and take a remainder from 1 to 6.
- Lower figure: add the lunar day of birth and the iljin number (일진수), divide by 3, and take a remainder from 1 to 3.

Eight times 6 times 3 gives **144 figures**. 384 is the day count of a lunar leap year. It is not the number of Tojeong figures. The traditional text is recorded as using 48 of the 64 I Ching hexagrams.

### How to read it today

Tojeong Bigyeol is better read as a reflection on how to live the year than as a proven prophecy. Each figure offers something to watch out for and something to strengthen.

<Callout type="info">
**Tojeong Bigyeol today**: Around Lunar New Year, newspapers and apps publish it. Do not take it as a heavy prophecy. Use it to reflect on how you want to live the new year.
</Callout>

## Myeongnihak — the Four Pillars

### Overview

Saju palja, the Four Pillars and eight characters, studies tendencies in a life from **the four pillars of the birth year, month, day, and hour, and the eight characters written on them**.

**The four pillars:**

- Year pillar: the stem and branch of the birth year
- Month pillar: the stem and branch of the birth month
- Day pillar: the stem and branch of the birth day
- Hour pillar: the stem and branch of the birth hour

**The eight characters:**

Each pillar has two characters, a heavenly stem and an earthly branch, so the chart has eight.

### The day master — the character read as you

The heavenly stem of the day pillar is the **day master**. In myeongnihak it is read as a sign of basic character. The table is traditional symbolism. It is not a measured personality.

| Day master | Element | Traditional image |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 甲 (gap) | Wood | Strong drive, leadership |
| 乙 (eul) | Wood | Flexibility, ease of adapting |
| 丙 (byeong) | Fire | Warmth, passion, an outward manner |
| 丁 (jeong) | Fire | Fine attention, focus |
| 戊 (mu) | Earth | Steadiness, a wide embrace |
| 己 (gi) | Earth | Care with detail, a practical bent |
| 庚 (gyeong) | Metal | Decisiveness, a straight manner |
| 辛 (sin) | Metal | Sensitivity, a wish for things to be exact |
| 壬 (im) | Water | Wisdom, ease of adapting |
| 癸 (gye) | Water | Feeling, intuition |

### Yongsin — the energy the chart is said to need

Many schools look for a **yongsin**, a useful element, when they read a chart. In the eokbu school (억부), the yongsin is the element that checks an element which has run too far. In the johu school (조후), it is the element that warms a cold chart or cools a hot one. The schools do not agree. There is no measured effect by which a color, a direction, or a job improves luck. Colors and directions here are folk custom.

## Cheonjung-il (천중일)

### The idea

Cheonjung-il is a day that folk calendars call an empty heaven. Every day in the 60-day cycle has a heavenly stem, so the name does not mean a day with no stem. In each ten-day span (a xun, 旬), two earthly branches are left without a matching stem. Those two are called gongmang (공망, 旬空). Some explanations use cheonjung-il and gongmang as the same word. Lists of dates differ from book to book, so this guide does not fix any dates.

### How people use the day

**What the custom says to avoid:**

- Important beginnings, such as contracts, moving house, marriage, and starting a business

**What the custom treats as fitting:**

- Sorting things out, rest and recovery, practice, and meditation
- Finishing work that ought to be closed

## Days without the Son

### The idea

In Korean custom, a day without the **son** was chosen for moving, marrying, or opening a shop. Some explain the name from sonnim, a guest. Others explain it from the character 損, to lessen or to harm. Either way, the son is a being said to wander east, west, south, and north and to interfere with people's work.

### How the days are counted

On the lunar calendar, the days without the Son are the **9th, 10th, 19th, 20th, 29th, and 30th**.

**The story that is passed on:** The Son follows the last digit of the lunar date. It is in the east on 1 and 2, the south on 3 and 4, the west on 5 and 6, and the north on 7 and 8. On 9 and 0 it is in heaven, so it is in no earthly direction. That story is not the same as one that keeps the Son in a single direction for ten days at a time. There is no scientific basis. It is a folk way of choosing a date. A short lunar month has no day 30.

<Callout type="info">
**A present-day reading**: You can take the day without the Son as traditional culture. Even if you do not believe in it, you can still use it as one reference when you choose a moving day.
</Callout>

## What these traditions can mean now

Korean fortune-telling is not only something to dismiss as superstition. Inside it are long habits of watching nature, thoughts about how people feel and act, and practical wisdom about a life.

**Ways people use it now:**

1. **A tool for reflection.** A chart or a year reading can help you look at tendencies you already sense in yourself.
2. **A sense of time.** Looking for a day without the Son, or for a day people call good, can make planning more deliberate.
3. **A cultural link.** It can be a way to touch the customs of earlier generations in Korea.
