Samjae is a phrase that keeps returning in the Korean folk calendar. Even people who say they do not believe in saju sometimes stop when they hear that this year is samjae. What follows separates the way the encyclopedia records the phrase from glosses added later. It is not a forecast that disaster is coming.

## The phrase three disasters

The Encyclopedia of Korean Culture explains samjae as three disasters said to befall a person. The list has two layers.

- The greater three are flood, fire, and wind.
- The lesser three are injury by blade or tool (刀兵災), epidemic (疾疫災 or 疫癘災), and famine.

This page does not assert that an agrarian society most feared only those three. Water, fire, wind, disease, weapons, and hunger all remain as names.

## Who meets which years

It does not fall on every person every year. Three years are bundled by the three-harmony group of the earthly branch of the birth year. The correspondence that the encyclopedia transmits from the method of Dongguk sesigi is this.

| Earthly branch of the birth year | Years grouped as samjae |
|------------------|----------------|
| Snake, Rooster, Ox (巳酉丑) | Pig, Rat, Ox (亥子丑) |
| Monkey, Rat, Dragon (申子辰) | Tiger, Rabbit, Dragon (寅卯辰) |
| Pig, Rabbit, Goat (亥卯未) | Snake, Horse, Goat (巳午未) |
| Tiger, Horse, Dog (寅午戌) | Monkey, Rooster, Dog (申酉戌) |

One round of the branches is 12 years. Three consecutive years inside that round are called samjae. When the encyclopedia says every nine years, it is speaking of the nine years outside those three. It does not mean that those nine years are an era of prosperity.

## Entering, staying, leaving

The first year is called **deul-samjae** (들삼재, entering). The second is called **nul-samjae** (눌삼재) or **nuul-samjae** (누울삼재, staying). The third is called **nal-samjae** (날삼재, leaving). Folk records disagree about which year is the strongest. Some sayings fear the entering year most. Some treat the leaving year as the last crisis to get through. Some call the second year ak-samjae (악삼재). This page does not settle one year as the most dangerous.

Dongguk sesigi records the custom of pasting, on the lintel, a picture of three hawks. The encyclopedia also introduces later customs: drawing a three-headed hawk in red and pasting it, burning the clothes of the person who has entered samjae, and praying at a three-way road on the first tiger day and the first horse day. These are not methods whose power to prevent disaster has been confirmed. This page does not recommend burning clothes.

Glosses added later divide the mood by year. They write the entering year as change and friction, the staying year as stagnation and gossip, and the leaving year as loss at the close. That is commentary from the fortune-telling market, not the encyclopedia's statement of fact. The advice to postpone a large contract is the same kind of commentary. The calendar does not judge a contract in one's place.

## Bok-samjae

Even inside the folklore, the claim that samjae is bad for everyone is divided. **Bok-samjae** (복삼재) is a folk saying that, according to the flow of the saju, trouble is slight, or good things even follow. The formula that record wealth and honor arrive when the incoming character is yongsin (용신) has not been confirmed as a rule of classical mingli. There is also no statistic that world-scale achievements clustered in samjae years. This page does not use the metaphor of coal becoming diamond.

## If only the word caution remains

Calling samjae a safety valve that the universe installed makes the folk calendar sound like a fact. The attitude that can remain is smaller. Some people use the three years as an excuse to look once more at large spending, at their words, and at their health. Caution is useful in years that are not samjae, too. It is not evidence that the calendar prevented a disaster.

It is not a prison sentence, and it does not reset by itself the way a season changes. There is no reason to read it as fear, and no reason to read it as a rite for taking the initiative back.
