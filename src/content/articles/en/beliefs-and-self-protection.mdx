## Beliefs do not change easily

Once we believe something, we tend to defend it. New information gets interpreted to fit, and facts that do not fit are uncomfortable enough to push away. This keeps our picture of the world stable, but it can also keep us from correcting wrong beliefs. This article covers six belief-related phenomena.

## Cognitive dissonance: when thought and action clash, the thought changes

Leon Festinger (1957) called the discomfort of holding conflicting thoughts or actions **cognitive dissonance**. To reduce it, people often **change the thought** rather than the behaviour.

In Festinger and Carlsmith's (1959) experiment, participants who had just finished a boring task were asked to tell the next person it was fun. Those paid $1 later **rated the task as more enjoyable** than those paid $20. The $20 group could explain the lie by the money; $1 was not enough, so the $1 group adjusted their view — "actually, it was sort of interesting."

Festinger also studied a group that had predicted the end of the world (Festinger et al., 1956). When the day passed uneventfully, some members, far from abandoning the belief, said their faith had saved the world and **began spreading it more eagerly**. The more we have invested in a belief, the harder it is to give up.

**Check question**: "What would I lose by changing my mind, and is that blocking my judgement?"

## Confirmation bias: looking first for the evidence we want

**Confirmation bias** is the tendency to seek out and remember information that supports what we already think, while looking less for — and discounting — what contradicts it (Nickerson, 1998).

Wason (1960) told participants that the numbers "2-4-6" followed a rule and let them propose their own triples to find it. Most formed a hypothesis such as "even numbers rising by 2" and **tested only examples that fit it**, like 8-10-12. The real rule was "any increasing numbers." Few tried examples, such as 1-2-3, that could have shown their hypothesis was wrong.

**Check question**: "If I were wrong, what would I expect to see — and have I looked for it?"

## Placebo: belief changes how we feel

The **placebo effect** is feeling better after a treatment with no active ingredient. It is not the cure-all it is often made out to be. An analysis of trials comparing placebo directly with no treatment (Hróbjartsson & Gøtzsche, 2001) found little clear effect on objective measures such as blood tests, and small effects mainly on **self-reported symptoms such as pain**.

Intriguingly, some studies found effects even when patients knew they were receiving a placebo. Kaptchuk and colleagues (2010) gave people with irritable bowel syndrome pills openly labelled as placebos and reported greater improvement than in a no-treatment group.

**Check question**: "Beyond feeling better, has any measurable indicator changed?" For health problems, check with a professional rather than relying on feelings alone.

## Normalcy bias: "it will be like usual"

**Normalcy bias** is interpreting disaster warnings or danger signs as ordinary — "it'll be fine," "it was fine last time." Disaster sociologist Drabek (1986) documented how people who hear warnings often do not evacuate at once, instead spending time confirming with others and gathering more information. The wish to keep familiar routines outweighs new information about risk.

**Check question**: "If this time is different, what should I do right now?" Deciding evacuation routes and actions in advance reduces hesitation in the moment.

## Learned helplessness: do we learn helplessness, or fail to learn control?

In 1967 Seligman and Maier reported that dogs exposed to inescapable shocks later stayed passive even when escape was possible, and called this **learned helplessness**. The idea became a widely used framework for depression.

Fifty years later, drawing on neuroscience, the same researchers revised their own interpretation (Maier & Seligman, 2016). Becoming passive under prolonged bad events is the **default response**; what people and animals learn is rather **a sense that they can control things**. Overcoming helplessness, then, depends less on erasing helplessness than on **building small experiences of control**.

**Check question**: "What part of this situation can I change, even a little?"

## The overview effect: when perspective shifts dramatically

Beliefs do not always hold firm. Writer Frank White (1987) described how astronauts who looked down on Earth from space, overwhelmed by a planet without borders, experienced a shift in worldview — the **overview effect**. Yaden and colleagues (2016) analysed it as an experience of awe and self-transcendence. Vast, overwhelming experiences can open cracks in rigid beliefs.

**Check question**: "Have I recently met an experience or perspective unfamiliar enough to widen my thinking?"

## Checklist at a glance

| Phenomenon | Check question |
|---|---|
| Cognitive dissonance | Is what I would lose by changing my mind blocking my judgement? |
| Confirmation bias | If I were wrong, what would I see? |
| Placebo | Beyond feelings, has any measure changed? |
| Normalcy bias | If this time is different, what should I do now? |
| Learned helplessness | What can I change, even a little? |
| Overview effect | Have I met a perspective that widens my thinking? |

The urge to protect our beliefs keeps us steady, but it also keeps wrong beliefs in place. The simplest antidote is the habit of asking ourselves the opposite question.
