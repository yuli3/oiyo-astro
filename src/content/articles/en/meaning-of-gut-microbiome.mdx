## 1. The Gut-Brain Axis

The gut is more than a digestive tube. It exchanges signals with the brain through the vagus nerve, the immune system, hormones and substances that gut microbes produce. Researchers call this two-way link the gut-brain axis.

You may have read that 90 to 95 percent of your serotonin is made in the gut. For the body as a whole, that figure is roughly right: most serotonin is produced by enterochromaffin cells in the gut lining, and gut bacteria help regulate how much they make. But gut serotonin does not cross the blood-brain barrier. The serotonin that acts in the brain is made in the brain. Gut serotonin mostly works on bowel movement, platelets and other parts of the body, so calling the gut the source of your «happiness hormone» goes beyond the evidence.

Animal studies show that gut microbes can change behavior and stress responses. Whether, and how much, they shape mood in people is still an open question.

## 2. Not Simply Good vs Bad

You may also have seen claims of an ideal ratio, such as 85 percent good bacteria to 15 percent bad. No study supports a fixed ratio like that. Researchers tend to look at diversity and at what the community as a whole does, because the same species can be harmless in one setting and cause problems in another.

- **Helpful roles:** many gut bacteria ferment fiber into short-chain fatty acids, make vitamin K and some B vitamins, and compete with harmful microbes for space.
- **Harmful roles:** some microbes, or an overgrowth of them, can produce toxins or drive inflammation.
- **About the word «probiotic»:** it does not mean any good bacterium in your gut. It means live microorganisms that give a health benefit when taken in adequate amounts. Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium strains are common examples.

The idea that gut bacteria make you crave sugar is a hypothesis researchers have proposed, not an established finding.

## 3. What Your Stool Says (Bristol Stool Scale)

The Bristol Stool Scale sorts stool into seven types. Types 3 and 4, shaped like a smooth sausage, are generally considered typical. Hard lumps (types 1 and 2) point toward constipation, and watery stool (types 6 and 7) toward diarrhea. A single odd day is common. If changes last, or you notice blood, black stool, unexplained weight loss or ongoing pain, please see a doctor.

## 4. Feeding Your Microbes

**Prebiotics** are substances that gut microbes use selectively and that benefit your health, mostly types of fiber found in onions, garlic, leeks, bananas, oats and legumes.

- **Helpful:** a varied diet with vegetables, fruits, whole grains and legumes. Fermented foods such as kimchi, yogurt and kefir are a separate group: in a 10-week trial with 36 adults, a diet high in fermented foods increased microbiome diversity and lowered some markers of inflammation.
- **Disruptive:** diets low in fiber and high in ultra-processed food are linked to lower diversity. Antibiotics also disturb the microbiome, but when a doctor prescribes them they are necessary, so do not stop or skip them for the sake of your gut. Chronic stress is linked to changes in the microbiome as well.

## 5. Conclusion: You Are Never Alone

One hamburger will not decide tomorrow's mood, and one salad will not fix it. What shapes your gut microbes is the pattern of what you eat over weeks and months. Your body carries trillions of passengers. A varied, fiber-rich diet is a good way to look after them.

### FAQ

**Q: Do I need to take probiotic supplements?**
A: Not necessarily. The effects depend on the strain and on the condition, and most supplement strains pass through without settling in your gut for good. There is reasonable evidence for some uses and weak evidence for many others. Diet tends to shape your microbiome more over time. If you are thinking about supplements, especially with a weakened immune system or a serious illness, please ask a doctor or pharmacist.

**Q: What do antibiotics do to the gut?**
A: They reduce harmful and helpful bacteria alike. In many people the microbiome largely recovers over weeks to months, though some changes can last longer. Taking probiotics afterwards is not always helpful: one small study found it slowed the return of the original microbiome. Take antibiotics exactly as prescribed, and ask your doctor what is right for you.
