### Starting with the name, and the limit

After a life-shaking event, some people say they see themselves, other people, or life differently. Richard Tedeschi and Lawrence Calhoun called that kind of report **posttraumatic growth (PTG)**. The center of the model is their 1996 Posttraumatic Growth Inventory and their 2004 paper on the concept.

This article explains that model. It is not a promise that trauma produces growth. It does not say that most people grow. No rate like that appears in the sources cited here. PTG is not a diagnosis, and it is not a treatment. **This article is not a diagnosis and not a treatment plan.**

PTSD and PTG are not steps on one ladder. A person can report growth and still be in deep distress. Another person can have the distress without the growth. A growth report does not mean the wound is finished (Tedeschi & Calhoun, 2004).

---

### 1. What the model means by trauma: a shaken assumptive world

Ronnie Janoff-Bulman wrote that people live inside an **assumptive world**: beliefs such as "I am reasonably safe," "the world is fairly predictable," and "I have worth" (Janoff-Bulman, 1992). Tedeschi and Calhoun's model borrows that idea. In it, trauma is an event that badly shakes those assumptions.

The shake can look like this.

- **Shock:** How could this happen to me?
- **Confusion:** The world no longer feels safe, and life no longer feels in your hands.
- **A crack in old beliefs:** Values, plans, and the picture of yourself no longer stand as they did.

That collapse hurts. Hurt is not the start of growth. The picture of an old house that must fall before a stronger one can rise does not fit. Rebuilding is not automatic, and a better result is not guaranteed. Some people make a way of living they value. Some live with pain for a long time. The model allows both.

### 2. The tunnel of pain: intrusive rumination

Soon afterward, memory can return when you do not want it. Nightmares, flashes of the scene, and a "why" that will not leave are examples. This research line calls that **intrusive rumination**.

Intrusive rumination is not something this article will call the brain's natural healing process. It is part of distress, and it can overlap with post-traumatic stress symptoms. It does not, by itself, settle a diagnosis. It is also not evidence that growth has already begun.

If the distress is severe, lasts, or threatens daily life or safety, a qualified clinician is the right person to ask. This page cannot sort out what you are living with.

### 3. Deliberate rumination is not a switch

The same researchers later distinguish **deliberate rumination**: choosing to think about what happened and about how to live now.

In one study of U.S. and Japanese samples, recent deliberate rumination had the stronger link with PTG scores at the time (Taku, Cann, Tedeschi, & Calhoun, 2009). That is a statistical association in those samples. It is not a personal forecast that thinking hard enough will bring growth. The model does not say everyone moves from intrusive rumination to deliberate rumination. Returning toward a former baseline, and reported growth, are not the same word.

### 4. Five domains are questionnaire domains

The 1996 inventory sorts reports of change into five areas (Tedeschi & Calhoun, 1996).

1. **Personal strength.** A report that trust in oneself, after enduring this, has changed.
2. **New possibilities.** A report that priorities or paths look different.
3. **Relating to others.** A report about who is actually there, and about other people's pain.
4. **Appreciation of life.** A report of seeing ordinary days again.
5. **Spiritual change.** A report that questions of meaning or faith have shifted. Later writing sometimes widens this area toward existential questions. This article stays with the five names from the 1996 inventory.

A high score is not a diagnosis of growth. A low score is not a failure. A self-report can be a real change, or a story a person holds in order to bear something hard. The model does not finish that distinction for you.

---

### Closing: the repaired bowl is a metaphor

**Kintsugi**, repairing cracked pottery with lacquer and gold, is a picture some people like. It is not evidence that a broken place becomes more beautiful. It is not a law that injury makes a person deeper or wider.

In this model, growth is not pain disappearing. It is a report that, with pain still there, a person sees self, relationships, or life differently. This article does not teach a method for producing that report.
