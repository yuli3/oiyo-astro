## What Is Stress?

**Stress** is how the body and mind respond when demands show up. A short push can sometimes help you focus. When the strain goes on and on, it wears people out. Making room to rest, and asking for help when you need it, matters.

This page is not a medical test. It describes four response patterns — tension, withdrawal, anger, and avoidance — and practical ways to cope with each. If pain, sleep, or appetite stays disrupted, do not stop at the practices below. See a clinician.

---

## 1. Four Stress Responses

### Type 1: Tension

**What it often looks like**: tight muscles, headaches, a stiff neck and shoulders, shallow breathing, a body that will not settle

**Situations that often come with it**: perfectionism, carrying too much responsibility, deadline pressure

**What you can try**:
- Tighten muscle groups from the feet upward, then let each one go
- 4-7-8 breathing: inhale for 4 seconds, hold for 7, exhale for 8
- A warm shower or a stretch, to ease a body that has clenched

If the pain goes on, see a clinician. The practices above do not replace a diagnosis.

---

### Type 2: Withdrawal

**What it often looks like**: low energy, little motivation, sleeping much more than usual, pulling away from people, a shift in appetite

**Situations that often come with it**: a sense of helplessness, repeated setbacks, few people to lean on

**What you can try**:
- Do one very small thing. It does not have to be an achievement
- Take a short walk, even a few minutes outside
- Ask for help. When you want to disappear, contact one person

---

### Type 3: Anger

**What it often looks like**: irritability, reactions that grow too fast, speaking or acting on impulse

**Situations that often come with it**: a strong wish to stay in control, unfairness that lands hard, a boundary that feels crossed

**What you can try**:
- Take a short walk and let your body change places
- Write it down. Look for the situations that set the anger off
- Before it grows, say what you need, or ask for help

---

### Type 4: Avoidance

**What it often looks like**: putting the problem off, overeating, drinking, or binge-watching, going blank or turning your attention somewhere else

**Situations that often come with it**: anxiety you would rather not face, fear of conflict, a stretch of low regard for yourself

**What you can try**:
- Split the avoided task into one step you can do today
- When anxiety rises, watch your breath for a moment without judging it
- Break a problem that feels huge into smaller pieces

## 2. At Work

| Situation | In the moment | A longer change |
|-----------|-------------------|---------------------|
| Overload | Learn to say no, and renegotiate priorities | Build a way to delegate, and redefine the role |
| Conflict with a manager or colleague | Wait through a cooling-off period, then use I-statements | Set a boundary, and talk with HR if you need to |
| Early burnout | Treat rest as an investment in keeping the work going | Consider redesigning the job, or changing the setting |
| Presentation or evaluation anxiety | Belly breathing, and prepare so less is unknown | Catch the thought again: a mistake will not be catastrophic |

<Callout type="warning" title="When to talk with a professional">
If any of the following lasts two weeks or more, talk with a mental health professional.
- Exhaustion or loss of motivation that does not lift
- Sleep trouble: difficulty falling asleep, or sleeping far more than usual
- Trouble concentrating that gets in the way of daily life
- Thoughts of self-harm or of death

**Lines in the United States** (24 hours):
- **988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline**: call or text **988**
- **Crisis Text Line**: text HOME to **741741**
- **SAMHSA National Helpline**: 1-800-662-4357

**Lines in Korea** (24 hours):
- Suicide prevention counseling: **109**
- Mental health crisis counseling: **1577-0199**
</Callout>
