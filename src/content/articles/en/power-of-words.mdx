## Separate what you say from how someone responds

How you express an opinion can make a conversation easier or harder to continue. It cannot control another person's response. Actions, circumstances, differences in power and past experiences also matter.

This is not a way to diagnose someone or guarantee reconciliation. It helps distinguish what you can change in your own language from what needs clarification or a boundary.

## When a joke hurts, ask about its impact

[Martin and colleagues' humor study](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0092-6566(02)00534-2) developed and initially validated a questionnaire covering affiliative, self-enhancing, aggressive and self-defeating humor. It offers distinctions for reflecting on whether you share enjoyment or put someone down. It was not a causal experiment proving that one joke inevitably destroys a relationship.

If someone says a remark bothered them, “Why are you so sensitive? It was a joke” closes off that explanation. You can ask what was uncomfortable and agree on what not to repeat. Equally, not laughing does not by itself establish hostile intent or reveal someone's character.

[APA's definition of gaslighting](https://dictionary.apa.org/gaslight) concerns manipulation that makes another person doubt their perceptions, experiences or understanding of events. Not every hurtful joke is gaslighting. Look at behavior and context rather than diagnosing someone from one sentence.

## Listening means checking understanding, not simply staying silent

Tone and facial expressions can provide clues, but a sigh or a glance does not reveal someone's thoughts with certainty. Try checking: “Are you tired, or is this subject uncomfortable?”

[Rogers and Farson's writing on active listening](https://www.gordontraining.com/free-workplace-articles/active-listening/) describes attending to both content and feelings, then reflecting back what you understood. You might ask, “It sounds as though you want me to listen rather than offer solutions. Have I understood?”

Supportive quiet is different from refusing to respond as punishment. If you need a pause, explain why and when you intend to return: “I'm too upset to talk clearly. I'd like a 20-minute break, then to continue.” This duration is an OIYO example, not a scientifically established optimum.

## An “I” statement still needs a clear observation and request

“I feel that you're selfish” starts with “I” but still evaluates the other person. [CNVC's training materials](https://www.cnvc.org/certification/cpp/preparation/) and the publisher's [four-part guide](https://www.nonviolentcommunication.com/pdf_files/4part_nvc_process.pdf) distinguish observation from evaluation, feelings from thoughts, needs from particular requests, and requests from demands.

The following is an example written by OIYO, not a research finding or a quotation.

| Element | Example | What to distinguish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Observation | You arrived 20 minutes after our agreed time without a message. | A specific event, not “You're always irresponsible.” |
| Feeling | I felt worried and frustrated while waiting. | Feelings rather than the interpretation “You disrespected me.” |
| Need | I'd like to be able to plan my time. | A need rather than an imposed solution. |
| Request | Could you message me beforehand if you're running late? | A feasible action with room for refusal or another proposal. |

You do not need to recite four steps every time. “I worry when you're late without a message. Could you let me know next time?” may be enough. Someone can still become defensive or decline. This is a framework for examining expression, not a formula for success.

## Expectations can influence behavior, not determine the future

[Merton's paper on self-fulfilling prophecy](https://www.jstor.org/stable/4609267) describes a social process in which an initially false definition of a situation prompts behavior that makes it come true. It is not an experiment showing that repeating positive phrases changes everyone.

Expecting someone to fail may affect whether you give them an opportunity. Encouragement alone cannot replace skills, time, resources or their own wishes. You can make support concrete: “Where are you stuck? Is there something I could review?”

## Apologies and boundaries include what happens next

Better wording cannot replace acknowledging a mistake or keeping a promise. Explain what you did, what impact you heard about and what you will change. Do not require immediate forgiveness or reassurance.

These are also OIYO examples, not guaranteed solutions.

| A phrase that can close discussion | Another way to try |
| --- | --- |
| It was a joke. Why are you so sensitive? | I'd like to understand what bothered you. I won't use that topic for a joke again. |
| You always ignore me. | Another topic started while I was explaining. Could you hear the rest now? |
| I said sorry. Get over it. | I'm sorry for what I said. I won't demand that you feel fine immediately. |
| Kind words solve everything. | I can change my wording, but we may still disagree. I need to decide on my boundaries too. |

When threats or coercion are involved, responsibility for better communication must not be shifted onto the person being harmed. Ending the conversation, moving somewhere safe, or seeking help from someone you trust or a local support service are also options.

## Choose one thing to check in your next conversation

You can work on your expression and actions, not remake someone else's personality. Describe an observable event instead of judging character, ask for a specific action, and check what you heard. Even if the outcome does not change, you can clarify what was agreed and what remains unresolved.
