## 1. An offset is not a time zone

A UTC offset tells you how a local clock differs from UTC at a particular instant. A location-based time zone supplies the rules for finding that offset on a date. Dividing the world into 24 equal longitude bands is an introductory picture, not an accurate description of civil time. Boundaries and clock-change rules reflect political decisions.

The IANA Time Zone Database records local-time history and changing rules. Use a location identifier such as `America/New_York` instead of assuming UTC−5 applies throughout the year. Abbreviations such as CST can mean different things in different regions.

| Information | What it establishes | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| UTC instant | A point on the shared timeline | The local calendar date |
| Offset, such as UTC−5 | The difference at that instant | Future daylight-saving rules |
| Location-based zone | Rules for converting local time and UTC | A person's working hours |

GMT and UTC often give the same zero-offset clock reading in everyday scheduling, but London is not on GMT all year: it uses BST during its summer-time period. Communicate a full date and location-based zone rather than relying on an abbreviation.

## 2. Check each city's clock-change calendar

Under the rules checked on 11 October 2026, New York observes US daylight saving from the second Sunday in March to the first Sunday in November; London changes on the last Sundays in March and October. New York and London are usually five hours apart when both use standard time or both use summer time, but four hours apart during the transition gaps. “Four hours apart all summer” is incorrect.

NIST documents the US rules and exceptions; GOV.UK publishes UK change dates. Not every jurisdiction observes daylight saving. Rules can change, so keep calendar and operating-system time-zone data updated. A local time may be skipped or repeated during a transition: “01:30” without a date, zone and ambiguity check may not identify one instant.

## 3. Calculate actual working-hour overlap

The examples below assume everyone works 09:00–18:00 locally, without lunch breaks, holidays or personal restrictions. They use standard-time offsets: Seoul UTC+9, Beijing UTC+8, London UTC+0, New York UTC−5 and Los Angeles UTC−8. These are worked examples, not a year-round timetable.

| Participants | Shared interval, Seoul time | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| Seoul, Tokyo and Beijing | 10:00–18:00 | Beijing's 09:00 is Seoul's 10:00 |
| Seoul and London | Only the 18:00 boundary | London's day starts when Seoul's ends; no positive-length overlap |
| Seoul and New York | None | New York's day starts at 23:00 in Seoul |
| Seoul and Los Angeles | None | Los Angeles starts at 02:00 on the following Seoul date |

A call outside someone's working hours is a negotiated exception, not working-hour overlap. Convert each participant's dated start and end to a shared timeline. Take the latest start and earliest end: if the end is not later than the start, there is no usable interval. Subtract breaks and other unavailable periods afterward.

## 4. Decide what a recurring event preserves

| Intention | Scheduling basis | What may change |
|---|---|---|
| A New York team's daily 09:00 meeting | New York wall-clock time | Other cities' local meeting times |
| A system action at a fixed UTC hour | UTC | Local wall-clock times |
| Sharing inconvenient attendance | An explicit rotation | Review each person's actual burden |

When offsets change, one recurring event cannot preserve both a fixed UTC hour and the same local hour in every city. For a one-off deadline, state the full date, zone and converted instant. For a recurring meeting, agree which city's clock owns the recurrence and review upcoming instances around transitions.

## 5. Reduce unnecessary simultaneous attendance

Written decisions can include an owner, context, requested action and response deadline. Reserve live meetings for matters that need them. There is no universal “less than two hours of overlap” threshold or ideal weekly meeting count: consider the work, urgency, accessibility and participants' constraints. Rotate unavoidable early or late calls.

For market-related planning, a time conversion is not a trading calendar. Check the exchange's current official session schedule, holidays, early closes, breaks and the broker's supported sessions. Pre-market trading is not futures trading. This article does not establish a profitable trading window.
