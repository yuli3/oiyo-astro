## What the word is doing

Empathy is a name for noticing another person's feeling and point of view, and sometimes for being stirred by it. It is not one ability. A common split is affective empathy, an emotional echo, and cognitive empathy, working out how a situation looks to someone else. That split is useful. It is not the only model.

This page is not medical advice.

## What a pain study measured

Tania Singer and colleagues studied empathy for pain, not empathy for every feeling. When people watched someone else in pain, activity overlapped in affective regions, including the anterior insula and the anterior cingulate. The overlap was not the sensory part of pain, and it was not the whole pain experience. It is not a blanket claim that the same brain areas light up whenever one person senses another.

Singer, T., Seymour, B., O'Doherty, J., Kaube, H., Dolan, R. J., and Frith, C. D. (2004). Empathy for pain involves the affective but not sensory components of pain. *Science*, 303, 1157–1162.

## Four scores, not one

Mark H. Davis (1983) treated empathy as more than one difference among people. The Interpersonal Reactivity Index has four subscales: perspective taking, fantasy, empathic concern, and personal distress. A single empathy score hides that mix. Someone can take another person's perspective and still report little personal distress, or feel a lot of distress without much perspective taking.

## A pit, drawn in a talk

Brené Brown's RSA talk uses a pit as a popular illustration, not as a study. In the picture, one response climbs down and sits in the dark with the person, and the other speaks from the rim. The image teaches a contrast between staying with someone and commenting from outside. It does not measure either response.

## Compassion fatigue is a term, not a diagnosis

Caregivers use compassion fatigue for a worn-down state after long exposure to other people's distress. Charles Figley used the term in that literature. Patterns people often discuss include numbness toward stories that used to matter, feeling flooded by someone else's pain, wanting to pull away from the work or the relationship, and a more cynical tone.

Those are commonly discussed patterns. They are not a diagnosis. If exhaustion is severe, or daily life is hard to manage, see a clinician.

People also pass along these suggestions. They are not proven treatments.

- Speak to yourself with the kindness you would offer a friend.
- Notice the limit of emotional labor, and leave time to recover.
- After work, mark a short transition: a walk, music, or a few lines in a notebook.
- Talk with a supervisor or a colleague, if you have that room.

## Self-compassion in Neff's model

Kristin Neff (2003) describes self-compassion as an attitude toward yourself. In her model it sits near compassion for other people. That is a relationship inside the model. It is not a proven direct proportion: the work does not show that self-compassion raises empathy for others by a fixed amount. Her site, [self-compassion.org](https://self-compassion.org), collects the writing.

Neff, K. D. (2003). Self-compassion: An alternative conceptualization of a healthy attitude toward oneself. *Self and Identity*, 2(2), 77–101.

## Practices you can try

Listening, perspective taking, and suspending a first judgment can stay on the list as suggestions. They do not guarantee that the other person feels understood. Some studies find that practice can shift questionnaire scores. That is not proof that training works in general, and it is not proof that a script produces understanding.

### Listening

Turn toward the person and put the phone away. Let them finish. Before advice, you can say that what they describe sounds heavy, and ask whether you heard it right.

### Perspective taking

Ask, quietly, how this could look from their history. The question matters more when their values differ from yours. You can still be wrong.

### Holding a first judgment

If the first thought is that you do not understand, treat that as missing context. Ask one more question before you argue.

A three-part suggestion some people like — name the feeling, say the reaction is understandable, then stay nearby — is only a suggestion. It does not by itself make someone feel understood.

## At work, with a partner, and with a child

These are practical suggestions, not proven techniques.

At work, you might ask what else is going on before feedback, and talk about a next try instead of only about blame.

With a partner, you might ask what was hardest before you explain your side.

With a child, you might name the feeling you think you see, and allow the feeling before you talk about the behavior. You can be wrong about the name. Asking is part of the suggestion.

The questions on this site are not a diagnosis, and they are not Davis's index.
