## Look at what you compared, not just who you compared with

Using a colleague’s promotion to gauge your own work and consulting other people’s opinions before a decision both involve comparison, but they are not the same situation. Identifying the criterion and the person can make reflection more specific than saying “I compare myself a lot.”

This article explains concepts and research limits for personal reflection. Comparing yourself with someone does not by itself establish jealousy, inferiority, or a mental health problem. The everyday examples and suggestions are editorial explanations, not treatments or training with demonstrated effects.

## Festinger’s theory starts with evaluating opinions and abilities

[Festinger’s original 1954 paper](https://www2.psych.ubc.ca/~schaller/528Readings/Festinger1954.pdf) concerns motivation to evaluate opinions and abilities, consulting others when objective nonsocial standards are difficult to use, and reduced comparison with people whose opinions or abilities differ greatly. Because the original is a scanned document, this discussion compares selected hypotheses with [the theoretical account in the 1999 INCOM development paper](https://pure.rug.nl/ws/files/257117700/Individual_differences_in_social_comparison_The_development.pdf). It is not based on a new complete reading of the 1954 paper.

In a fictional editorial example, a colleague who completed a similar task may offer a reference point when assessment criteria are unclear. But working hours, support, and responsibilities may differ. The final outcome alone is not enough to judge a difference in ability; the conditions also need examination.

A “similar person” does not mean someone whose age, gender, and circumstances all match yours. Nor do we extend this theory into a law explaining safety, belonging, or the value of an entire life.

## Upward and downward describe direction, not an emotional outcome

**Upward comparison** involves someone who appears better off on the criterion in question. **Downward comparison** involves someone who appears worse off on that criterion. Neither describes a person’s overall superiority or inferiority. Direction alone does not determine whether you feel inspired, disappointed, grateful, or afraid.

[Buunk and colleagues’ 1990 study](https://taylorlab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/5/2014/10/1990_The-Affective-Consequences-of-Social-Comparison.pdf) examined reports from 55 cancer patients and 632 married participants, in which both directions could accompany positive or negative feelings. Findings from these samples are not a universal formula for emotion. The [abstract of a 1995 follow-up study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7551772/) also discusses responses in relation to identification with a comparison target and perceived control; its full text was not reviewed here.

## INCOM’s dimensions are not upward and downward

[Gibbons and Buunk’s 1999 development paper](https://pure.rug.nl/ws/files/257117700/Individual_differences_in_social_comparison_The_development.pdf) introduces the Iowa-Netherlands Comparison Orientation Measure, which examines individual comparison orientation. Its dimensions are **ability comparison** and **opinion comparison**: comparing ability or performance with others, and consulting others about opinions or reactions. Upward and downward describe the direction of the target, a separate distinction.

The original INCOM has 11 items: six related to ability and five to opinions. It uses a five-point agreement format and includes two reverse-scored items. Development and validation in English and Dutch and in US and Dutch samples do not automatically validate an arbitrary translation or modified items. Comparison orientation scores are not clinical diagnoses or boundaries between normal and abnormal.

The current activity averages responses on each dimension, averages those two values, and displays four descriptions using its own cutoffs. Those cutoffs and the bars’ 0–100 conversion are not clinical thresholds or population percentiles. The activity does not calculate upward and downward comparison separately, so it cannot be described as measuring those directions.

Do not infer from a result alone that you have firm personal standards, compare at the same level as most people, or have damaged self-esteem. Use it to organize your reflections, not for ability assessment, hiring, or diagnosis. Availability in six languages does not mean validity has been established in each language.

## Read social media research alongside its design and usage conditions

[Verduyn and colleagues’ 2015 study](https://ppw.kuleuven.be/okp/_pdf/Verduyn2015PFUUA.pdf) examined active and passive Facebook use and affective well-being. The first study randomly assigned 84 college students to ten minutes of active or passive use; 67 remained in the analysis after exclusions. Lower mood in the passive-use group was observed that evening, not immediately.

The second study collected reports from 89 people five times a day for six days and examined relationships among passive use, envy, and subsequent mood. Causal inference within a particular experimental condition differs from relationships in observational data. An envy mediation relationship in experience sampling should not become a proven causal pathway. Neither study established a decline in life satisfaction.

These findings do not cover every social platform, every usage pattern, or everyone’s long-term mental health. Repeated discomfort after use may be worth reflecting on, but we do not diagnose it as a validated sign of an “overloaded comparison system.”

## Reflect on a particular comparison

These are optional editorial suggestions, not prescriptions with guaranteed effects. Recall one comparison and note whether it concerned work performance, opinions, or something else. Then look at differences in circumstances and whether you actually know anything about the process behind the outcome.

You might also ask what you wanted in that situation. Recognition, security, or learning could come to mind, but an answer does not establish the cause of an emotion. You do not have to eliminate every comparison or seek relief in others’ difficulties. Muting an account or taking a break is also an option, not a method with guaranteed therapeutic effects.
