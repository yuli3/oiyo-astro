## Workaholism is not another word for diligence

"Working a lot because you love your work" and "working constantly because you cannot stop" can look alike from the outside. Both involve frequent late nights, checking work messages on days off, and caring a great deal about results. Psychologically, though, the two states differ in important ways.

A passionate person may work long hours yet still keep some balance with the other areas of their life. Workaholism, by contrast, brings anxiety or guilt whenever you are not working, and makes it hard to cut back even when your health, relationships, and rest are suffering. This article is a guide for self-reflection and does not replace a medical diagnosis.

---

## The seven components of the Bergen Work Addiction Scale (BWAS)

One tool often cited in research on workaholism is the **Bergen Work Addiction Scale (BWAS)**, proposed by Norwegian researcher Cecilie Schou Andreassen and colleagues. It applies the framework used to study addictive behaviors to work, and describes the core features of work addiction through seven components.

| Component | How it shows up at work |
|------|----------------------|
| Salience | Work fills most of your thoughts, and it stays on your mind even while you rest |
| Mood modification | Working brings relief, excitement, or a sense of control; stopping feels uncomfortable |
| Tolerance | You need to work longer and harder to get the same satisfaction as before |
| Withdrawal | When you cannot work, restlessness, irritability, and guilt grow |
| Conflict | Work comes first even when it clashes with family, friends, health, or sleep |
| Relapse | You resolve to cut back, but soon slip back into overworking |
| Problems | You cannot lower the intensity of your work even after your body or mind starts to suffer |

These seven components raise a question that matters more than "Do I work a lot?": **Is work expanding my life, or is it pushing the other parts of my life aside?**

## The difference between passion and addiction

People who love their work get absorbed in it. In that kind of absorption, time flies, the challenge fits your skills, and you finish tired but fulfilled. Workaholism, on the other hand, does not bring ease when the work is done. Even after an achievement, a sense of "not enough yet" lingers, and anxiety rises unless you find the next task.

The key difference between the two is **a sense of choice**. Passion is closer to "I do it because I want to," while workaholism is closer to "I do it because I can't bear not doing it." A passionate person can adjust their work when an important relationship or a health issue calls for it, whereas a workaholic pattern makes any adjustment feel like failure or a threat.

Another difference is **the breadth of identity**. Work can be an important part of life, but it becomes risky when it turns into the whole of your worth. Performance becomes self-esteem, and a mistake feels like a failure of your very being. Then even time off looks less like recovery and more like falling behind.

## Why is it so hard to stop?

Workaholism cannot be explained by personality alone. Anxiety, perfectionism, the need for approval, financial pressure, and competitive workplace cultures all play a part. People who strongly believe "I am only lovable if I am useful" are especially likely to seek security through work.

The trouble is that work does lower anxiety in the short term. When worry builds up, getting one more task done brings a brief feeling of control. But as this repeats, rest becomes more and more unfamiliar, and the body stays in a state of tension. In the end, work stops being a tool for solving problems and becomes a way of avoiding feelings.

Organizations matter too. A messaging culture that expects instant replies, an atmosphere that reads overtime as dedication, and vague performance standards all reinforce workaholic patterns. If you only ask yourself "Is the problem me?", it is easy to miss the structural pressure around you.

---

## Recovery is not learning to be lazy

Recovering from workaholism does not mean loving your work less. Rather, it means creating a healthy distance between you and your work so that you can keep working for the long run.

First, set **small boundaries around working time**. A perfect work-life balance is hard to build from day one. What you need are small, observable boundaries, such as turning off notifications for 30 minutes after work, or keeping one block of a weekend morning free of work.

Second, track **recovery indicators, not just performance**. Check the signals that your body is recovering: hours of sleep, meals, walks, conversations with family, hobbies that don't require focus. In workaholic patterns, performance metrics tend to grow while recovery indicators quietly disappear.

Third, learn **ways to deal with anxiety directly**. Simply writing down the guilt, the fear of falling behind, or the worry about not being recognized that surfaces when you stop working can slow down your automatic reactions. If needed, working through perfectionism or self-criticism with a counselor can also help.

## Questions to ask yourself

The following questions are for self-checking, not for diagnosis.

| Question | What to look at |
|------|-----------|
| Do I feel strong anxiety or guilt when I take a break? | Withdrawal and mood regulation |
| Have I tried to cut back on work and failed repeatedly? | Relapse pattern |
| Do I put work first even when relationships, health, or sleep suffer? | Conflict and problems |
| Without results, do I feel as if my worth has dropped? | A narrowing identity |

If you would like to look at your current pattern more closely, you can check signs related to workaholism with the [Workaholic Self-Test](/en/workaholic-test/). It is best to treat the result not as a label but as a starting point for a conversation.

---

## Key takeaways

- Workaholism is less about diligence and more about a repeating pattern in which it is hard to stop working.
- Andreassen's BWAS describes workaholism through seven components: salience, mood modification, tolerance, withdrawal, conflict, relapse, and problems.
- Passion leaves you with a sense of choice and recovery, while addiction keeps you working to ease anxiety and guilt.
- Recovery is not giving up work; it is the process of rebuilding work boundaries, recovery indicators, and ways of managing anxiety.
