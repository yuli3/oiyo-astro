## Capacity for change is not a guarantee of a chosen outcome

Neuroplasticity describes changes in nervous system structure or function in response to experience, injury and other conditions. [The NIH workshop consensus report](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3102236/) discusses both adaptive and maladaptive plasticity. It is a consensus and translational research report, not an original experiment. Plasticity is not always beneficial and does not mean you can create any personality you want.

First identify the change being discussed. A record of doing something more often, a questionnaire about how automatic it feels, an MRI difference and a cellular mechanism are different kinds of evidence. One does not establish all the others.

## What the juggling study measured

[Draganski and colleagues’ university-hosted original](https://dbm.neuro.uni-jena.de/pdf-files/Draganski-Nature.pdf) divided 24 people without juggling experience into learning and nonlearning control groups matched for age and sex. The learning group had a second MRI after three months of practice, when they could perform for at least 60 seconds, and a third after another three months without practice. The performance criterion was an experimental condition, not a habit goal recommended to readers.

Voxel-based morphometry (VBM) compared small volume units in the images. Grey matter changes were observed around bilateral hMT/V5 and the left posterior intraparietal sulcus in the learning group, with some reduction after stopping practice. The nonlearning group showed no grey matter changes over the same period. This was a group comparison for a particular task and schedule, not direct observation of cells.

A grey matter difference on MRI does not establish that myelin increased, new neurons appeared or a particular microscopic mechanism caused the change. The paper states that the microscopic causes are unclear. It did not directly test anxiety treatment or personality redesign. Improved juggling and reduced worry are not interchangeable outcomes.

## Habit automaticity and the meaning of 66 days

[Lally and colleagues’ Wiley abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ejsp.674) describes 96 people repeating an eating, drinking or activity behaviour in the same context over 12 weeks. Automaticity was self-reported. The study did not image neural circuits or measure an anxiety recovery schedule.

In the [results returned from the university repository copy](https://repositorio.ispa.pt/bitstream/10400.12/3364/1/IJSP_998-1009.pdf), 66 days is the estimated median time to reach 95% of the modelled automaticity asymptote among 39 participants whose data fitted the model well. The range of 18 to 254 days is also a model estimate. It is not the mean for all 96 people or a direct 254-day observation of every participant. Direct access to the repository full text failed; these detailed results were checked through search returns.

## Myelin and neurogenesis are different questions

[McKenzie and colleagues’ 2014 abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25324381/) concerns blocking new myelin formation during learning of a particular motor skill in mice. This animal experiment is not a formula explaining every human skill, angry response or addiction. Feeling that practice went well is not a measurement of your myelin.

For new neurons in the adult human hippocampus, [UCSF’s 2018 institutional report](https://www.ucsf.edu/news/2018/03/409986/birth-new-neurons-human-hippocampus-ends-childhood) and [Columbia’s 2018 institutional report](https://www.cuimc.columbia.edu/news/even-old-brains-can-make-new-neurons-study-finds) describe conflicting study conclusions. We read the institutional reports, not the full texts of both original papers. Two reports from 2018 do not establish today’s scientific consensus or that lifelong neurogenesis directly causes trauma recovery. Learning, neurogenesis and clinical recovery require separate evidence.

## Limits of BDNF, rest and mindset claims

[The 2016 acute exercise abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27450438/) concerns acute exercise and cognitive tasks in healthy young adults. BDNF indicators measured peripherally, such as in blood, cannot be treated as measurements of changes inside your brain or long-term personality change. [The fasting pilot abstract with 28 participants](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33376976/) reports decreased serum BDNF after a particular four-week fasting regimen. It is a counterexample to “fasting always raises BDNF,” not proof that every fasting method is ineffective or must be prohibited. This article does not recommend HIIT, fasting or DHA for rewiring a brain.

[The Yoga Nidra abstract with 22 participants](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36731199/) is a preliminary study of physiological and sleep measures. It does not establish that NSDR, a label for nonsleep rest, and NREM sleep are the same state or share an identical mechanism of synaptic strengthening.

[The 2023 mindset abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36442746/) concerns an association between mindset scores and grey matter volume. It does not establish that thinking “I am bad at maths” causes pruning of maths circuits. Distinguish behaviour change, peripheral measures and imaging correlations before attaching biological promises to everyday advice.

## Everyday records describe experience, not brain measurements

If you want to try language learning after lunch, you can note the context and what you actually did. The table is an editorial example, not a validated scale or treatment procedure. It guarantees neither a repetition count nor a completion deadline.

On a missed day, recording obstacles can inform the next attempt. It does not mean your brain failed or that you lacked willpower. If anxiety persists or interferes with everyday life, [the limits of anxiety and neuroplasticity explanations](/en/neuroplasticity-habit-formation/) distinguish professional support from everyday recording.

## What we checked in the sources

On 9 October 2026, we checked the NIH consensus report, the methods, results and mechanism limitations in Draganski’s original, the Wiley abstract and both institutional neurogenesis reports. Detailed Lally results were available through official repository search returns only. The PubMed sources were checked at abstract level. We did not review or reanalyse every full paper.

[NIMH’s generalized anxiety disorder guide](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/generalized-anxiety-disorder-gad) explains that lifestyle measures do not replace treatment suited to an individual’s situation. The learning and habit studies here do not establish anxiety treatment efficacy or the brain mechanisms of a particular self-training exercise.
