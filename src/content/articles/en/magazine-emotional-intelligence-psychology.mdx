## 「Success Isn't Only About IQ」

Someone with an IQ of 145 can sour the mood of a whole meeting because they don't notice how their colleagues feel. Someone with an IQ of 110 can calm a conflict between teammates and stay composed when things get tense. Which of them would you rather have leading your team?

**Emotional intelligence (EI, often called EQ)** was first proposed by psychologists Peter Salovey and John Mayer in 1990 and popularized by Daniel Goleman's 1995 book *Emotional Intelligence*. It has since become one of the most widely studied topics in psychology.

There are two broad families of models. Mayer and Salovey define emotional intelligence as an ability (perceiving, using, understanding, and managing emotions), measured with performance-based tests. Goleman's popular version is broader and also includes traits such as motivation and social skill. The five components below follow Goleman's framework.

---

## The Five Components of Emotional Intelligence

In Goleman's framework, emotional intelligence is made up of five core capacities.

### 1. Self-Awareness

**「I know what I'm feeling right now.」**

This is the ability to accurately recognize your own emotions, strengths, weaknesses, needs, and impulses. People with strong self-awareness understand how their feelings affect others.

When self-awareness is low: 「I'm not angry」 (while everything about their behavior says otherwise). If you can't recognize your emotional state, you can't regulate it.

**How to build it**: Keep an emotion journal (name what you felt during the day), notice body signals (tension, quick breathing, and so on), and set aside time for regular reflection.

---

### 2. Self-Regulation

**「Even when emotions rise, I don't act on impulse.」**

This is the ability to manage disruptive impulses and moods. It means adapting to changing situations and thinking before acting.

Signs of strong self-regulation:
- Feeling anger without exploding
- Not getting defensive when someone disagrees in a meeting
- Making important decisions based on values and judgment rather than the emotion of the moment

This isn't about suppressing emotions. It's about acknowledging them without letting them run your behavior.

---

### 3. Motivation

**「I'm driven by inner reasons, not just external rewards.」**

In the EQ context, motivation means **intrinsic motivation**: acting for the meaning and value of the work itself, beyond money, status, or recognition.

Signs of strong intrinsic motivation:
- Enjoying a challenge even when the outcome is uncertain
- Raising your own standards
- Returning to an optimistic approach after a setback

This is closely linked to optimism. A way of explaining events such as 「I failed this time, but I can try a different approach next time」 helps keep intrinsic motivation alive.

---

### 4. Empathy

**「I recognize and understand how others feel.」**

Understanding what other people feel is the foundation of social relationships. An empathetic leader understands the individual needs and motivations of team members and can encourage them in ways that resonate.

Empathy doesn't mean being swept away by other people's emotions. It means reading and understanding them while keeping your own footing.

---

### 5. Social Skill

**「I can manage relationships and move people in a good direction.」**

This includes building relationships, handling conflict, collaborating, and persuading. People with strong social skills find common ground, maintain wide networks, and can lead change.

It isn't manipulation or technique. It is genuine relationship management that grows out of empathy, self-awareness, and self-regulation working together.

---

## EQ vs. IQ: What Predicts Success?

In his 1995 book, Goleman wrote that IQ contributes at best about 20% to the factors that determine success in life, leaving 80% to other forces. That figure was a rough estimate, not a measured share for EQ, and the popular slogan 「EQ matters more than IQ」 goes beyond what the research shows.

Later meta-analyses give a more measured picture:

- Joseph and Newman (2010) found that emotional intelligence is related to job performance, but ability-based EI tests added little prediction once cognitive ability and personality were taken into account. Self-report (mixed-model) EI measures predicted more, partly because they overlap with personality and self-efficacy.
- The link was stronger in jobs with high emotional labor, such as customer service or care work, and weaker in jobs with few emotional demands.
- Cognitive ability remains one of the most consistent predictors of job performance overall.

In short, EQ doesn't replace IQ. It is one more piece of the picture that matters especially in emotionally demanding work and in relationships.

---

## Can EQ Be Developed?

**To some extent, yes.** A meta-analysis of emotional intelligence training studies (Mattingly & Kraiger, 2019) found moderate improvements after training. Many of the studies relied on self-report and short follow-up periods, so how long the gains last is still an open question.

Training programs commonly use practices such as naming emotions, mindfulness, feedback on how you come across to others, and deliberate practice in real conversations. Please treat this framework as a guide for self-reflection, not as a diagnosis.
