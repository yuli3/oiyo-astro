## 1. Starting point: a living tradition

The Maya built a civilization that paid close attention to numbers and to the sky. They developed a sign for zero independently, and the Dresden Codex preserves a Venus table based on a cycle of about 584 days.

When we talk about Maya mythology, it is worth remembering that this is not a museum piece. Daykeepers (ajq'ij) in the Guatemalan highlands still count days in the traditional 260-day cycle, and many communities keep their own rituals and languages. Because this is a culture people are living now, we try to introduce it through the sources rather than speak on its behalf.

## 2. The Popol Vuh: from mud to maize

The Popol Vuh is the sacred book of the K'iche' Maya. The text we have was written down in the Latin alphabet by K'iche' authors in the mid-16th century. It survived because the Dominican friar Francisco Ximénez copied it and translated it into Spanish in the early 18th century.

In it, the creator gods try several times to make beings who can speak and remember them:

- **People of mud** dissolved and could not stand, so they were abandoned.
- **People of wood** could talk and have children, but they had no hearts and did not remember their makers. They were destroyed by a flood and by their own household objects turning against them.
- At last the gods kneaded people out of **white and yellow maize**. This bond between maize and people remains an important image in many Maya communities today.

## 3. The Hero Twins: winning the ballgame in the underworld

The best-known part of the Popol Vuh is the story of the **Hero Twins, Hunahpu and Xbalanque**.

Their father and uncle had been summoned by the lords of Xibalba, the underworld, to play the ballgame, and were killed there. Later the twins are summoned too, and pass through a series of trials such as the Dark House, the Razor House and the Jaguar House. They see through the lords' tricks with wit rather than strength, and in the end they defeat the lords of Xibalba. At the close of the story the twins rise into the sky and become the sun and the moon (translations differ slightly on the details).

<HighlightBox type="insight" title="A way of reading">
  The Hero Twins story is often read as a narrative of death and renewal, and of meeting power with cleverness. That is a modern reading, and you can use it as material for reflection. It is not a Maya verdict on any individual's destiny.
</HighlightBox>

## 4. How the Maya counted time

The Maya calendar is made of several interlocking cycles:

- **Tzolk'in:** the numbers 1 to 13 and twenty day names advance together and return to the start after 13 × 20 = 260 days.
- **Haab':** a 365-day solar year made of eighteen 20-day months plus five days. Used together with the tzolk'in, the same combination returns every 52 years, a period known as the Calendar Round.
- **Long Count:** a running count of days from a starting point equivalent to 3114 BCE. Thirteen b'ak'tuns last about 5,125 years and ended around December 2012.

## 5. Modern readings: Dreamspell and the galactic signature

Phrases you often see online, such as Red Dragon, Blue Monkey, Magnetic Tone or your Kin, do not come from ancient Maya texts. They come from the American writer José Argüelles, who published *The Mayan Factor* in 1987 and, with Lloydine Argüelles, released Dreamspell (the 13 Moon calendar) in 1990. The slogan Time is Art also comes from this modern system.

A few things are worth keeping apart:

- The Dreamspell count **does not match** the traditional tzolk'in kept by Guatemalan daykeepers. The same day often carries a different day name in each.
- **Hunab Ku** appears in colonial-era Yucatec sources meaning the one god, and scholars suggest missionary influence. Drawing it as a yin-yang-like emblem and calling it the galactic center is a late 20th-century reinterpretation.
- Mayan astrology that reads your personality or mission from your birth day sign is a modern popular system. It can serve as a symbolic tool for reflection, but there is no scientific evidence that it predicts personality or destiny.

## 6. In closing: reading with respect

Maya calendars and myths remind us that time can be seen as cycles and rhythm, not only as a list of tasks to finish. That is a perspective worth learning from. At the same time, the most respectful way to approach Maya culture is to tell tradition apart from modern reinventions, and to remember that it belongs to people living today.

### Frequently asked questions (FAQ)

**Q: Was the 2012 end-of-the-world prophecy real?**
A: No. Around December 2012 the Long Count simply completed its thirteenth b'ak'tun, and the count of days carried on, much like an odometer rolling over. Very few Classic Maya inscriptions mention the date, and none predict destruction. The 2012 apocalypse and the idea of a leap in consciousness are products of late 20th-century reinterpretation and popular culture.

**Q: How do I find my Kin?**
