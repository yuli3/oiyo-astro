### Introduction: Why similar feelings repeat with similar people

Some people feel anxious in love. Others feel crowded as soon as someone comes closer. Others can stay in a conflict and still talk. Personality alone does not explain that difference.

John Bowlby and Mary Ainsworth started the account with **attachment**. An early bond with a primary caregiver can build an **internal working model**, a map of what to expect from closeness. The map is a guide. It does not decide adult relationships. This page is for self-understanding. It does not diagnose or treat a mental health condition.

The four names below follow Bartholomew and Horowitz (1991). Hazan and Shaver (1987) used three groups, so their percentages are not pasted onto these four boxes.

---

### 1. Secure attachment: depending and leaving room

In the Hazan and Shaver (1987) newspaper survey, about 56% chose the secure description, about 25% the avoidant description, and about 19% the anxious description. That is one three-group sample, not a population rate for four adult styles. People in the secure group more often recalled care that was relatively consistent. The description is of someone who can trust others and still feel worth staying close to.

- **What adult accounts often mention:** They lean when they need to, and they leave the other person room. A conflict does not have to mean the end. Fear of rejection can be there without running the whole relationship.

### 2. Anxious-preoccupied: asking for reassurance again

This pattern is often linked to care that came and went: close on some days, unanswered on others. In adult relationships, a small change in the other person can feel like the start of a loss.

- **What adult accounts often mention:** They want love confirmed often. The wish to be very close can look like jealousy or pursuit. The thought that the other person loves them less can fill the relationship. Not every anxious person is explained by this path.

### 3. Dismissive-avoidant: stepping back as closeness grows

When a caregiver often ignored or refused a child's emotional bids, the child may learn to dampen feeling in order to cope. That habit is what this page calls avoidance, a defense, not a character verdict.

- **What adult accounts often mention:** Deeper intimacy can feel like being tied down. They may stop talking in a conflict, or cool the relationship, rather than share what they feel. A strong talk of independence sometimes sits on top of a fear of rejection. That is not true of every person with this label.

### 4. Fearful-avoidant: wanting closeness and fearing it

This is the fourth cell in Bartholomew and Horowitz. This page does not call it the rarest style, and it does not say abuse is required. It is not the same thing as infant disorganized attachment. Stories about a person who was both a comfort and a source of fear are part of the discussion, not a single cause.

- **What adult accounts often mention:** They want to be loved and also fear being hurt. They may move closer and then push away. The relationship itself can become a source of strain, and settling their own feelings can feel hard.

---

### Closing: attachment is a record, not a fate

An anxious or avoidant label does not fix the future of a relationship. The style is an expectation left by earlier relationships, not a fate.

Researchers use **earned security** for people who describe difficult early care and later show a more secure way of relating, often after repeated corrective experience, including therapy. Meeting one calm partner is not a promise that the style will change.

What helps is noticing the pattern. When you can see why you feel anxious, or why you want distance, you do not have to follow the old map by default.
