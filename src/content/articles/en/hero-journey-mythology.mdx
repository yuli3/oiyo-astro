## The hero’s journey is one way to read a story

Joseph Campbell compared recurring patterns in myths in *The Hero with a Thousand Faces*, published in 1949. His proposed monomyth, or hero’s journey, describes leaving a familiar world, encountering unfamiliar experiences and returning with something gained.

Please do not treat “all great stories are the same” as a scientific law. The [Joseph Campbell Foundation](https://www.jcf.org/learn/joseph-campbell-heros-journey) cautions against reading the details as a fixed sequence present in every myth. The framework can help trace change in a work, but it is not the only valid way to evaluate it.

## Campbell’s three movements: departure, initiation and return

These movements can reveal changes in a character’s relationship with the world, not just geographical travel. Ask what is lost or learned as well as where someone goes.

| Movement | Change to examine | Reading question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Departure | A break with the familiar order | What prompts departure, and why is the character hesitant? |
| Initiation | Encounters with new rules, trials and help | Where does the old approach fail, and what is learned? |
| Return | Bringing something gained into everyday or communal life | How do relationships or daily life change afterward? |

A story is not defective because it omits a return or ends in failure. Differences from the framework are also material for interpretation.

## Vogler’s twelve stages are a writing adaptation

Christopher Vogler adapted Campbell’s concepts for writers and screenwriting. The [official publisher](https://mwp.com/product/writers-journey-25th-anniversary-edition-mythic-structure-writers/) describes a twelve-stage, myth-inspired method. Calling it Campbell’s original “twelve stages” conflates the models.

The labels below follow [Vogler’s teaching handout](https://chrisvogler.wordpress.com/2023/11/04/a-handy-handout/). The questions are editorial examples for reviewing a scene, not quotations from the handout.

| Stage | Question for a scene |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Ordinary world | Can we see the character’s life and what is missing? |
| 2. Call to adventure | What event disrupts the current situation? |
| 3. Refusal of the call | Why does the character hesitate to act? |
| 4. Meeting the mentor | What can help provide, and what can it not do instead of the character? |
| 5. Crossing the threshold | What choice leads into an unfamiliar situation? |
| 6. Tests, allies and enemies | How are new rules and relationships learned? |
| 7. Approach | What preparation precedes the central challenge? |
| 8. Ordeal | What problem cannot be solved by the old approach? |
| 9. Reward | What is gained, and at what cost? |
| 10. The road back | What remains to be done to bring the gain home? |
| 11. Resurrection | Does a final test show the change through action? |
| 12. Return with the elixir | What does the outcome mean for the person or community? |

This is not a production rule requiring one scene for each stage. If a question does not fit your story, you need not invent a character or event to satisfy it.

## Try it with a fictional example

This example was written for the article. A repair apprentice in a small village is asked to fix a communal pump. At first, the apprentice expects to solve the problem alone but fails because the equipment is unfamiliar. They ask a colleague for help, organise a temporary water-sharing plan with residents and investigate the fault together.

Departure can be read as accepting the request; initiation as the failure of a solitary approach and learning to cooperate; return as bringing restored equipment and a maintenance practice to the village. The “reward” can be knowledge and changed relationships rather than an object.

You could also write a version without a mentor or final test. Examine whether the character’s goals, choices and consequences are convincing before trying to match a stage count.

## What the framework can miss

Grouping myths under the same labels can obscure ritual, religion and historical context. Reading only through an individual hero may miss important features of stories centred on communities, multiple protagonists, cycles or journeys without a return.

Archetypes and the collective unconscious are terms of theoretical interpretation, not brain structures or personality-test findings established by this article. Usefulness for writing, universality across cultures and guaranteed commercial success are separate claims. We do not supply a percentage asserting that most hits from a particular studio follow this model.

## Leave room for choice when applying it to life

When reflecting on a career change or new learning, you might note what you left, the help you received and what you brought back. This is an optional editorial reflection example, not a developmental sequence or treatment programme.

You do not have to take a frightening risk to grow, and choosing a safe routine does not make you a failure who refused the call. Stopping, returning or asking for help are available choices. You can tell a story about your life without expecting every difficult experience to lead to a reward.

## Sources and what they support

The Foundation source supports the theory and three movements; the publisher and Vogler sources support the twelve-stage writing model. They are not cited as research proving therapeutic effects, universal laws of the psyche or box-office outcomes.
