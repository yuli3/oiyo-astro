## Reference points and four distinct phenomena

Prospect theory is a descriptive model of **gains and losses relative to a reference point** in risky choice. It also concerns probability weighting, so it is not simply another name for “losses hurt more.” See [Kahneman and Tversky’s 1979 paper](https://doi.org/10.2307/1914185).

This article separates loss aversion, the endowment effect, choice overload and peak-end evaluation. They ask **different empirical questions**; they are not four deductions from one universal law.

## Loss aversion: weighting changes below a reference point

Loss aversion is a tendency to weight losses below a reference point more heavily than equivalent gains. First establish how gains and losses were defined. Avoiding a gamble can also reflect risk attitudes or limited resources.

“Losses always count twice” is not a universal constant. A parameter estimated in a particular model cannot be assigned to every person, amount or setting. Nor does a larger stake or a clearer risk guarantee a stronger effect.

Feeling reluctant when a free trial ends might reflect loss aversion, actual usefulness or the hassle of switching. Write down future needs and costs before settling on a single explanation.

The [sunk cost fallacy](/en/sunk-cost-fallacy-investment/) concerns unrecoverable past spending. It can be discussed alongside loss aversion but is not the same phenomenon. The distinction does not prescribe a share trade or a cancellation.

## Endowment effect: ownership and trading prices

The endowment effect concerns differences between the price an owner will accept and the price a non-owner will pay. [Kahneman, Knetsch and Thaler’s 1990 experiments](https://doi.org/10.1086/261737) observed such a gap in trading randomly allocated mugs.

A high valuation of a used possession need not be a bias. Its usefulness, replacement cost or information about it may genuinely differ. To isolate ownership, conditions such as item quality, information and trading rules need to be comparable.

“What would I pay if I did not own it?” is a change of perspective, not a formula for the correct selling price. Record what you need and compare alternatives under similar conditions.

## Choice overload: option counts and decisions

Choice overload is the hypothesis that more options can delay decisions or reduce satisfaction. The well-known jam study by Iyengar and Lepper (2000) is one case, not a result that automatically applies to every menu or career choice.

The [2010 meta-analysis](https://scheibehenne.de/ScheibehenneGreifenederTodd2010.pdf) combined 50 experiments: its average effect was near zero, with substantial variation. That means neither “it never happens” nor “these conditions guarantee it.”

Checking comparison difficulty, clarity of preferences and time pressure can be a useful **decision aid**. Those three checks are not established necessary and sufficient conditions.

Try defining two or three essential criteria before narrowing candidates. Record decision time and satisfaction to see whether fewer options help in your setting. Reducing choice is not automatically the answer.

## Peak-end evaluation: experience versus recollection

Peak-end evaluation is a tendency for intense and final moments to weigh relatively strongly in a retrospective rating. It does not mean every memory is stored in two moments or duration never matters.

[Kahneman and colleagues’ 1993 experiment](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.1993.tb00589.x) offered a repeat choice between 60 seconds in 14°C water and that interval plus 30 seconds becoming less cold. A majority chose the longer experience; that does not generalise automatically to every life experience.

Discomfort during an event and its later rating are different outcomes. A considerate ending may help, but the finding does not justify adding real suffering or caring only about the finish. Trips, lessons and medical procedures have different purposes and risks.

## One question for each effect

| Effect | Question to ask yourself |
|---|---|
| Loss aversion | Setting the feeling of loss aside, which way is better from here? |
| Endowment effect | If I did not have it, would I buy it back at this price? |
| Choice overload | What are the two or three criteria that really matter? |
| Peak-end rule | Beyond the peak and the ending, how was the whole thing? |

This checklist is a thinking aid, not a proven guarantee of fewer errors. Compare actual evidence and alternatives, and record what would change your judgement.
