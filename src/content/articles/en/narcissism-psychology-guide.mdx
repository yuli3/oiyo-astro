The word narcissism comes from the Greek myth of Narcissus, who fell in love with his own reflection in the water and could not leave it until he wasted away. In psychology, narcissism refers to **excessive preoccupation with oneself, limited empathy, and a tendency to use other people**. This guide explains narcissism from a psychological point of view and offers ways to protect yourself in a relationship with someone who shows these traits.

<Callout type="info">
**Clinical note**: Narcissistic traits exist on a spectrum. Not every self-centred behaviour is narcissistic personality disorder (NPD). A formal diagnosis can only be made by a qualified professional.
</Callout>

## The narcissism spectrum

### Healthy narcissism (normal range)

- Loving and respecting yourself
- Taking pride in your achievements
- Recognising your own worth and needs
- This is a healthy and necessary part of self-concept

### Excessive narcissism (worth paying attention to)

- Heavy reliance on other people's approval and admiration
- Extreme sensitivity to criticism, or anger in response to it
- Limited capacity for empathy

### Narcissistic personality disorder (NPD)

- DSM-5: five or more of nine diagnostic criteria
- A diagnosis looks at a lasting pattern that begins by early adulthood and shows up across many situations, not at a passing behaviour

## Two main types of narcissism

### Grandiose narcissism

**Characteristics**:
- A strong conviction of being special and superior
- Overconfidence and a charismatic presence
- Responds to criticism with indifference or aggression
- Enjoys attention and praise

**How it looks from outside**: Confident and charming. Impressive at first, with problems surfacing over time.

### Vulnerable narcissism

**Characteristics**:
- Defences that hide shame and feelings of inferiority
- A strong sense of being wronged
- Very sensitive to criticism and easily hurt
- Comes across as introverted rather than outgoing

**How it looks from outside**: Quiet and sensitive. Often harder to recognise than the grandiose type.

## Relationship patterns with a narcissistic partner

### The love bombing → devaluation → discard cycle

This cycle is not a DSM-5 criterion. It is a descriptive framework often used in counselling and popular books, and not every relationship follows this order.

**Stage 1: Love bombing**
- An overwhelming flood of affection and attention from the very start
- "You're the most special person I've ever met."
- The relationship moves fast, with grand promises about the future

**Stage 2: Devaluation**
- The early sense of perfection turns into criticism and dismissal
- Gaslighting: "That never happened. You're overreacting."
- Self-esteem worn down through comparisons

**Stage 3: Discard**
- Once a new "source" is found, the previous partner is dropped
- Or the relationship is kept going so that control, and the hurt, continue

### Gaslighting

Gaslighting is a manipulation tactic that makes the other person doubt their own sense of reality.

**Signs of gaslighting**:
- "I never said that. You imagined it."
- "You're too sensitive."
- "Why would I ever say something like that?"
- Making you remember events in a completely different way

**Signs that you may be experiencing gaslighting**:
- □ You often doubt your own memory and judgement
- □ It always seems to be your fault
- □ Your confidence is much lower than it used to be
- □ You keep working to fit around your partner's moods

## Protecting yourself in the relationship

### Setting boundaries

In a relationship with a narcissistic person, boundaries are a necessity, not an option.

**How to set clear boundaries**:
1. Work out what is not acceptable to you
2. State the boundary calmly and firmly
3. Follow through consistently when the boundary is crossed

### Not becoming a source of supply

A narcissistic person constantly needs "narcissistic supply": praise, attention and reactions.

- Keep emotional reactions to a minimum (the Grey Rock Method)
- Communicate only what is necessary
- Limit how much personal information you share

### The Grey Rock Method

Where possible, the idea is to become as uninteresting as a grey rock to the narcissistic person.

- Give short answers
- Do not show emotional reactions
- Do not share personal stories, dreams or fears

### Professional support

A long relationship with a narcissistic person can leave significant psychological harm. Support from a therapist experienced in trauma can be an important part of recovery.

## Healing after the relationship

### Recognising it

"This is not my fault." This is the most important first realisation.

### Rebuilding your sense of self

If the relationship has worn down your sense of self:
- Practise trusting your own reality, feelings and judgement
- Practise autonomy with small decisions
- Lean on relationships you can trust

### Relearning boundaries

It helps to learn again what boundaries look like in a healthy relationship.
