## The more famous the study, the more the follow-ups matter

Psychology stories about motivation and happiness are often quoted in self-help content. But many first results were later **weakened or reinterpreted** by larger studies. This article reads four famous topics side by side with their original and follow-up research.

## Flow: when challenge matches skill

In *Flow* (1990), Csikszentmihalyi called the state of being so absorbed that you lose track of time **flow**. He studied the daily lives of many people using the **experience sampling method**, which asks people to record what they are experiencing at random moments during the day.

The conditions that foster flow are fairly clear.

- **Balance of challenge and skill**: too easy is boring; too hard is anxious.
- **Clear goals**: you know what to do next.
- **Immediate feedback**: you can tell right away whether you're doing well.

Interestingly, **during** flow there is no room to feel happy. Satisfaction arrives afterwards, when you look back. So flow is closer to **using your abilities fully** than to "feeling good." Ways to build a focused environment are covered further in the [article on focus, habits and practice](/en/focus-habits-and-deliberate-practice/).

## The marshmallow test: willpower or environment?

Mischel and colleagues (Mischel, Ebbesen & Zeiss, 1972) let children choose between eating one marshmallow now or waiting a little for two. A later follow-up (Shoda, Mischel & Peake, 1990) reported that children who waited longer did better academically in adolescence, and the story that "self-control determines success" spread.

Follow-up research changed the picture.

- Watts, Duncan and Quan (2018) re-examined the question with a larger, more diverse sample. The link between waiting time and later achievement was **about half the size** of the original, and mostly disappeared once family background and early cognitive ability were taken into account.
- Kidd, Palmeri and Aslin (2013) showed that children who had just seen an adult break a promise **waited less**. Not waiting may be a **rational judgement about whether the environment is reliable**, not a lack of self-control.

## Decision fatigue and willpower: a well that runs dry?

Baumeister and colleagues (1998) proposed **ego depletion**: willpower is a resource that gets used up. **Decision fatigue** — the idea that judgement blurs after a day of decisions — grew from it. A study finding that Israeli parole judges granted parole less often just before meal breaks (Danziger, Levav & Avnaim-Pesso, 2011) is often cited as the classic example.

The evidence has since weakened.

- In a large study where 23 labs ran the same ego-depletion procedure, the effect was **close to zero** (Hagger et al., 2016).
- For the parole study, critics pointed to **case ordering**: cases without lawyers may have clustered just before meals (Weinshall-Margel & Shapard, 2011).
- Job, Dweck and Walton (2010) showed that depletion effects were larger in **people who believed** willpower runs out easily.

Everyone knows that tiredness blurs judgement. But the strong claim that "willpower exists only in a fixed amount" is hard to accept on current evidence.

## The hedonic treadmill: does happiness always return to baseline?

Brickman and Campbell (1971) proposed the **hedonic treadmill**: even after good things happen, we soon get used to them and happiness returns to its usual level. A well-known study found lottery winners were not as much happier as expected (Brickman, Coates & Janoff-Bulman, 1978).

Diener, Lucas and Scollon (2006) reviewed later research and **revised** the theory.

- Set points differ between people, and most are not neutral but **mildly happy**.
- Adaptation to events like unemployment or disability is **incomplete**, so happiness can stay lower for a long time (Lucas, 2007).
- Happiness is therefore not fixed; **it can change**. Effects tend to last longer from things that stay fresh, like relationships and experiences, than from possessions we quickly get used to.

## Summary at a glance

| Topic | The first story | After follow-up research |
|---|---|---|
| Flow | Flow makes you happy | The conditions are clear; satisfaction comes afterwards |
| Marshmallow | Children who wait succeed | The link is small; environment matters a lot |
| Decision fatigue | Willpower runs out | Near-zero effect in large replications |
| Hedonic treadmill | Happiness returns to baseline | Set points vary and can change |
