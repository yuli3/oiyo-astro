## Folk beliefs aren't just "believe it or not"

Before asking whether folk beliefs are scientifically true or false, they are a cultural record of **how people have handled anxiety and hope**. Anthropology studies what role these beliefs play within a community.

## Korean shamanism: gut rituals and mudang

**Musok (Korean shamanism)** is an old Korean belief in which a shaman, the **mudang**, mediates between spirits and people. In the ritual called **gut**, the mudang uses song, dance and *gongsu* (the words of the spirits) to resolve a household's illness or misfortune, or the grief of the dead.

- **History**: shamans were looked down on under the Confucian order of the Joseon dynasty and suppressed by modern "anti-superstition" campaigns, yet the practice continued among ordinary people (Kim, 1981).
- **Women's ritual**: from fieldwork in rural Korea in the 1970s, the anthropologist Kendall (1985) analysed shamanism as a **ritual space where women, responsible for household well-being, dealt with family problems**.
- **Cultural heritage**: several gut rituals, such as Jindo *ssitgimgut* and the West Coast *baeyeonsingut* and *daedonggut*, are designated National Intangible Cultural Heritage and continue to be passed on.

## Totems and "spirit animals"

The word "totem" comes from the Ojibwe language of North America. It referred to a system in which a clan had a special relationship with a particular animal or plant and used it as the clan's emblem.

The sociologist Durkheim (1912) saw the totem as **the way a community symbolises itself** and used it to explain the origins of religion. Lévi-Strauss (1962), by contrast, criticised grouping "totemism" as a single form of religion, explaining it instead as **a way of thinking about differences between social groups** through differences between natural species.

Today's self-help idea of **"your spirit animal"** is different from these traditions. It is closer to a modern metaphor comparing an individual's personality to an animal, and it helps to know that it differs from the totem, which was originally a community institution.

## The evil eye: amulets against envy

The **evil eye** is the belief that someone's envious or covetous gaze can cause harm. It appears across a very wide area, including the Mediterranean, the Middle East, South Asia and Latin America. The folklorist Dundes (1981) edited a casebook comparing how the belief appears across cultures.

Typical amulets include the Turkish blue-eye glass **nazar boncuğu** and the palm-shaped **hamsa** of the Middle East and North Africa. Customs such as deliberately finding fault when praising a child, or "knocking on wood," are thought to stem from similar ideas.

The belief captures people's experience that **envy can damage relationships within a community**. Research on envy as an emotion is covered in the [article on dark traits and manipulation](/en/dark-traits-and-manipulation/).
