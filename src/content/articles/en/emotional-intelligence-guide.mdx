## What is emotional intelligence (EQ)?

**Emotional intelligence (EQ)** is a concept proposed in 1990 by psychologists **Peter Salovey** and **John Mayer**, and popularized by **Daniel Goleman** in his 1995 book *Emotional Intelligence*.

EQ is the ability to **notice, understand, and manage your own emotions and other people's, and to use that in pursuing goals and building relationships**.

It is a useful frame for relationships and collaboration. It does not, by itself, explain job performance or leadership. Studies define and measure emotional intelligence differently, as an ability, a trait, or a mixed model. A single score, or a dramatic performance percentage, is the wrong way to read it.

---

## 1. What to watch when measuring EQ

- **There is more than one model.** A test of skill with emotion and a self-report of personality are not measuring the same thing.
- **Self-report has limits.** Mood at the moment, how well you know yourself, and the wish to answer in a socially desirable way can all move the score.
- **Context belongs in the picture.** Repeated behavior in conflict, feedback, and stress is more useful than one score.

## 2. Goleman's five components

### 1. Self-awareness

The ability to notice your emotions, strengths, weaknesses, values, and motives.

**Signs often tied to stronger self-awareness**:
- You can say, with some precision, why you are angry
- You can take feedback without getting defensive
- You notice your own biases
- Under stress, you notice what your body is doing (heart rate, tension)

---

### 2. Self-regulation

The ability to hold an emotional impulse and turn it toward something more useful.

**Signs often tied to stronger self-regulation**:
- When you are angry, you think before you answer
- You adapt when things change
- People can count on you to act in a steady way
- You admit mistakes and take responsibility

<Callout type="tip" title="A practice for self-regulation: pause before you react">
When the feeling is strong, pause before you speak or decide, and check the tension in your body and the thought that just appeared. How long that takes depends on the person and the situation. A fixed number of seconds does not reset the brain.
</Callout>

---

### 3. Motivation

The ability to draw motivation from inner satisfaction and growth, rather than only from external rewards such as money or status.

**Signs often tied to stronger motivation**:
- Getting back up after a failure
- Putting off a short-term pleasure for a goal
- A steady wish to keep learning
- A strong wish to achieve

---

### 4. Empathy

The ability to notice and understand another person's emotions. This is not the same as feeling what they feel. It is **taking their point of view**.

**Signs often tied to stronger empathy**:
- You read nonverbal cues (face, posture, tone)
- You get along with people from different cultures and backgrounds
- You notice a teammate's emotional state with some care
- You pick up what a client actually needs

---

### 5. Social skills

The ability to build and look after relationships, and to influence other people.

**Signs often tied to stronger social skills**:
- In a conflict, you naturally take a mediating role
- You can point a team in one direction
- You communicate in a way people can follow
- You build a network without forcing it

## 4. Using EQ at work

Skills related to emotion are not reserved for one kind of job. Roles with heavy emotional labor, such as working with customers, may ask for them more often. How much they matter still depends on the job, the team, and the organization.

### In conflict: responses often called low EQ and high EQ

| Situation | Response often called low EQ | Response often called high EQ |
|-----|---------|---------|
| A teammate makes a mistake | Immediate blame, and the teammate gets defensive | Look at what happened, then ask what went on |
| A clash of views | Hold your own position and react from the feeling | Acknowledge the other view first, then offer an alternative |
| A teammate who is burned out | Decide they are not trying | Look for the source of the stress and offer support |
| Critical feedback | Defend yourself and explain it away | Listen, then say that it helped |

## 5. Ways to practice

### Self-awareness
- **An emotion note**: at the end of the day, write down three emotions you felt
- **Five minutes of meditation**: practice watching your thoughts (mindfulness apps such as Calm or Headspace)
- **Ask for feedback**: ask people you trust for specific feedback on how you usually react

### Self-regulation
- **STOP**: Stop, take a breath, observe, proceed
- **Name your triggers**: list the situations and people that make you react at once
- **Regular movement**: a routine that makes a pause easier, and leaves a little room before you react

### Empathy
- **Active listening**: put the phone down while someone speaks, meet their eyes, nod
- **Imagine another view**: think of three reasons they might be acting that way
- **Hold the verdict**: before you decide someone is wrong, step back and watch

<Callout type="info" title="Burnout and EQ">
Burnout comes from several personal and organizational factors together: workload, control, reward, relationships, and a clash of values. Noticing your emotions and your limits, and asking for help, can support how you respond. Do not blame a harmful workplace, or an excessive workload, on someone's low EQ.
</Callout>

## 7. Can EQ be developed?

Specific skills can be practiced and can take feedback: naming an emotion, slowing a reaction, checking the other person's point of view. A higher score on a particular test does not prove that every relationship skill, or every job outcome, improved with it.

The aim is not a vague project of raising EQ. It is to choose a behavior you want to change in a real situation, and to watch it. If the difficulty keeps returning, or the distress is large, consider counseling or another suitable kind of help rather than self-help practice alone.
