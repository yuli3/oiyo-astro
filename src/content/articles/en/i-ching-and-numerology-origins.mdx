## The I Ching: from divination manual to philosophical classic

The core of the **I Ching (Zhouyi)** is the **64 hexagrams**, each made of six stacked yin (⚋) or yang (⚊) lines, plus short texts attached to each hexagram and line. This core is thought to have been compiled from divination records of the late Western Zhou period, around the 9th century BCE (Shaughnessy, 1996).

Later came the **Ten Wings**, commentaries interpreting the hexagrams philosophically. Tradition attributes them to Confucius, but scholars see them as the work of several authors from the Warring States period into the Han. When Confucianism became the state orthodoxy under Emperor Wu of Han, the *Classic of Changes* became one of the Five Classics and went on to shape East Asian philosophy, politics, medicine and art for more than 2,000 years (Smith, 2008).

Divination methods include an old one using 50 yarrow stalks and a simpler one tossing three coins. The I Ching became widely known in the West through Richard Wilhelm's German translation (1924); Carl Jung wrote the foreword to its English edition (1950). And in his 1703 paper on binary arithmetic, Leibniz noted that the arrangement of the hexagrams resembles binary numbers.

Reading the I Ching as **a classic for reflecting on change** is well worthwhile. There is, however, no scientific evidence that hexagrams predict the future.

## Numerology: from Pythagoras to the "life path number"

The idea that "all things are number" goes back to the **Pythagoreans** of ancient Greece. Aristotle reports in the *Metaphysics* that they took the principles of number to be the principles of all things. For them, number was an object of philosophical and mathematical inquiry into the order of the cosmos.

The now-familiar method of **adding up your birth date to a single digit, the "life path number," and reading personality from it** is far more recent. It belongs to modern Western numerology, systematised in the early 20th century by L. Dow Balliett (1908) and others in the United States; no such calculation appears in Pythagorean texts. Converting the letters of your name into numbers is a product of the same period. OIYO's [numerology calculator](/en/numerology/calculator/) is a for-fun tool that calculates in this modern way.

## Why do readings feel so accurate?

The psychologist Forer (1949) gave students a personality test, then handed **everyone the same reading** and asked them to rate how accurate it was. The average rating was about 4.3 out of 5, because sentences like "You have a great need for other people to like you, yet you tend to be critical of yourself" **fit almost anyone**. This is known as the **Barnum (or Forer) effect**.

When an I Ching judgement or a numerology reading feels "exactly like me," it helps to ask whether the same sentence would fit other people too.
