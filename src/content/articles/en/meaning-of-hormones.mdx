## 1. Dopamine: More About Wanting Than Pleasure

- **The popular label:** "The pleasure chemical" or "the molecule of more".
- **What research says:** Dopamine is linked more to motivation, "wanting" and learning which actions lead to rewards than to the feeling of pleasure itself (Berridge & Robinson, 1998; Schultz, Dayan & Montague, 1997). It is also essential for movement.
- **The caveat:** Phones or sugar do not "burn out" your dopamine, and dopamine is not addictive in itself. Habits that are hard to stop are better understood as learned behaviour patterns.
- **Everyday idea:** Breaking a big goal into small steps and noticing your progress is a sensible way to stay motivated. It is a psychological tip, not a way to "hack" a chemical.

## 2. Serotonin: Not Simply the Happiness Chemical

- **The popular label:** "The natural antidepressant" or "the happiness chemical".
- **What research says:** Serotonin is involved in many functions, including mood, sleep, appetite and digestion. Most of the body's serotonin is made in the gut, and it does not cross into the brain.
- **The caveat:** The idea that depression is simply caused by low serotonin is not supported by the current evidence (Moncrieff et al., 2023). Depression has many contributing factors. This does not mean antidepressants are useless; many people benefit from them, and any change should be discussed with a doctor.
- **Everyday idea:** One study found higher brain serotonin turnover on brighter days (Lambert et al., 2002). Daylight and walking are generally good for wellbeing, but they are not a treatment for depression.

## 3. Oxytocin: More Complicated Than the Love Hormone

- **The popular label:** "The love hormone" or "the cuddle hormone".
- **What research says:** Oxytocin is made in the hypothalamus and released by the pituitary gland. It plays clear roles in childbirth and breastfeeding, and animal studies link it to social bonding.
- **The caveat:** Its effects depend on context and on the person. In one set of experiments it increased favouritism toward one's own group (De Dreu et al., 2011). Human studies using oxytocin nasal sprays have produced mixed results with methodological weaknesses (Walum, Waldman & Young, 2016). It is not true that oxytocin switches off fear or cancels cortisol.
- **Everyday idea:** Social support and welcome physical touch, such as hugs, were associated with better resilience to stress in one study (Cohen et al., 2015). There is no magic number of seconds, and a hug only helps when both people want it.

## 4. Cortisol: Not Simply the Enemy

- **The popular label:** "The stress hormone".
- **What research says:** Cortisol is made by the adrenal glands and follows a daily rhythm, usually highest in the morning. It helps regulate energy, blood sugar and the immune system, so we need it.
- **The caveat:** Long-lasting stress can take a toll on the body (McEwen, 1998), but the claim that cortisol "kills you slowly" or directly makes you gain weight is an oversimplification. Popular "cortisol hacks" and supplements that promise to lower cortisol are not backed by good evidence. Real cortisol disorders are diagnosed by a doctor with tests.
- **Everyday idea:** Slow, deep breathing is associated with feeling calmer and with physiological signs of relaxation in studies, though the evidence is still limited (Zaccaro et al., 2018).

## 5. In Summary: Chemistry Is Part of the Story

Your mood is not "just chemistry", and you cannot mix your hormones like a cocktail. Biology, thoughts, relationships and environment all interact.
Habits such as daylight, movement, sleep, time with people you trust and small achievable goals are generally good for wellbeing, even if they do not target one specific molecule. If you have been struggling for a while, reaching out to a professional is a sign of strength, not failure.

### FAQ

**Q: Does a "dopamine detox" work?**
A: Taking a break from screens or other habits can help you rest and notice what pulls your attention. However, there is no evidence that it "resets" your dopamine or your receptors, and you cannot fast from a chemical your brain makes all the time.

**Q: Does chocolate cure sadness?**
A: Enjoying chocolate is fine, but it is not a mood treatment. A meta-analysis found that sugar does not improve mood and is linked to more tiredness and lower alertness shortly afterwards (Mantantzis et al., 2019). If sadness lasts, talking to someone you trust or a professional helps more.
