## 1. Sinnaerim: Called Rather Than Choosing

Korean shamans are usually described in two groups: **gangsinmu**, who become shamans through *sinnaerim* (spirit descent), and **sesumu**, who inherit the role through their family line. In the Seoul and Gyeonggi region, a shaman is also respectfully called a **mansin**.

The path of a gangsinmu often begins with a story of **sinbyeong** (spirit illness). Within the tradition, unexplained suffering, dreams or visions are interpreted as a spirit's call, and the person becomes a shaman after a **naerim-gut** (initiation ritual) performed by a senior shaman, often called their spirit mother. Because someone who has been through pain then tends to the pain of others, the mudang is sometimes compared to the "wounded healer."

> This is an interpretation within the belief system. If you have persistent physical or mental symptoms, please talk to a medical professional first.

## 2. Gut: A Ritual for Releasing Han

A **gut** is a full ritual performance that brings together song, dance, music, offerings and colorful costumes. In some gut, the shaman stands on sharp fodder-cutter blades (*jakdu-tagi*); believers take this as a sign that the spirit has descended on the shaman.

At the heart of the gut is **releasing han**. In rituals for the dead, the shaman delivers *gongsu*, words spoken on behalf of the spirits or the deceased, and the family pours out the words and tears they never had the chance to share. Anthropologists have interpreted these moments as a communal space where the living can express grief and guilt together and come to terms with the relationship.

> There is no scientific evidence that gut rituals or fortune-telling cure illness or predict the future. This article introduces musok from the perspective of culture and belief.

## 3. Household Gods: A Home Shared With Spirits

For Koreans of the past, the home was a place where many spirits dwelt.

- **Seongjusin**: the god believed to protect the whole house and the head of the household.
- **Jowangsin**: the god of the kitchen and the hearth fire.
- **Teojusin**: the god who guards the house site.
- **Cheuksin**: the goddess said to live in the outhouse, portrayed in folktales as famously short-tempered.

Living carefully, mindful of what dwells in each space, was a way of bringing reverence and courtesy into every corner of daily life.

## 4. Musok Today

Under the Confucian order of the Joseon dynasty, shamans were looked down on, and in the modern era they were suppressed by "anti-superstition" campaigns. Even so, the practice continued among ordinary people, and some gut, such as the Jindo Ssitgimgut, are now designated as National Intangible Heritage and passed down to new generations.

Today, many shamans run shrines in the city and share their work online. People visit them for many reasons, but one may be that musok answers the question "Why did this happen to me?" with a story and a ritual.

## 5. In Summary: The Ones Who Do Not Leave

In the worldview of musok, the dead never fully leave. They remain as beings who are remembered, called upon and comforted. Whether or not you believe in spirits, musok can be seen as a tradition that expresses, through ritual, the hope that our bond with those we love does not simply end at death.

### FAQ

**Q: Is musok superstition or a scam?**
A: Musok is a faith that many people still practice and a recognized part of Korea's cultural heritage. The existence of spirits has not been proven by science, and there is no evidence that a gut cures illness. Anthropological research, however, describes the gut as a ritual space for sharing grief and working through family relationships. If you are urged to pay for an expensive ritual, please take time to think it over carefully.

**Q: Why are so many shamans women?**
A: In an era when the Confucian order shut women out of public ritual, shamanism was one of the rare areas where women could hold religious authority. Kendall (1985) analysed musok as a ritual space where women, responsible for household well-being, dealt with family problems. There are also male shamans, called *baksu*.
