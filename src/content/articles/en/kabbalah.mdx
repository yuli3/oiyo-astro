## 1. Origins: Legend and History

Tradition tells several stories about where Kabbalah came from. One legend says the angel Raziel gave Adam a book of secrets after he left Eden; Kabbalists also speak of an ancient teaching passed on by word of mouth from master to student. These accounts express how the tradition understands itself.

Historians describe a gradual development:

- **Early Jewish mysticism (late antiquity):** visionary “Merkavah” and “Hekhalot” texts about ascent to the divine throne.
- **Sefer Yetzirah (Book of Formation):** a short, hard-to-date text (often placed between roughly the 3rd and 6th centuries CE) that describes creation through ten *sefirot* (originally “numbers”) and the 22 letters of the Hebrew alphabet.
- **Medieval Kabbalah (12th–13th centuries):** the movement properly called Kabbalah emerged in Provence and Spain. The **Zohar** (“Book of Splendor”) appeared in Castile in the late 13th century. Tradition attributes it to the 2nd-century sage Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai; most scholars attribute it to Moses de León and his circle.
- **Safed, 16th century:** Moses Cordovero and especially Isaac Luria shaped **Lurianic Kabbalah**, which deeply influenced later Judaism, including Hasidism.

From the Renaissance onward, Christian scholars such as Pico della Mirandola and Johannes Reuchlin adapted these ideas into a **Christian Cabala**. In the 19th century, occultists such as Éliphas Lévi and the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn built a **Hermetic Qabalah** linked to tarot, astrology and ritual magic. In the 20th century, popular organizations such as the Kabbalah Centre offered universalized versions, which drew media attention when celebrities such as Madonna took an interest. Many rabbis and scholars regard these later forms as distinct from traditional Jewish Kabbalah.

## 2. The Tree of Life: The Ten Sefirot

A central image is the **Tree of Life** (*Ilan*), a diagram of ten **sefirot** (singular *sefirah*). In Kabbalistic teaching, Ein Sof, the infinite God, lies beyond all description; the sefirot are the attributes or emanations through which the divine is understood to relate to the world. They are traced from Keter at the top down to Malkhut, and contemplation can also move back up the tree.

### ① The Upper Triad (Intellect)

- **1. Keter (Crown):** The highest sefirah, the primordial divine will, beyond human understanding.
- **2. Chokhmah (Wisdom):** The first flash of insight, the seed of an idea.
- **3. Binah (Understanding):** The power that develops that seed into structure, often described with maternal imagery.

### ② The Middle Triad (Emotion and Ethics)

- **4. Chesed (Lovingkindness):** Generous, overflowing love.
- **5. Gevurah (Strength), also Din (Judgment):** Limits, discipline and restraint.
- **6. Tiferet (Beauty or Harmony):** The balance of Chesed and Gevurah, often linked with compassion.

### ③ The Lower Sefirot (Action and Manifestation)

- **7. Netzach (Eternity or Victory):** Endurance and initiative.
- **8. Hod (Splendor):** Acknowledgment, form and humility.
- **9. Yesod (Foundation):** The channel that gathers the flow above and passes it on, associated with covenant and procreation.
- **10. Malkhut (Kingship or Kingdom):** The receiving sefirah, linked with the *Shekhinah* (the divine presence in the world).

The short descriptions above are simplified. Psychological readings, such as treating Yesod as “the subconscious”, come mainly from modern and Hermetic interpretations rather than from classical Jewish sources.

## 3. A Core Idea: Vessels, Light and Repair

Lurianic Kabbalah tells a dramatic story of creation. Ein Sof first “contracts” to make room for the world (*tzimtzum*). Vessels (*kelim*) meant to hold the divine light could not contain it and shattered (*shevirat ha-kelim*), scattering sparks of holiness. Human beings take part in **Tikkun (repair)**: in traditional Judaism, through prayer, fulfilling the commandments and ethical action.

In the 20th century, Rabbi Yehuda Ashlag framed this as turning the “desire to receive for oneself” into a “desire to receive in order to give”. This reading is central to several modern popular teachings.

## 4. The Red String

The red string worn on the wrist is a Jewish folk custom, often linked to the Tomb of Rachel near Bethlehem, where strings are wound around the tomb. Classical Kabbalistic texts do not prescribe it. It became widely known through the Kabbalah Centre, which teaches wearing it on the left wrist as the “receiving” side, as protection from the “evil eye” (envy). Some rabbis have criticized the practice as superstition. It is best understood as a sign of belief and intention; there is no evidence that it has any protective effect.

## 5. Summary: The Human Being as a Microcosm

Kabbalah describes the human being as a small image of the cosmos. Traditional texts map the sefirot symbolically onto the body: Keter above the head, Chesed and Gevurah as the right and left arms, Tiferet as the torso, and Malkhut at the base. For many readers, this image is an invitation to look for the balance of love and limits, thought and action, within their own lives.

<Callout type="info">
This article introduces Kabbalah as a religious and cultural tradition. Its descriptions of divine light and the sefirot express belief and symbolism, not verified facts. There is no scientific evidence that Kabbalistic practices, the red string or Tree of Life correspondences can protect a person, predict events or determine personality.
</Callout>

### FAQ

**Q: Can I study it if I am not Jewish?**
A: Introductory books and academic courses are open to anyone. Within Judaism, deep study was traditionally reserved for mature students already grounded in the Torah and Talmud, and Jewish communities still differ on who should study it and how. Some organizations teach universalized versions, which many rabbis and scholars see as different from traditional Kabbalah. Whichever path you choose, approaching it with respect for its religious context is recommended.

**Q: Is this the same as the Tree of Life in tarot?**
A: Not exactly. Tarot began as a card game in 15th-century Italy. Its link to Kabbalah was made by 18th- and 19th-century occultists, and the Golden Dawn systematized it: the 22 Major Arcana were assigned to the 22 Hebrew letters and paths of the tree, and the minor cards numbered 1 to 10 to the ten sefirot. These correspondences belong to Hermetic Qabalah, not to Jewish Kabbalah. There is no scientific evidence that tarot or these correspondences can predict the future.
