"What will people think of me?" — if that thought runs your day, you may be dealing with social anxiety. A U.S. survey estimated that about 12% of adults have social anxiety disorder at some point in their lives (Kessler et al., 2005). It's not simple shyness — it's a genuine psychological condition.

## What is social anxiety?

Social anxiety is a state of fearing or avoiding social situations out of a fear of **being negatively judged** by others.

**How it differs from shyness**:
- Shyness: the natural discomfort of a new or unfamiliar situation
- Social anxiety: fear and avoidance intense enough to interfere with everyday functioning

<Callout type="info">
**Self-check**: If thinking about social situations (presentations, gatherings, meeting new people) triggers intense anxiety, or if that anxiety leads you to avoid important activities, it's worth talking to a professional.
</Callout>

## The psychological structure of social anxiety

### The cognitive model

The sketch below summarizes the cognitive model of social phobia in Clark and Wells (1995).

The core of social anxiety: the gap between **the social standard you believe you're being judged against** and **your perception of your own ability to meet it.**

```
Perceiving a threatening situation
      ↓
Self-focused attention ("How do I look right now?")
      ↓
Negative prediction ("I'll mess this up," "I'll look foolish")
      ↓
Physical symptoms (racing heart, blushing, shaky voice)
      ↓
Safety behaviors or avoidance → short-term relief, long-term reinforcement
```

### What keeps it going

Why social anxiety persists:

1. **Avoidance**: avoiding the feared situation brings instant relief → which reinforces the avoidance
2. **Safety behaviors**: managing anxiety in real social situations with tactics like "avoiding eye contact" → the underlying fear never actually gets disconfirmed
3. **Post-event processing**: replaying your own behavior after a social event and judging it more harshly than it deserves

## Psychological approaches discussed in the research

This page is not a treatment order. It does not choose a drug, and it does not rank treatments.

### Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT)

CBT, including the Clark and Wells 1995 model, has strong evidence as a first-line psychological approach. That is not a claim that it is the most effective treatment.

**Three core components**:

**1. Cognitive restructuring**:
Identifying and challenging automatic negative thoughts.

| Automatic thought | Checking the evidence | A more balanced thought |
|-----------|---------|------------|
| "Everyone thinks I'm weird" | Is there actual evidence for that? | "Some people might notice, but most are focused on themselves" |
| "If I make a mistake, it's over" | How did it go the last time I made a mistake? | "Mistakes happen, and they're usually not a big deal" |

**2. Exposure**:
Practice staying in the feared situation so the feared prediction can be updated. It does not guarantee that anxiety is extinguished.

| Step | Situation | Anxiety level |
|------|------|---------|
| 1 | Thanking a cashier at a store | Low |
| 2 | Asking a stranger for directions | Medium |
| 3 | Attending a small gathering | High |
| 4 | Giving a small presentation | Very high |

**3. Social skills training**:
Practicing eye contact, starting conversations, and appropriate self-disclosure.

### Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT)

Rather than trying to eliminate anxiety, ACT focuses on **accepting the anxiety while still acting on your values.**

Core idea: "I can feel anxious and still go to the gathering I want to attend."

### Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT)

Practicing reducing self-focused attention and staying present, specifically in socially anxious moments.

## Exercises people often try

The exercises below are common. They are not proven cures, and this page does not call them a self-cure. No paper is cited for them here.

### In the moment

**The 5-4-3-2-1 grounding exercise**:
- 5 things you can see
- 4 things you can touch
- 3 things you can hear
- 2 things you can smell
- 1 thing you can taste

**The physiological sigh**:
- Short inhale through the nose → a second short inhale → a long exhale through the mouth
- A common breathing exercise. Not a proven cure.

### Cognitive strategies

**The spotlight effect**:
- Gilovich, Medvec, and Savitsky (2000) reported that people overestimate how much others notice them.
- A common reading of that finding is that other people are more occupied with themselves than with you.

**Realistically assessing the worst case**:
- "What's the actual probability the worst case happens?"
- "If it does happen, how long will it actually affect me?"
- "What happened the last time I was in a similar situation?"

### A gradual exposure plan

Design one small social challenge each week:

**Week 1**: Greet the barista first when ordering
**Week 2**: Ask a stranger something small
**Week 3**: Reach out to a friend first and suggest getting together
**Week 4**: Speak up once in a small group conversation

<Callout type="tip">
**When daily life is disrupted**: See a clinician. Clinicians sometimes use medication. This page does not recommend a drug. CBT is offered in person and online in many places. That is not an order from this page.
</Callout>
