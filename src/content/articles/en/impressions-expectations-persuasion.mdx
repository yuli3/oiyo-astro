## We read people all at once

When we meet someone, we do not assess each of their traits separately. One first impression colours other judgements, and our expectations can even change how the other person behaves. The way we ask and refuse moves people too. This article covers five effects at work when we **judge** people and when we **persuade** them.

## When judging: impressions and expectations

### The halo effect: one good trait lights up the rest

In 1920 Edward Thorndike found that when army officers rated soldiers, scores on quite different traits — physique, intelligence, leadership, character — **moved together far too closely**. If one trait looked good, the others were rated well too.

Nisbett and Wilson (1977) showed students one of two videos of the same lecturer, speaking warmly or coldly. Those who saw the warm version rated his appearance, accent and mannerisms as more appealing — and **denied that liking him had affected those ratings**.

**Check question**: "Taken on its own, how good is this particular trait?" In hiring and evaluation, scoring each criterion separately — ideally by different people — helps.

### The Pygmalion effect: expectations change people

Rosenthal and Jacobson (1968) told elementary-school teachers that certain randomly chosen pupils were about to "bloom" academically. Eight months later, especially in the youngest grades, those pupils showed larger gains in IQ scores. The interpretation: teachers' expectations turned into attention, praise and opportunity, which produced real change.

The effect is smaller than its reputation, though. Reviewing decades of research, Jussim and Harber (2005) concluded that teacher-expectation effects **are real but usually small**, larger for young children and for groups prone to stigma, and do not accumulate much over the years. It is less "believe and anything happens" than **"expectations are passed on, a little, through behaviour."**

**Check question**: "What do I expect of this person, and how does it show in how I treat them?"

## When persuading: asking and resisting

### The Ben Franklin effect: people like us more after doing us a favour

In his autobiography, Benjamin Franklin wrote that a hostile fellow legislator warmed to him after Franklin asked to borrow a rare book. Jecker and Landy (1969) tested this: when an experimenter asked participants who had won money to return it as a personal favour, they **came to like him more** than those who were not asked. One explanation is that we bring our feelings into line with our actions — "I helped him, so he must be all right."

**Check question**: "Could I ask someone I want to reconnect with for a small, specific favour?" Big, burdensome requests can backfire.

### Reactance: forbid it and people want it more

Jack Brehm (1966) described **psychological reactance**: when people feel a freedom is threatened, they become motivated to restore it. "Never do that" can make the forbidden act more attractive, and heavy-handed, commanding health campaigns can backfire for the same reason.

**Check question**: "Have I left the other person a choice?" When persuading, offering reasons and options rather than "you must" reduces reactance.

### The door-in-the-face: a small request after a big one

Cialdini and colleagues (1975) first asked students to volunteer as counsellors for juvenile offenders, two hours a week for two years. Everyone refused. When they then asked for help chaperoning a one-day zoo trip, **about 50% agreed**, compared with about 17% when asked about the zoo trip alone. The pressure of **reciprocal concessions** — they gave ground, so I should too — was at work.

**Check question** (when you are the one being asked): "Would I agree to this if the first request had never been made?" Knowing the technique makes you less easy to steer in sales and negotiation.

## Checklist at a glance

| Effect | Check question |
|---|---|
| Halo effect | Taken on its own, how good is this trait? |
| Pygmalion effect | How does my expectation show in my behaviour? |
| Ben Franklin effect | Could I ask for a small, specific favour? |
| Reactance | Have I left the other person a choice? |
| Door-in-the-face | Would I agree without the first request? |

These effects are less manipulation tricks than things that happen between people all the time. Knowing them helps you be swept along less by first impressions and pause once more before saying yes.
