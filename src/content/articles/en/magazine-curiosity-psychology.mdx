## Seeking something new is not the same as welcoming it

Looking for books on an unfamiliar subject and enjoying an unexpected experience may seem similar, but they are not identical. You might enjoy researching a topic while disliking a sudden change of plans. Distinguishing these responses can make reflection more useful than simply calling yourself a curious or incurious person.

The CEI-II introduced here is a research instrument that uses self-reported responses to examine two aspects of curiosity. It does not determine intelligence, job performance, or future success, or classify people as good or bad. The everyday examples and suggestions below are editorial explanations, not a training program with established effects.

## Read the two dimensions carefully

[Kashdan and colleagues’ original 2009 paper](https://mason.gmu.edu/~tkashdan/publications/curiosity_CEI_II_jrp.pdf) distinguishes **Stretching** from **Embracing**. Stretching concerns motivation to actively seek new information and experiences. Embracing concerns a willingness to welcome novel, uncertain, and unpredictable experiences. It is not simply enduring anxiety or persevering until a task is finished.

A single example does not determine a score. Disliking unfamiliar experiences does not establish that someone cannot tolerate anxiety or develop further. The dimensions overlap, and the original paper calls for further examination of what Embracing explains beyond Stretching.

## Separate the findings from conclusions they cannot support

The original paper reports three studies with US college-student samples of 311, 150, and 119 participants. The final CEI-II has **10 items**, uses a five-point response format, and assigns five items to each subscale. Internal consistency observed in those samples does not guarantee the same measurement quality across other languages, ages, or cultures. Internal consistency describes how item responses relate to one another; it is not the probability that a judgment about a person is correct.

In the second study, CEI-II scores correlated with happiness at **r=.32** and personal growth at **r=.49**. These are relationships between variables observed in that study. Participants were not assigned a curiosity-building intervention to test whether it increased happiness. The results therefore do not establish that asking more questions causes greater happiness. [See the methods, tables, and limitations](https://mason.gmu.edu/~tkashdan/publications/curiosity_CEI_II_jrp.pdf).

The college-student samples, cross-sectional data, and limited behavioral validation also matter. This article provides no basis for interpreting a small score difference as a personality change or estimating your percentile in the general population.

## Two dimensions are not the whole map of curiosity

CEI-II offers a compact perspective, not the only classification of every form of curiosity. The [abstract of a 2018 multidimensional study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0092656617301149) distinguishes joyous exploration, deprivation sensitivity, stress tolerance, social curiosity, and thrill seeking. The [abstract of a 2020 revision](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S019188692030026X) further distinguishes forms of social curiosity.

Only the abstracts of these later studies informed this discussion. We have not reviewed their complete item sets, validation evidence, or conditions of use here, nor implemented those scales in the OIYO activity. CEI-II Embracing should not simply be renamed as the later framework’s stress tolerance.

## OIYO’s 14-question activity is not the original scale

The [OIYO curiosity test](/en/curiosity-test/) is an **original 14-question self-reflection activity** inspired by the ideas of Stretching and Embracing. It does not administer the original 10-item CEI-II. Its item content as well as its item count differs, so evidence for the original scale cannot establish the validity of this activity.

The current activity averages responses to seven questions per dimension and displays descriptions using its own score boundaries. Those boundaries are neither clinical thresholds nor population norms. Treat the result labels as prompts for reflection, not comparisons of ability, hiring criteria, or diagnoses. Having a translation available does not mean the activity has been validated in that language.

## Observe one question in everyday life

These are optional editorial suggestions, not prescriptions with guaranteed effects. In a low-pressure situation, you could write down something you would like to understand, consult one short source, and reflect on whether it answered your question. You can consider separately whether you wanted more information and whether the unfamiliar experience itself interested you.

You do not need to seek uncomfortable or unsafe experiences. Resting or choosing a familiar approach is also fine. Curiosity need not become a self-improvement obligation; noticing what interested you in a particular situation can be enough.
