## Where does the word "feng shui" come from?

The term "feng shui" (風水, wind and water) is traced to a line in the *Zangshu* (Book of Burial), traditionally attributed to Guo Pu in the 4th century: **"Qi scatters when it rides the wind and stops when it meets water."** A good site, in other words, is one that **shelters from the wind and gathers water** (藏風得水). It began as a technique for choosing grave sites and expanded to siting houses, villages and capitals.

Feng shui has two main streams.

- **Form School**: reads the shapes of mountains and water and the flow of the land by eye. Korea's **baesan-imsu** — a site with mountains behind and water in front — is a classic example.
- **Compass School**: measures directions with the **luopan** compass and calculates auspiciousness using the trigrams, yin-yang and the Five Phases.

## When did the Bagua map appear?

The **Bagua map** often seen in interior-design magazines — a 3×3 grid laid over a floor plan so that "the far left is wealth, the far centre is fame" — is relatively recent. It comes from **Black Sect (BTB) feng shui**, popularised in the United States in the 1980s by Thomas Lin Yun. It differs from the traditional Compass School in that the grid is **aligned to the front door rather than to compass directions**. It helps to know it is a modern variant distinct from traditional feng shui.

## Entrances, bedroom mirrors and desk placement: what research says

Science does not directly prove common feng shui advice. But some advice turns out to be **supported by environmental psychology for different reasons**.

- **Keep the entrance tidy**: there is no evidence that it "lets good fortune in." However, in a study by Saxbe and Repetti (2010), people who described their homes as "cluttered" showed a pattern of poorer daily recovery in the stress hormone cortisol. A tidy entrance can change **how you feel when you come home**.
- **The "commanding position" for desk and bed**: the advice is to place them where you can see the door without being directly in line with it. Appleton's (1975) **prospect-refuge theory** explains that people feel comfortable in spots where they **can see out well while their back is protected** — a conclusion similar to feng shui's.
- **Bedroom mirrors**: there is no evidence that "mirrors bounce qi and disturb sleep." But if movement or light reflected in a mirror bothers you at night, covering it is an option. It is well established that a dark, quiet, cool room helps sleep.
- **Window views and nature**: Ulrich (1984) reported that surgical patients whose rooms looked out on trees had **shorter hospital stays and used fewer painkillers** than those facing a brick wall. This echoes feng shui's advice to keep nature close.

## Summary at a glance

| Advice | Feng shui's explanation | What research says |
|---|---|---|
| Mountains behind, water in front | A good site that blocks wind and gathers water | Practical benefits for sunlight, drainage and wind shelter |
| Bagua map | Areas of the home linked to areas of life | A 1980s Western variant; no evidence |
| Tidy entrance | The doorway for good fortune | Research links clutter and stress |
| Commanding position | A spot that commands the energy | Similar to prospect-refuge theory |
| Bedroom mirrors | Bounce qi and disturb sleep | No evidence; cover if light or movement bothers you |
| Closeness to nature | Brings in vital energy | Research shows benefits for recovery and stress |

Rather than believing feng shui as **laws that change your destiny**, it is more useful to read it as **an old living culture** that prompts you to think about a comfortable space to live in.
