<HighlightBox type="abstract" title="Abstract">
  In Celtic myth, trees, springs and hills are often treated as sacred places. This article first explains where our knowledge of Celtic mythology actually comes from, then separates the history of Ogham, the so-called «tree alphabet», from the «Celtic tree calendar», which only appeared in the 20th century. Finally, it looks at this worldview of people and nature answering each other, and at how it echoes the Eastern idea of «following the way of nature».
</HighlightBox>

## 0. First things first: how do we know any of this?

The ancient Celts left almost no writing about their own myths. The Celtic mythology we read today comes mainly from medieval Irish and Welsh texts, such as the *Lebor Gabála Érenn* (the «Book of Invasions», compiled around the 11th century), which tells of the peoples who settled Ireland, and the Welsh tales known as the *Mabinogion* (surviving manuscripts date from around the 14th century). These texts were recorded and shaped by Christian monks and scribes centuries after Christianity arrived, so they inevitably carry later adaptation and reinterpretation.

Most of what we read about the druids, meanwhile, comes from Greek and Roman authors such as Julius Caesar (*The Gallic War*) and Pliny the Elder (*Natural History*). These are outsiders' views, sometimes written with political aims. It helps to keep this in mind: much of «Celtic mythology» is later record and imagination, not a direct picture of ancient belief.

## 1. Worldview: a forest full of spirit

Medieval texts and archaeological finds suggest that springs, rivers, groves and hills in the Celtic world were often linked to deities or holy places. Many river names even preserve the names of old goddesses.

The name of the Celtic sages, the <Term definition="The class of priests, judges and scholars in ancient Celtic society. What we know of them comes mainly from Greek and Roman writers and from medieval Irish literature.">druids</Term>, is often explained as «those who know the oak», though this etymology is still debated. According to Caesar, the druids did not commit their teachings to writing but learned them by memorizing verses over many years; the reasons he gives are his own guesses. This image of oral learning and reverence for nature calls to mind the Daoist pursuit of simplicity and authenticity.

## 2. The calendar: the «Celtic tree calendar» is a modern creation

The «Celtic tree calendar» you often see online, with 13 trees for 13 months of 28 days each, was not the calendar of the ancient Celts. It comes from *The White Goddess* (1948) by the English poet Robert Graves, and 20th-century popular books later turned it into «tree astrology». There is no ancient evidence for this system.

The ancient Celtic calendar that actually survives is the <Term definition="Fragments of a bronze calendar found at Coligny, France, in 1897. Dated to around the 2nd century CE and written in Gaulish, it is a lunisolar calendar combining lunar months with the solar year.">Coligny Calendar</Term>. It is based on lunar months and adds intercalary months to keep in step with the solar year. It contains no tree names.

So if you have found your «birth tree» in a Graves-style tree calendar, it is best treated as a modern game of self-reflection that borrows tree symbolism, not as an ancient tradition or a sign of destiny.

## 3. Symbols: the Ogham script

Ogham is an ancient alphabet made of straight and slanted strokes. The earliest surviving examples are inscriptions from roughly the 4th to 6th centuries, cut along the edges of standing stones in Ireland and western Britain. Most of them record personal names and served as memorials or boundary markers.

Medieval Irish scholars called Ogham a «tree alphabet» and gave each letter the name of a tree or plant (birch, willow, holly and so on). Linguists point out, however, that this pairing was probably a later systematization, and some letter names did not originally refer to trees at all.

Even so, tree symbolism can still serve as a prompt for reflection. Thinking of the willow may remind you to bend and adapt to change like its branches; thinking of the holly may remind you to keep your warmth through the winter. This is metaphor, not prophecy.

<HighlightBox type="insight" title="Across Traditions">
  Celtic ideas of sacred trees and groves resemble East Asian traditions of protected village groves and guardian trees. Both remind us that humans are not masters of the earth but members of the forest that lives alongside us.
</HighlightBox>

## 4. Why it matters today: reconnecting with nature

In a digital life, the images of Celtic myth offer a way back. They remind us that spirituality does not have to mean leaving the world behind; it can be like a tree: **roots in the earth, crown toward the sky**.

Noticing the trees around you on a walk, learning their names and watching them change with the seasons can help you feel connected to nature again, and give you a sense of **rootedness** that goes beyond worldly success or failure.

## 5. Conclusion: the endless cycle

One theme that keeps returning in Celtic myth is the **cycle**: the seasons turn, and after the leaves fall come new buds. Seen this way, we may be able to meet life's ups and downs a little more calmly.

<HighlightBox type="info" title="Note">
  This article describes the cultural history of myths and symbols. There is no scientific evidence that tree calendars or Ogham divination can predict the future or determine personality. Please use them as tools for self-reflection.
</HighlightBox>

### Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

**Q: Can Ogham be used for divination?**
A: A medieval Irish tale, *The Wooing of Étaín*, describes a druid cutting Ogham into yew rods to find someone who had gone missing. That is a literary scene, though, and it does not prove a systematic method of divination existed. Most of today's Ogham divination, such as casting inscribed wooden staves, is a modern practice from the 20th century onward. Like runes or the *I Ching*, it can be used as a tool for reflection, but there is no scientific evidence that it predicts the future.

**Q: Why do people say the Celtic calendar had 13 months?**
A: The idea of «13 months of 28 days» comes from Graves's tree calendar, not from an ancient calendar. A real lunar month lasts about 29.5 days, and the surviving ancient Celtic calendar (the Coligny Calendar) is built on 12 lunar months, with extra months inserted to keep pace with the solar year.
