## What is self-esteem?

**Self-esteem** is a subjective evaluation of your own worth: the sense that you are a person of value. Nathaniel Branden, in *The Six Pillars of Self-Esteem* (1994), formulated it as the disposition to experience one's own worth. That is his formulation in a book. It is not a study of how common low self-esteem is.

People also distinguish **state self-esteem**, which can shift with the situation, from **trait self-esteem**, a more stable tendency. A steadier self-esteem means the core sense of worth does not collapse at every outside judgment, while an honest picture of yourself remains. It is not a fixed score or a diagnosis.

## 2. What can affect a low sense of worth

These are factors that often appear in the story. They are not a list of causes, and no single one decides an adult's self-esteem.

### 1. Repeated early relationship experience

Conditional regard (love only when you behave perfectly), or repeated rejection and neglect, can feed a belief such as "I am not worthy of love." One early experience does not decide adult self-esteem.

### 2. Repeated criticism and shame

A line such as "Why can't you do anything right?", heard again and again, can become an inner evaluating voice. People often call that voice an inner critic.

### 3. Social comparison

Comparison can always produce someone who looks further ahead. A rule about hours online is less to the point than what you compare and how you feel and act afterward.

### 4. Stretching one failure into a whole identity

Turning one failure into "I always fail" is a cognitive distortion. One result is not evidence about your entire worth.

<Callout type="info" title="Self-esteem and self-efficacy">
- **Self-esteem**: "I am a person of worth" — an evaluation of your existence
- **Self-efficacy**: "I can do this" — a belief about a particular task

The two are related and not the same. If worth is hung only on performance, a failure can shake self-esteem as well. Separating worth from output is a distinction this topic often uses.
</Callout>

## 4. Practices you can look at

These are not a treatment protocol. One is a picture of a thought record. The others are small things a person can try.

### Practice 1: A thought record, as an illustration

Cognitive-behavioral work often shows a thought record: notice an automatic thought, then question a conclusion that has been stretched. The block below is an illustration. It is not a treatment plan.

```
Automatic thought: "I ruined that presentation. I always do."
Question 1: "Always? What about the last one?"
Question 2: "Does a disappointing presentation shrink my whole worth?"
A less lopsided sentence: "This one was disappointing, and I can prepare differently next time."
```

### Practice 2: Self-acceptance and self-compassion

Self-acceptance is not self-justification. It is allowing the sentence "this is how I am right now, and this struggle is part of the present." Kristin Neff, in *Self-Compassion* (2011), argues that self-compassion tracks well-being differently from self-esteem. That is her argument. It is not a proof that self-compassion is stronger.

### Practice 3: Saying a boundary

A boundary is one way to practice self-respect in action. You can name a limit on time, energy, or emotional availability and say it clearly. It is not a technique with a guaranteed effect.

### Practice 4: Keeping a small promise

Instead of a large goal, you can choose a small action you can actually keep. The point is to notice whether a small action, a completion, and a sense that you kept your word can sit together. It is not a law about how self-esteem is built.

<Callout type="tip" title="Three lines in a self-esteem note">
You can write three things at night.
1. One small thing you did today
2. Something that was hard and that you got through
3. One line you want to leave for tomorrow's you

Reading the notes back now and then can help you see repeated self-criticism beside things you already did. There is no promise that a set number of weeks will produce a pattern.
</Callout>

## 5. When to talk with a counselor

Self-guided practice does not fit every situation. If the following continue, it is worth looking for counseling. This is not a diagnostic list.

- Ongoing self-hatred or a persistent sense of worthlessness
- The same painful pattern repeating across relationships
- Depression or anxiety alongside the self-esteem problem
- Abuse or trauma that still sits under how you evaluate yourself

This page does not replace that conversation.
