## What the tradition says

In the Indian teaching called Jyotish, a birth chart is read as a map of karma and of timing. The word is Sanskrit for a study of light. Teachers in this line say the chart shows tasks a soul carries. That is the tradition's claim, not a laboratory result. This page sets out those claims, and where they stop.

## A name, and a later natal system

Inside the tradition, Jyotish is counted as one of the Vedangas, the limbs of Vedic learning. An early text, the Vedanga Jyotisha, is a ritual calendar. It is not the natal system people meet now.

The rashis (signs), the grahas (planets the chart uses), and the Vimshottari dasha belong to a later tradition. Historians, including David Pingree, discuss contact with Hellenistic astrology in the early centuries CE. That is a historical argument about transmission. It does not date the system to a remote prehistoric start, and it does not offer astronomy as proof that a chart predicts a life.

## Two zodiacs, and neither is the real sky alone

A tropical zodiac fixes the March equinox at 0° Aries. It follows the seasons. A sidereal zodiac uses a star reference. Indian practice often uses the Lahiri ayanamsa. In the early 21st century that offset is about 24°, and the number changes as the reference drifts.

A tropical sun sign and a sidereal sun sign can therefore differ. Someone called Leo in one book can be called Cancer in the other. Neither label is the real sky that makes the other false. They are two ways of naming the same ecliptic.

## Karma names in this teaching

The teaching uses three names.

- Sanchita is stored karma, in the tradition's picture of what a soul carries.
- Prarabdha is the portion said to be assigned to this life.
- Kriyamana is karma being made now.

These are the tradition's terms. The chart does not prove past lives. Reading prarabdha in a horoscope is an interpretation inside the books, not a measurement of a previous life.

## A timetable inside the system

Vimshottari dasha divides a life into planetary periods. In that system the Jupiter mahadasha lasts 16 years. That is a rule of the system. It does not say that you are in a Jupiter period, and it does not say that the next 16 years favor marriage or study. A personal period appears only after a chart is calculated. Even then, the sentence is a traditional reading, not a forecast shown to come true.

Western books often ask what a sign says about temperament. Jyotish books often ask which planetary period is said to be active. Those are different questions inside their own books.

## Remedies in the literature

Gemstones, mantras, and charity appear as remedies, called upaya. They are traditional practices. They are not shown to change events, and they are not medical care. In this teaching a difficult chart is not a diagnosis.

## Yoga, Ayurveda, and Jyotish

Yoga, Ayurveda, and Jyotish are three Indian traditions sometimes taught as a set: practice of body and attention, a traditional picture of health, and timing. Ayurveda here is not clinical medicine. Jyotish is not a science of fate.

## Doctrine, not a finding

Some teachers say that nothing is accidental, and that the stars watch over you. That is doctrine inside the tradition. It is not a result from a test.

## Questions readers ask

**Which is more accurate, the tropical zodiac or the sidereal one?**

This page does not pick a winner. Controlled tests have not shown birth charts predicting personality or events. Carlson (1985) reported a double-blind test: natal-chart readings did not match personality profiles above chance. Hartmann, Reuter, and Nyborg (2006) found no link, in large samples, between date of birth, including sun signs, and personality or general intelligence. The two traditions ask different questions inside their own books.

**Does this belong with yoga?**

They are sometimes taught together, as above. Studying one does not turn the others into treatment, and it does not prove a destiny.
