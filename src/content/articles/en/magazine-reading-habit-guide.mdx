## Why read books at all?

Why books still matter in a flood of digital content:

- **Depth**: a 10-minute YouTube video = the highlights; a book = years of the author's thinking
- **Focus training**: practice in digging into one topic without interruption
- **Vocabulary**: stronger text-based thinking and expression
- **Imagination**: the ability to build images yourself, without visual stimulation

Many of the world's best-known leaders are avid readers. Bill Gates reads about 50 books a year, Warren Buffett around 500 pages a day, and Oprah Winfrey 1–2 books a month.

## Building a reading habit

### Why the habit doesn't stick

- Picking the wrong book: boring or far too difficult
- Stalling midway: giving up once it gets hard
- Lack of time: no room for books in a busy day
- Digital competition: your phone is more stimulating

### Principles of habit formation

**Make the start tiny**: begin with "10 pages a day." Opening the book beats a perfect reading plan.

**Fixed time and place**: the same time, the same spot, every day. Your brain gets ready automatically.

**Keep a book within reach**: put a book next to your phone charger, so your hand reaches for the book first.

**Read two books at once**: when your focus fades, switch to a book in a different genre (whether one book at a time or two in parallel works better varies from person to person).

## Choosing what to read

### Finding books that fit you

The first-30-pages rule: if it doesn't interest you, drop it. You're under no obligation to finish.

**Starter picks by genre**:
- Self-improvement: *Atomic Habits* (James Clear)
- Economics and finance: *The Psychology of Money* (Morgan Housel)
- Psychology: *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (Daniel Kahneman)
- Fiction: fast-paced bestselling mysteries and sci-fi

### Which books to choose

**Quality filters**:
1. Books published 5+ years ago that people still talk about (classics and steady sellers)
2. Recommendations from people you trust
3. Books that tackle a real problem you have (need creates motivation)

**Books to avoid**:
- Short-lived trendy bestsellers (often shallow)
- Books so hard you can't get through them (start at your level)

## Reading speed and depth

### Speed reading vs. close reading

| Method | Speed | Depth | Best for |
|------|------|------|---------|
| Speed reading | Fast | Low | Gathering information, spotting trends |
| Close reading | Slow | High | Academic books, classics, books you need to remember |

**Your purpose decides how you read.**

Not every book needs a close reading. If you just need information, speed-reading the key chapters is fine.

### Reading nonfiction

1. **Read the table of contents first**: grasp the overall structure (20 minutes)
2. **Read the introduction and conclusion first**: identify the core argument
3. **Read the body**: chapter by chapter, as deeply as you need
4. **Take notes**: underline key sentences and jot notes in the margins

### Reading fiction

Read without breaking the flow. Immersion comes before analysis.

Stopping halfway is poison for a novel. Once you start, read for at least 30 minutes straight.

## How to remember what you read

Reading and then forgetting is the biggest source of frustration for readers.

### Take notes right away

- While reading: underline key sentences and note reactions or connected ideas in the margins
- When you finish: write down 3–5 key lessons yourself

### Keep a reading journal

**Option 1 — Notion/Obsidian**:
- Title, author, date read
- 3 core arguments
- Memorable passages
- What to apply to your own life

**Option 2 — Readwise**:
- Automates spaced-repetition review of your e-book (Kindle) highlights
- Emails you highlights from past reading every day

### Active recall

After you finish:
1. Close the book
2. Ask yourself: "What are 3 things I learned from this book?"
3. Answer, then open the book and check

Retrieving information strengthens memory more than simply rereading it.

### Teach it

Explaining what you learned to someone else makes it stick much better.

- Write a summary post on your blog
- Present at a book club
- Tell family or friends "about the book I read today"

## A plan for 50 books a year

### Do the math

30 minutes a day × 350 days = 175 hours

An average book = 200–300 pages = 3–5 hours

**175 hours ÷ 4 hours = about 43 books**

Just 30 minutes a day makes 40–50 books a year possible.

### Where to find the time

- **Commute**: e-books or audiobooks
- **Lunch break**: 20 minutes of reading after eating
- **Before bed**: 15 minutes with a book instead of your phone
- **Weekend mornings**: an hour at a café

### Use audiobooks

Audiobooks while driving, working out, or doing chores can more than double how much you read.

Platforms:
- **Audible (Amazon)**: the largest library of English-language audiobooks
- **Millie (Korea)**: a reading subscription that includes Korean audiobooks
- **Storytel**: audiobooks in many languages and markets

## Join a book club

On your own, the habit may last three months; with a group, it can last ten years.

What a book club gives you:
- A sense of social responsibility to actually read
- Shared interpretations → deeper understanding
- A wider range of reading (you read books you'd never pick)

**How to start a book club**:
- Find a local book club on Meetup.com
- Start small with 3–5 coworkers
- Run an online book club (on Zoom)

## E-books vs. print books

| Item | E-books | Print books |
|------|-------|--------|
| Price | Cheaper (subscriptions) | Higher per copy |
| Portability | Excellent | Heavy |
| Retention | Slightly lower | Slightly higher (tactile memory) |
| Highlights and notes | Convenient digital tools | For those who prefer analog notes |
| Eye strain | Low with an E-ink reader | None |

**Bottom line**: e-books for commuting and travel, print books for deep reading at home.

Reading is about consistency, not speed. Five years from now, someone who steadily reads even one book a month will be far ahead of someone who reads ten a month and then quits.
