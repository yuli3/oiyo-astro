## Where did tarot come from?

You may have heard that tarot preserves the secret wisdom of ancient Egypt. Historians say otherwise. Tarot began in **mid-15th-century northern Italian courts as a card game called *tarocchi***. Lavish cards painted for the Visconti and Sforza families of Milan are the oldest surviving tarot (Dummett, 1980).

Its use for divination and the occult came much later. In 1781 the Frenchman Antoine Court de Gébelin claimed that tarot held Egyptian wisdom — without evidence. Fortune-tellers and 19th-century occult societies then layered astrology, Kabbalah and other symbol systems onto it (Decker, Depaulis & Dummett, 1996). The most widely used deck today, the Rider–Waite–Smith, appeared in 1909, designed by Arthur Edward Waite and **illustrated by Pamela Colman Smith**.

Knowing this history makes tarot easier to approach. It is not an ancient book of prophecy but **a collection of symbols that people have been adding meaning to for centuries**.

## The 78-card structure

| Section | Cards | Content |
|---|--:|---|
| Major Arcana | 22 | From 0 The Fool to 21 The World — life's big themes |
| Minor Arcana | 56 | Four suits × 14 cards (Ace–10 plus four court cards) |

### The four suits and elements

In the 19th-century occult tradition, the four suits were paired with the four elements. In the Rider–Waite–Smith tradition they are commonly read like this:

- **Wands** — fire: passion, action, beginnings
- **Cups** — water: emotions, relationships
- **Swords** — air: thought, conflict, judgement
- **Pentacles (coins)** — earth: work, money, body, practical life

### Court cards: people and attitudes

Each suit's **Page, Knight, Queen and King** are often read as real people or as attitudes you are taking: the Page as the learning stage, the Knight as the pushing stage, the Queen as inward maturity and the King as outward, leading maturity. When a court card appears, asking "Who — me or someone around me — is showing this attitude?" makes interpretation easier.

## The Fool's Journey: the Major Arcana as one story

Reading the Major Arcana as a **coming-of-age story** in which 0, The Fool, meets each card in turn from 1, The Magician, to 21, The World, is called the "Fool's Journey." The original game cards carried no such narrative; it is a **reading tradition** that spread through 20th-century guidebooks. Rachel Pollack (1980) read the Major Arcana as a journey in three stages, and Sallie Nichols (1980) interpreted it through Jungian archetypes and individuation.

The framework helps you **remember the 22 cards as a flow** rather than memorising them one by one. Just keep in mind that it is a modern way of reading, not the historical original meaning. Because it resembles Joseph Campbell's "hero's journey," many guides compare the two.

## The link to numerology

The numbers on tarot cards are often linked to numerology, the tradition of giving meaning to numbers. For example, 1 is read as a beginning (The Magician, the Aces) and 10 as the completion of a cycle (the Wheel of Fortune, each suit's 10). Adding up a birth date to find a "birth card" is also a popular practice.

Such links are useful for sparking associations, but **there is no scientific evidence that numbers determine a person's character or fate**. They work best as a playful prompt.

## Using tarot for self-reflection

There is no evidence that tarot predicts the future. Many people use it anyway because randomly drawn symbols can **bring up perspectives they had not considered**. It tends to be more useful when you:

- **Ask open questions, not yes/no ones**: rather than "Will it work out with them?", ask "What am I missing in this relationship?"
- **Write down what the card brings to mind**: the thoughts the image sparks matter more than the book meaning.
- **Make decisions yourself**: for important matters such as health, money or law, decide on facts and professional advice, not cards.
