## An annual fortune text, not an agricultural almanac

Tojeong-bigyeol (土亭秘訣) is a Korean text used to consult an individual’s fortunes for the coming year. The [Academy of Korean Studies entry on the text](https://encykorea.aks.ac.kr/Article/E0059207) describes personal annual fortune reading. This is different from an agricultural or weather calendar and from a validated prediction model.

This article introduces the history and calculation structure described by institutional sources. It does not compare a complete historical edition or calculate your personal combination. Please do not treat it as evidence of fortune-telling efficacy, prevention of misfortune, or improved health.

## Attribution to Yi Ji-ham is not confirmed authorship

The [biographical entry on Yi Ji-ham](https://encykorea.aks.ac.kr/Article/E0046084) identifies him as a Joseon figure, 李之菡, who lived from 1517 to 1578. It connects his pen name Tojeong (土亭) with an earthen-walled dwelling by the river in Mapo. His biography alone cannot establish why this book was written or when it took shape.

The entry on the book says it is uncertain whether he wrote it himself or whether his name was used as an attribution. It also notes that the text is absent from Tojeong yugo, his collected writings. We therefore do not assert that he personally wrote it in the sixteenth century to protect ordinary people from fraudulent fortune-tellers. A tradition of attribution is not the same as confirmed authorship.

The institutional explanations were checked directly; the earliest Joseon edition or an autograph manuscript was not. An exact date of composition remains unverified here.

## New Year personal fortunes are not agricultural divination

The [entry on seasonal customs](https://encykorea.aks.ac.kr/Article/E0029819) discusses consulting personal fortunes early in the year. The book entry suggests that this became an established seasonal custom from the time of King Jeongjo onward. That is the entry’s historical estimate, not a verified date of first circulation.

It does not establish an identical ritual in every household on New Year’s morning, unchanged for centuries. Asking about a person’s fortunes and divining a harvest also concern different subjects.

## The three numbers also depend on the year and calendar

The book entry describes selecting a combination through upper, middle, and lower numbers. Not using the birth hour does not mean the birth date alone supplies every input. The year being consulted, age, the length of the birth month in that year, and numbers associated with calendrical stem–branch terms enter the procedure.

<LectureTable title="Selection structure described by the AKS entry" headers={["Position", "Information added", "Divisor"]} rows={[
  ["Upper", "Age + that year’s taese number", "8"],
  ["Middle", "Length of the birth month that year (29 or 30 days) + wolgeon number", "6"],
  ["Lower", "Birthday number + iljin number", "3"]
]} />

The entry describes adding the two inputs at each position and taking the remainder after division by the stated number. This table summarizes the institutional explanation; it is not an executable specification. If the three number ranges contain 8, 6, and 3 choices, the arithmetic relationship is 8 × 6 × 3 = 144 combinations.

The source reviewed here does not settle how zero remainders are replaced by 8, 6, or 3; leap-month handling; a birthday on day 30; the definition of age; or the precise calendrical number tables. A working calculator would first need a stated edition and calendar rules. We do not fill those gaps to produce a personal result.

## Keep the text’s phrases separate from modern advice

The book entry describes monthly readings as three phrases of four characters each. Quotation marks can imply a historical quotation when its edition, combination, and month have not been checked. We therefore do not present the earlier seed, southern-helper, or water-warning examples as verified original passages.

A modern reader may use a phrase to reflect on a plan. That does not establish the author’s intention or prove that the reading prevents misfortune. Studies of Western astrology or birth dates and personality also do not directly test Tojeong-bigyeol.

## Read the culture; check decisions against other evidence

It can help to separate three questions: what wording is transmitted, whether its source and edition are known, and what factual information a current decision requires.

For example, if a reading prompts you to review spending, you can examine your budget and contract terms. This is an everyday editorial example, not a demonstrated effect of the text. Base health, borrowing, investment, and safety decisions on relevant facts and professional help, not on a fortune combination.
