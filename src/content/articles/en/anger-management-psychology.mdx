## When anger rises

Anger often appears on lists of basic emotions. The six that Paul Ekman introduced widely are happiness, sadness, anger, fear, disgust, and surprise. He later discussed more emotions as well. In an evolutionary account, it is explained as a response when a boundary is crossed or a goal is blocked. It is not fixed here in the sentence that it is essential for survival.

Four triggers are often written down.

- **Boundary.** When a right, a space, or a value feels violated
- **Unfairness.** When the treatment feels unjust
- **Frustration.** When an important goal is blocked
- **Threat.** When you feel that you, or someone close, is in danger

## Feeling and action

Anger does tell a person something. It is sometimes read as a signal that something matters, as a force that makes a boundary speakable, and as a motive that makes a needed change visible.

What usually becomes the problem is the action afterward, more than the feeling itself. That is when a relationship breaks, when regret follows the words, or when the action harms oneself or someone else. The link between chronic hostility and health differs in scope from study to study. This article does not assert cardiovascular disease or a decline in immunity as a result of anger. If the body keeps hurting, it is better to see a clinician.

The common idea that pouring anger outward reduces it does not fit well with the experiment of Bushman (2002). In the condition of hitting a punching bag while thinking of the other person, anger did not go down. Sometimes it grew.

## The five are a distinction of this article

Not every psychologist divides anger into five types. The STAXI-2 of Spielberger (1999) separates state anger and trait anger and, in expression, anger expressed outward, anger held in, and control. The five below are **this article's distinction**, unfolding that language into everyday scenes. They are not a diagnostic name.

### 1. Letting it burst

This is closer to shouting at once, throwing an object, or attacking the other person. There may be a feeling of release for a moment. The relationship and the regret still tend to remain. Wetting the wrists in cold water, or stepping away from the spot for a short time, is introduced as practice that lowers the body first. It is not a treatment protocol.

### 2. Swallowing it

The anger is felt, but it is not spoken, and it is kept inside. It is talked about together with a belief that anger is bad, or with a habit of avoiding conflict. There are clinical accounts that mood can sink, or that the head and the stomach can hurt from tension. A diagnosis of depression does not follow automatically. If it lasts, it is better to get counseling or clinical care. A journal, or a safe conversation, is a way to practice expression.

### 3. Speaking sideways

It is expressed indirectly, by sarcasm, by being late, by acting as if one did not see, or by quietly blocking the work. It can come from fear of speaking directly, or from a relationship in which power is tilted. Trust is easily damaged. Passive aggression is not given a disease name here. The practice is to say what is wanted, without speaking sideways.

### 4. Stating it

The anger is acknowledged, and it is said in concrete terms without attacking the other person. The I-message of Thomas Gordon is close to this form.

> When you do [the action], I feel [the feeling]. I want [what is wanted].

It becomes information for dealing with the problem. It is not a guarantee that the relationship will necessarily grow deeper.

### 5. Dealing with it after it cools

A person does not react the moment it is felt, and speaks after it has settled. It can look like regulation. If it is never said, it becomes the same as swallowing it.

## It shows in the body first

These are body signals often written down when anger rises. They are not a diagnostic list.

- The shoulders rise and the neck stiffens
- The chest feels tight, or the heart speeds up
- The jaw closes, or the fists clench
- The face grows hot

If this is noticed early, there is time to leave one beat before bursting or swallowing.

A textbook sketch goes like this. The amygdala responds to a stimulus it reads as a threat, and the sympathetic nervous system raises heart rate and muscle tension. An account follows that the prefrontal cortex takes part in regulation. A clock on which this process lasts from 6 to 20 minutes is not used in this article as scientific ground. Arousal differs from person to person. It does not end at one length. The advice not to react at once is a practical pause. It is not the conclusion of that clock.

## Methods people use when dealing with it

Right then:

- **Breath.** The pattern of breathing in for 4 seconds, staying for 7, and breathing out for 8 is a breath that Andrew Weil spread. It is not a settled treatment for anger.
- **Pause.** Leave the place for about 5 to 20 minutes. It is not a magic length.
- **Walking.** Put off the argument while the body is moving.
- **Cold water.** Cold water on the face can set off a short dive reflex. It is not a medicine for anger.

Over a longer view:

- Write down when, after what, and how the reaction went.
- Try making an I-message sentence ahead of time.
- Reappraisal means trying to change a sentence of how terrible it is into a sentence that it is uncomfortable, but can be dealt with. It does not always work.
- Before judging anger as good or bad, try naming that anger is there. Lieberman et al. (2007) report that, in the laboratory, a task of putting words on emotion appeared together with a lowering of amygdala response. That does not mean everyday anger is solved by that task.

## What sits under the anger

In clinical talk, anger is often said to cover an emotion that came first. Fear, the hurt of having been dismissed, a feeling of having no way through, and disappointment when an expectation breaks are examples. It is not a formula that this is always so. The question does not ask only why the anger came. It asks what is underneath.

## Signs that help is needed

In the cases below, it is better to get psychological counseling or a visit in psychiatry. This article is not treatment instructions.

- A relationship or a job has been lost because of anger
- After anger, there is an urge to harm oneself or another person
- The anger does not disappear, and it lasts
- It feels as if it cannot be regulated
