## Why these eight maps

A lot of daily life moves before an explanation does. That is a familiar experience. It is not, by itself, a map of the brain. This page is an introduction to eight questions people file under the unconscious: perception, habit, imagination, memory, unusual experiences, hearing voices, hypnosis, and the sense of a single self.

Where a listed source speaks, the section says what that source actually reported or proposed. Where a mechanism is not in the references, it is marked as a hypothesis or left out. Nothing here is a diagnosis, a test you can give yourself, or a way to treat distress. If an experience disrupts sleep, work, or relationships, or if you do not feel safe, talk with a clinician.

## 1. A gap between seeing and saying that you see

The source for this section is Weiskrantz's 1986 case study of blindsight, centered on a patient known as D.B., after damage to the primary visual cortex. On ordinary testing, part of the visual field was blind. In forced-choice tasks, D.B. could still discriminate some events there — where a target was, how it was oriented, whether it moved, simple form — while saying that he was guessing and that he did not see them.

That is a reported gap between performance and acknowledged awareness, in a person with a specific injury. The book reviews other cases and the arguments around them. Critics pointed to possible stray light and to cortex that might not have been fully destroyed. The finding is not a slogan that the unconscious sees everything, and it is not a finding about healthy vision.

What this source does not show:

- It does not show that ordinary perception copies the world, or that it invents the world.
- Filling in the eye's natural blind spot, the border between dream and waking, face recognition in the temporal lobe, and auditory illusions are different topics. They are not results of this book.
- Blindsight is not a story of the brain reorganizing itself after blindness. The report is about residual discrimination without acknowledged seeing.

Core question: **can a response use visual information that the person says they do not see?** In this case study, sometimes yes. That does not answer whether everyday seeing is the external world itself.

## 2. Habit, in one model

The source is Wood and Neal's 2007 model of habits and goals. It is a model, not a measurement of how much of your day is automatic.

In their account, a habit forms slowly as an association between a response and features of the context that have gone with it: a place, a preceding action. Once formed, noticing that context can start the response without a goal sitting in between. Current goals, and the occasional opposite action, do not by themselves rewrite the association quickly. Goals still meet habits in a few ways. A goal can motivate the repetition that builds the habit, and it can bring the person to the cue. A person may also infer a goal from a habit. Goals and habits can interact while the learned link stays in place.

What people often add, and what this paper does not establish:

- Driving that feels automatic is a common illustration of skill. This paper is not a study of driving.
- Eating with no hunger is an implication people draw, because a cue could fire a response when the original goal is absent. The paper does not report a rate of that eating.
- Executive-function disorders and the limits of multitasking are not claims of this model. An earlier draft of this page mixed in a stray word from another language at that bullet. It was an editing error, not a finding, and it is removed.

Core question: **am I choosing, or is a cue starting a practiced response?** On this model, both can be true, and the paper does not tell you which one your afternoon was.

## 3. Does imagination change the body?

None of the references below studies mental imagery, mirror neurons, empathy, or phantom pain. This guide therefore does not state, as fact, that the brain fails to separate a real action from an imagined one, or that imagination changes the body.

What can be said without those sources:

- People notice bodily shifts with a vivid thought: a tighter muscle, a faster heart. That is an everyday observation, not a mechanism.
- Sport and rehabilitation sometimes use mental practice. Whether imagined movement shares the circuits of performed movement is a hypothesis. It is not tested by the works listed here.
- Explaining empathy by mirror neurons, and explaining phantom pain as imagery rewriting a body map, are separate claims. They are not findings of this reference list.

This is not a training plan. Do not use imagery as a way to treat pain or illness on your own.

Core question: **why can the body stir at a thought?** The question stands. A shared neural code is not established here.

## 4. One study in which a question altered a memory report

The source is Loftus and Palmer, 1974. People watched films of car accidents and then answered questions.

In the first experiment, the question about speed used one of five verbs. Smashed produced higher speed estimates than collided, bumped, contacted, or hit. In the second experiment, the mean estimate was 10.46 mph with smashed and 8.00 mph with hit. A week later, people were asked whether they had seen broken glass. There was no broken glass in the film. Sixteen of 50 who had heard smashed said yes, against 7 of 50 who had heard hit, and 6 of 50 who had not been asked about speed.

The authors' conclusion is narrow: a question asked after an event can lead someone to reconstruct the memory of that event. The verb and the later yes are what the experiments show.

What this paper does not show:

- It does not show that emotion in general colors memory.
- It does not show how the brain encodes disaster or pain.
- It does not show a self-centered bias, or that people distort memory in order to protect a belief.
- A film and a leading verb are not a theory of the autobiographical self.

Core question: **is the person I remember a faithful record?** This study shows that a report can change after a question. It does not decide what the rest of a life story is.

## 5. Unusual reports, without a borrowed mechanism

People describe experiences at the edge of sleep, experiences they place near death, and events they interpret as alien contact or as something paranormal. Those reports exist as reports.

No reference on this page explains them. A story in which the brain invents a scenario to fill a gap, or in which fear demands a meaning, is a hypothesis. It is not a result of Weiskrantz, Wood and Neal, Loftus and Palmer, Frith, or Gazzaniga. Blindsight is a clinical visual finding. It does not account for abduction narratives or near-death experiences.

This page does not tell you that an experience of yours was unreal, and it does not name it as a symptom. If the experience frightens you or disrupts daily life, talk with a clinician. The page cannot interpret it for you.

Core question: **is an unusual experience proof of poor reasoning, or a guess the brain had to make?** The sources here do not choose.

## 6. Hearing voices: one proposal, not a test

In *The Cognitive Neuropsychology of Schizophrenia* (1992), Frith proposed that some auditory hallucinations, and some experiences of one's own actions or thoughts as alien, might follow from a problem in self-monitoring: inner speech or an action would not be tagged as one's own, and could be heard or felt as coming from outside.

That is a model of a clinical phenomenon studied in schizophrenia research. It is not a demonstration that every voice is mislabeled thought. It is not a description of ordinary inner conversation, which can already feel dialogic without being a symptom. Evidence since the book has been discussed and disputed elsewhere. This page does not treat the 1992 proposal as settled, and it does not turn the proposal into a checklist.

If voices, or the fear around them, disrupt sleep, work, or relationships, or if you do not feel safe, talk with a clinician. Do not use this section to diagnose yourself, and do not use it as instructions for self-treatment.

Core question: **why can a voice in one's own head feel like someone else's?** Frith offered one research proposal about some clinical hallucinations. The question, for any one person, stays open.

## 7. Hypnosis and suggestion, with no mechanism claimed

None of the references is a hypnosis study. This section does not state what hypnosis can do, and it does not state how it works.

Popular culture treats hypnosis as a special power. Researchers do not agree with each other. Some accounts speak of a distinct state. Others speak of suggestion, expectation, and where attention goes. Because this guide's sources do not include that debate, the mechanism stays unmarked. Narrowed attention is one hypothesis among others, not a fact established here.

Repeated advertising can be noticed and talked about. This page does not claim that hidden commands control behavior. No listed source shows that, and it does not describe a warning signal in the brain that would catch them.

Hypnosis here is not a home treatment. Do not use suggestion on yourself to manage distress. If a session, or the pull of suggestion, leaves you worse, talk with a clinician.

Core question: **how far do outside cues shape a person without their noticing?** The question is fair. A number, and a mechanism, are not supplied by these references.

## 8. Split-brain research is not a theory of several ordinary selves

The source is Gazzaniga's 2005 review of about forty-five years of split-brain research. The surgery it looks back on divided the corpus callosum, in most of the studied cases as a treatment for severe epilepsy. After that disconnection, what one hemisphere receives is not always available for the other hemisphere to put into words. Gazzaniga has argued, across that body of work, that the hemisphere which can speak — usually the left — offers an explanation, an interpreter, even for an action that began on the other side.

The review is about that rare surgical condition and about hemispheric specialization. Patients in this literature generally did not describe themselves as having become two people. The paper does not show that an ordinary self is a committee. It does not show that defense mechanisms manufacture extra selves. It does not show that trauma fragments the self. Those are other literatures, and they are not findings of this review. Split-brain research is also not an explanation of dissociative identity.

Core question: **what makes a person one person?** The review does not answer it for an intact brain. It shows how a speaking hemisphere can supply a story when information was not shared.

## How to read this guide

1. Start with blindsight and with the memory experiment, and keep the limits next to the findings.
2. Read the habit section as a model of cues and goals, not as a census of your choices.
3. Treat imagination, unusual experiences, voices, and hypnosis as questions. Use them to see what is not known here.
4. Return to the split-brain review last, as a study of a rare disconnection, not as a portrait of everyone.
