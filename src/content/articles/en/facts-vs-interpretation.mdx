## Check interpretations without erasing facts

An unanswered message may bring the thought “They dislike me.” No reply is an observation; another person’s feelings are an inference. Separating them makes it clearer what needs checking.

This does not mean all events are neutral or suffering comes only from thoughts. Violence, discrimination, illness and financial hardship have real consequences. People who have been harmed do not owe anyone optimism or personal growth. Examining thoughts cannot replace changing harmful conditions or getting support.

## Four columns make the distinction visible

This fictional example is not a diagnosis or a validated assessment.

| Category | A late reply | Question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Observation | Yesterday’s message has no reply yet. | When did I send it, and what have I directly checked? |
| Interpretation | I think this person dislikes me. | What supports this, and what weighs against it? |
| Feeling and body | I feel hurt and anxious; my chest feels tight. | How can I describe this without judging myself? |
| Action | Explain whether it is urgent and check once. | What small, safe action is available? |

“They might be busy” is also a hypothesis. Do not replace a negative guess with a pleasant guess and call it a fact. Leave room for uncertainty, then consider actual replies and repeated behaviour. Repeated broken agreements count as evidence too.

## Reframing means checking evidence, not compulsory optimism

“I could not answer one presentation question” can become “I am always incompetent.” That turns one event into a judgement about the whole person. “I lacked information for this question” is narrower and checkable. Find out whether preparation was insufficient or the question was outside the agreed scope. An alternative thought should fit the evidence, not merely reassure.

CBT addresses behaviour and feelings as well as thoughts; the [NHS introduction](https://www.nhs.uk/tests-and-treatments/cognitive-behavioural-therapy-cbt/) explains its scope. This writing exercise does not replace treatment or an individual assessment. Changing one sentence does not guarantee symptom relief or make the brain physically manufacture a positive reality.

## Keep philosophy separate from psychological evidence

Reading Nietzsche’s perspectivism can prompt reflection on your viewpoint. It does not make “There are no objective facts, so all claims are equally correct” a sound everyday rule. Comparing records and evidence still matters.

The saying about a space between stimulus and response needs care too. The [Viktor Frankl Institute](https://www.viktorfrankl.org/quote_stimulus.html) explains that its attribution is uncertain because Covey did not record the original author. Do not present it as a verified direct quotation from Frankl or as proof that attitude alone can overcome severe harm.

## When to reconsider a thought—and when to change the situation

A feeling is not a wrong answer to refute. You can acknowledge anxiety while examining the separate claim “This person definitely dislikes me.” Feelings, physical states, relationships and circumstances cannot be reduced to one cause.

Try the four columns for one event, leaving unknown information marked “unknown.” Write down one fact to check and one small next action. An uncomfortable experience does not need a happy ending.

If you face danger or mistreatment, prioritise safety and support. If symptoms persist or everyday life becomes difficult, consider appropriate professional help. The aim is not to make you responsible for suffering, but to distinguish checkable thoughts from actions and support you may need.
