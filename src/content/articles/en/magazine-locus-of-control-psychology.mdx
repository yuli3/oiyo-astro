## When it feels like nothing you do makes a difference

When something goes wrong, some people first ask, "Which part of this can I change?" Others feel that "luck, circumstances and timing decide it anyway." These two reactions are not simply optimism versus pessimism. Psychology describes this difference with a concept called locus of control.

Locus of control is a tendency in where we place the causes of what happens in our lives. If you feel that your choices and effort shape the outcome, you lean towards an internal locus of control. If you feel that external factors — luck, powerful people, institutions or other people's decisions — call the shots, you lean towards an external locus of control.

This article is a psychology guide meant to support self-reflection. Locus of control is not a measure of whether a personality is good or bad, and it should not be used as a tool to diagnose anyone's mental health or to assign blame for their life.

---

## Rotter's theory of locus of control

The psychologist Julian B. Rotter proposed locus of control within the framework of social learning theory. Our motivation and choices can differ depending on what kind of link we believe exists between our behaviour and its results.

| Type | Core belief | Typical thought |
|------|-------------|-----------------|
| Internal control | My actions affect the outcome | "If I change how I prepare, the next result could be different" |
| External control | Outcomes depend mainly on outside forces | "In the end, it comes down to luck or circumstances" |

A strong internal locus of control does not mean blaming everything on the individual. A strong external locus of control does not automatically mean being passive, either. In reality, many conditions are genuinely hard for one person to control. What matters is the ability to tell the controllable from the uncontrollable.

A healthy sense of agency sounds less like "everything is my fault" and more like "I'll look for the parts I can influence."

## The strengths and pitfalls of internal control

An internal orientation can support learning and growth. When a result disappoints, someone who asks "What can I change?" is more likely to adjust their strategy and try again. In areas with repetition and feedback — study, exercise, work, relationships — this attitude can be a real source of strength.

Taken too far, however, internal control can slide into self-blame. If you read every failure purely as a personal shortcoming, you can miss structural problems or unfair conditions. Even when an excessive workload, discrimination, financial constraints, health problems or plain luck played a large part, you might conclude only that "I should have done better."

Internal control is healthiest when it focuses on what can be adjusted rather than on blame. It helps to separate what you are responsible for, what you can influence, and what you need to accept.

---

## What external control means, and how it can protect

An external orientation is often described only in negative terms, but it is not always harmful. In situations that truly are hard to control, acknowledging outside factors can be psychologically protective. Accidents, illness, recessions, office politics and family circumstances are real examples of problems that individual effort alone cannot solve.

The trouble starts when external control spreads to every area of life. If even small choices you could change start to feel like "there's no point," you act less. Acting less means fewer experiences of success, and fewer successes can feed back into a growing sense of helplessness.

So the goal is not to eliminate external control. It is to acknowledge real constraints while finding the smallest unit of action you can still take within them.

## Learned helplessness and the loss of control

Research on learned helplessness by Martin Seligman and Steven Maier shows why a sense of control matters. The work began with animal experiments in the 1960s and was later extended to studies with people. After repeatedly finding that effort does not change the outcome, we may stop trying even in situations that are actually controllable.

In a 2016 review, the two researchers partly revised their interpretation. In light of neuroscience findings, becoming passive in the face of a distressing stimulus looks more like a default response than a learned one; what experience teaches is rather the sense that "I can have control". This view fits well with the importance of small experiences of control discussed below.

Learned helplessness is not the same as laziness. It is closer to a process in which repeated past failures or experiences of powerlessness reshape what we expect now. The thought "it won't work anyway" is not merely an excuse; it can be a conclusion learned from real experience.

That is why rebuilding agency calls for small experiences of control rather than big wins. Only as you collect experiences of tiny actions actually changing outcomes does the mind begin to learn again that "if I act, things can change."

## The first question for building agency

To handle locus of control in a healthy way, it can help to sort any problem into three boxes.

| Area | Question | Examples |
|------|----------|----------|
| Controllable | What can I change directly? | Study time, how I make a request, sleep routine |
| Influenceable | What can I partly influence? | Negotiations, the mood of a relationship, team decisions |
| Uncontrollable | What can't I change right now? | Past events, other people's personalities, decisions already made |

This sorting is not about giving up. It is about spending your energy in the right place. Clinging to what you cannot control wears you out; giving up on what you can control leaves you feeling powerless.

## Designing small experiences of control

Agency is hard to restore through thinking alone. You need to experience the link between action and result again, and for that, smaller goals work better. "This week I'll go to bed 20 minutes earlier" can be more effective than "I'm going to change my life."

A good small goal meets three conditions. First, it is something you can do yourself. Second, you can check the result within a short time. Third, failing costs little. Goals like these send your mind the signal that it is safe to try.

Recording the process also matters more than the result. Write down what you did today, what changed a little, and what you could adjust next time. A sense of control grows less from grand achievements than from tiny pieces of evidence that "what I did made a difference."

---

## A sense of control grows on a footing of realism

A healthy locus of control is not unconditional positive thinking. It means seeing the constraints of reality while looking for the moves still possible within them. Believing you can control everything is risky, and so is believing you can change nothing.

Life certainly involves luck, social structures and other people's choices. At the same time, there are parts you can influence: small preparations, the way you speak, when you ask for help, recovery habits and how you design your surroundings. Agency begins with telling these two apart.

---

## Key takeaways

- Rotter's locus of control describes whether we connect life outcomes to our own actions or to external factors.
- Internal control can support growth, but in excess it can turn into self-blame.
- External control helps us acknowledge real constraints, but when it spreads to every area it can deepen helplessness.
- Learned helplessness describes how, after repeated experiences of having no control, attempts to act decrease.
- Agency recovers when we separate the controllable, the influenceable and the uncontrollable, and build up small experiences of control.
