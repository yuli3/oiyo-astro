## These are not five kinds of people

Postponing a disagreement does not necessarily mean rejecting a relationship, and a long discussion does not necessarily produce a good resolution. The same person can respond differently across issues, people, and deadlines. Start with “What did I protect or postpone this time?” rather than “Am I an avoider?”

The Thomas–Kilmann model describes conflict behavior through **assertiveness**, pursuing one’s own concerns, and **cooperativeness**, pursuing the other person’s concerns. These are not scales of loudness or politeness. [Co-developer Kilmann’s TKI overview](https://kilmanndiagnostics.com/a-brief-overview-tki/) explains that a person can use all five modes. This is the provider’s account of the model, not validation of OIYO’s activity.

## Five options, not five grades

| Mode | How the two sets of concerns are approached |
| --- | --- |
| Competing | Pursuing your concerns strongly while giving less weight to the other person’s. |
| Collaborating | Exploring ways to address both sets of concerns. |
| Compromising | Making partial concessions toward a partial agreement. |
| Avoiding | Not directly addressing the issue now, or postponing it. |
| Accommodating | Giving priority to the other person’s concerns over your own. |

These distinctions do not rank people’s character or ability. They do not mean collaboration is always best or compromise is the most common response. Splitting something equally is one option, not an automatic guarantee of fairness when burdens and authority differ.

### Competing: examine authority and consequences

Imagine a deadline requires choosing one proposal. Stating a position clearly is different from insulting someone. You can also check who has decision-making authority, who bears the consequences, and how objections can be recorded. Making a quick decision does not, by itself, make it a good one.

### Collaborating: look for constraints behind positions

Arguing only about “launch today” versus “delay a week” narrows the options. One person may be concerned about a customer commitment, the other about defects. A smaller launch or further checks may be worth exploring, but whether they meet both sets of needs still requires examination. Without enough time or willingness, demanding collaboration can itself become a burden.

### Compromising: record what was conceded

Specify the subject and duration of the concessions. Agreeing to shorten a meeting is not the same as dividing responsibility for work equally. A temporary agreement can include a review date. A desire for a quick agreement does not mean every unexamined risk should simply be split in half.

### Avoiding: distinguish a pause from leaving something unattended

Missing information, fatigue, and competing priorities can all lead someone to postpone a discussion. If you intend to return, you can name a condition: “Let’s check the documents and talk tomorrow afternoon.” You can also choose not to discuss something. The label alone does not reveal hidden motives or the depth of a relationship.

### Accommodating: examine choice and recurring burdens

Letting someone choose a date that matters less to you differs from repeatedly taking on work because you cannot say no. Look at what you agreed to and what burden remains. Frequent concessions do not establish exceptional kindness or prove that someone is not respected.

The scene includes a pause, exploration of both sets of concerns, and partial agreement. It is not a procedure that guarantees success. If the reduced release does not meet the customer commitment, the alternative needs revisiting. ## Conversation wording is not a formula for controlling a response

The Center for Nonviolent Communication (CNVC) describes expressing feelings and needs without blame and making requests rather than demands. [Its guide to NVC’s purpose and process](https://www.cnvc.org/about/purpose-of-nvc/) also emphasizes being prepared to hear a refusal. That is not evidence that a particular sentence will prevent defensiveness.

The following is an example written for this article. It does not reduce all of NVC to four sentences or present an official assessment.

- Observation: “In today’s meeting, we moved to the next item without discussing my proposal.”
- Feeling: “I felt disappointed and frustrated.”
- Need: “I want to understand the decision criteria.”
- Request: “Could you share the review criteria before the next meeting?”

“I was ignored” can include an interpretation of another person’s behavior. Separating observation from interpretation does not guarantee agreement. After a refusal, you can propose another time or format, but you do not gain a right to demand explanations or closeness. You do not need to tolerate unwanted contact or threats as a conflict-resolution exercise.

## How to read the OIYO activity

The [conflict-style activity](/en/conflict-style-test/) adds up the answers selected across 10 pairs of statements into five mode scores. It is not the official TKI and is not presented as a validated psychological measure or personality diagnosis. The result summarizes choices within these particular options.

In a tie, the implementation can show the first mode compared as the representative label. Look at the scores and actual situations rather than the headline alone. A score difference is not the proportion of your real-life behavior or a difference in ability. There is no need to ask someone else to take the activity or share a result.

For one recent disagreement, record your concerns, the other person’s concerns that you actually checked, constraints of time, information and authority, your action, and what remains unresolved. Use the record to make a concrete next attempt, not to fit yourself to a result label.
