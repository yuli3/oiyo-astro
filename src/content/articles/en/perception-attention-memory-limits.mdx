## The brain does not process everything

A huge amount of information reaches our eyes and ears every moment. The brain does not process all of it; it picks what seems important and interprets it quickly. Those choices and shortcuts make everyday life possible, but illusions slip through the gaps. This article covers nine effects arising from the limits of **perception**, **attention**, and **memory and preference**.

## Perception: we see what we interpret, not just what is there

### The Stroop effect: words we cannot help reading

John Ridley Stroop (1935) asked participants to name the **ink colour** of words. When the word "red" was printed in blue, naming the colour became noticeably slower and more error-prone. Reading is so practised that it **happens automatically, without intention**, and it interferes with naming the colour.

**In everyday life**: well-practised responses are hard to switch off. It is why people often slip on screens where button colour and label clash — a green "Cancel" button, for instance.

### Change blindness: missing changes before our eyes

In Simons and Levin's (1998) field study, a person asking for directions was **swapped for someone else** while a door was carried between them. About half the participants did not notice. In Simons and Chabris's (1999) video study, about half of those counting basketball passes failed to see a person in a gorilla suit walk across the scene — **inattentional blindness**.

**In everyday life**: the confidence that "I would have noticed" is not reliable. Take particular care when glancing at a phone while driving or skimming an important document.

### Pareidolia: faces in clouds and power sockets

**Pareidolia** is seeing faces or meaningful shapes in random patterns. Liu and colleagues (2014) showed participants pure-noise images while telling them faces were hidden inside; many reported seeing faces, and brain areas involved in face processing became more active. For a social species, **seeing a face that is not there was less risky than missing one that is**.

**In everyday life**: when you feel drawn to find messages in patterns and coincidences, remember that it may simply be the brain's default setting.

## Attention and processing capacity

### The frequency illusion (Baader-Meinhof phenomenon): once you know it, it is everywhere

You learn a new word or car model and suddenly see it everywhere. In 2005 linguist Arnold Zwicky called this the **frequency illusion**. It has not become more common; your **attention now selectively picks it up**, and each sighting is remembered as evidence that "it is suddenly everywhere."

**In everyday life**: treat the feeling that "this has really increased lately" as a hypothesis until you check the numbers.

### Miller's law: how much we can hold at once

George Miller (1956) summarised the observation that people can hold and handle about **seven, plus or minus two** items at once. Cowan (2001) later revised this: when rehearsal and grouping strategies are blocked, true capacity is **around four**. **Chunking** — remembering a phone number in groups, for example — is the key to stretching it.

**In everyday life**: when explaining something, bundle your points into three or four.

### Hick's law: more choices, slower responses

Hick (1952) and Hyman (1953) showed that reaction time grows with the **logarithm of the number of choices**. Doubling the options does not double the time; it adds a constant amount.

**In everyday life**: menus, screens and forms get faster when frequent options come first and the rest sit one level deeper. Note that the law comes from simple reaction tasks and should not be transferred wholesale to complex decisions.

## Memory and preference

### Negativity bias: bad things stick more

After reviewing a large body of research, Baumeister and colleagues (2001) concluded that **"bad is stronger than good."** One criticism lingers longer than several compliments, and a bad first impression is harder to change than a good one. Rozin and Royzman (2001) likewise found that negative information draws more attention and sways judgement more.

**In everyday life**: deliberately noting what went well when you review your day restores some balance. Keep the asymmetry in mind when giving feedback, too.

### The Zeigarnik effect: unfinished tasks stay on our mind

Bluma Zeigarnik (1927) reported that people remember interrupted tasks better than finished ones. The **better-memory** part, however, has not replicated consistently (Ghibellini & Meier, 2023). The sturdier finding is that unfinished tasks **keep circling in our thoughts**. Masicampo and Baumeister (2011) showed that simply **making a concrete plan** for the task reduces those intrusive thoughts.

**In everyday life**: a task that will not leave your head often feels lighter once you write down "when, where and what."

### The mere-exposure effect: seeing something often makes us like it

Robert Zajonc (1968) showed that even unfamiliar Chinese characters or nonsense words were **rated more favourably the more often they had been seen**. A meta-analysis of some 200 experiments (Bornstein, 1989) confirmed the effect, adding that too much repetition can breed boredom instead.

**In everyday life**: when an advertised or familiar brand feels good, ask whether it is quality or just familiarity.

## Summary at a glance

| Effect | One line to remember |
|---|---|
| Stroop effect | Practised responses butt in whether we want them or not |
| Change blindness | Do not trust "I would have noticed" |
| Pareidolia | Finding meaning in patterns is the brain's default |
| Frequency illusion | "It has increased lately" is a hypothesis until checked |
| Miller's law | We hold about four things at once — bundle them |
| Hick's law | More options, slower responses |
| Negativity bias | Bad things stick more |
| Zeigarnik effect | Writing a plan lightens tasks that keep circling |
| Mere exposure | Familiar is easily mistaken for good |

These limits are not faults but design — efficient ways for a brain that cannot process everything. Knowing them lets you set up safeguards in advance for the moments that matter.
