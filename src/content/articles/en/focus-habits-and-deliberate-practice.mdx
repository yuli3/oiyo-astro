## Not the buzzwords — what the research says

"Deep work," "dopamine fasting," "21 days to a habit," "the 10,000-hour rule." Self-help content is full of attractive phrases. This article sets out **which research each came from**, **how far it actually holds**, and **what you can try today**.

## Focus: there are reasons it scatters

### Deep work and attention residue

In *Deep Work* (2016), Cal Newport called focused, distraction-free time on cognitively demanding work **deep work** and argued it is becoming scarce. Research supports parts of this. Leroy (2009) showed that when we switch tasks, thoughts about the previous task linger and lower performance on the next — **attention residue**. Mark and colleagues (2008) found that when work is frequently interrupted, people work faster but experience **more stress and pressure**.

**Try today**: block one or two 60–90-minute periods in your calendar for a single task with notifications off.

### Environment beats willpower

Summarising habit research, Wendy Wood (2019) emphasised **friction**. Making distractions hard to reach — leaving your phone in another room — works better than straining to resist them.

**Try today**: when focusing, put your phone out of reach and move your most-used apps off the home screen.

### Digital minimalism and "dopamine fasting"

In *Digital Minimalism* (2019), Newport proposed a 30-day "digital declutter": step away from optional technologies, then bring back only what truly adds value.

The trend of **"dopamine fasting"** that grew up alongside it is misnamed. Dopamine is not something you deplete or "reset" by fasting. Neuroscience describes dopamine less as pleasure itself than as a signal of **reward anticipation and prediction** — of wanting (Berridge & Robinson, 1998; Schultz, 2016). Cutting back on highly stimulating activities for a while may help focus and rest, but there is no scientific basis for explaining that as "lowering dopamine."

**Try today**: rather than "quitting dopamine," decide **what to cut and what to replace it with** — for example, a paper book instead of a screen for the last 30 minutes before sleep.

## Habits: they take longer than you think, and plans help

### Not 21 days — a median of 66

Lally and colleagues (2010) had participants repeat a new behaviour daily (such as eating fruit at lunch) and tracked when it became automatic. The time varied enormously, **from 18 to 254 days** depending on the person and behaviour, with a median of **66 days**. Missing a day or two did not seriously derail the process.

### Cues and repetition

Wood and Neal (2007) explained that habits are tied less to goals than to **context cues** — time, place, a preceding action — and run automatically. That is why attaching a new behaviour to an existing one ("after brushing my teeth, two minutes of stretching") works well. Gollwitzer's (1999) research on **implementation intentions** likewise showed that people who decide "when, where and what" in advance follow through more often.

### Kaizen: very small, and continuous

**Kaizen**, from Japanese manufacturing, means accumulating small improvements steadily (Imai, 1986). Applied to personal habits, it becomes a strategy of starting at a size that is **hard to fail** — "one minute a day," "one page" — and growing gradually. Small starts fit the cue-and-repetition structure the research describes.

**Try today**: write one new habit as "after (something I already do), I will (new behaviour) for (small amount)," and aim for two months.

## Practice: what matters more than 10,000 hours

### Deliberate practice

Ericsson and colleagues (1993) reported that the best violinists had accumulated more hours of solitary **deliberate practice**. Deliberate practice is not mere repetition; it has **specific goals targeting weaknesses, immediate feedback, and difficulty just beyond your comfort level**. As the research spread, the phrase "10,000-hour rule" caught on.

### But practice is not everything

In a meta-analysis of 88 studies, Macnamara, Hambrick and Oswald (2014) found that deliberate practice explained 26% of performance differences in games, 21% in music, 18% in sports, 4% in education and under 1% in professions. Practice matters, but **starting age, ability, quality of teaching and environment** matter a great deal too.

**Try today**: rather than adding practice time, first decide "the one thing I will fix this session" and "how I will get feedback on it."

## Writing: morning pages and expressive writing

In *The Artist's Way* (1992), Julia Cameron proposed **morning pages**: three handwritten pages of whatever comes to mind each morning. It is well known as a way to unblock creativity, but little research has tested the method itself.

Better studied is Pennebaker's (1997) **expressive writing**. In studies where people wrote about their thoughts and feelings on difficult experiences for 15–20 minutes over a few days, small improvements in health and mood were reported. The effects are generally modest.

**Try today**: on a day when your head feels crowded, write by hand for ten minutes without judging. The goal is not good writing but a clearer head.

## Summary at a glance

| Buzzword | What the research says | Try today |
|---|---|---|
| Deep work | Switching leaves attention residue | A 60–90-minute block, notifications off |
| Designing your environment | Friction beats willpower | Phone in another room |
| Dopamine fasting | Dopamine is not something you "reset" | Decide what to cut and what to add |
| 21-day habits | Median 66 days, wide variation | Aim for two months; attach to an existing habit |
| Kaizen | Small improvements add up | Start at a hard-to-fail size |
| 10,000 hours | Practice explains only part | Start with one fix and a feedback method |
| Morning pages | Expressive writing has small effects | Ten minutes of unjudged writing |

Good habits and focus come less from strong willpower than from **well-designed environments and realistic expectations**. It is a little slower than the buzzwords promise, but it lasts.
