## A treatment helping and knowing why it helps are different questions

Christopher Palmer’s *Brain Energy* invites readers to consider mental health in relation to metabolism. That perspective does not establish that one mechanism explains every treatment’s benefits and adverse effects. Rather than claiming to substantiate individual passages in the book, this article offers a way to assess metabolic explanations.

An improvement after treatment, evidence that a treatment outperforms a comparison condition, and a claim that mitochondrial recovery caused that improvement are different propositions. The last requires investigating whether the proposed pathway actually mediates the effect, not merely whether treatment and symptoms are associated.

| Claim being read | Question to check separately |
| --- | --- |
| Someone improved after treatment | What changed, by how much, and over what follow-up period? |
| A treatment is effective | For which patients, comparison conditions, and outcomes? |
| Metabolic changes caused improvement | Were those changes measured and distinguished from alternative explanations? |

This distinction does not dismiss established treatments. Clinical outcomes can be evaluated without a complete account of mechanism; a plausible biological explanation does not, by itself, establish clinical benefit.

## Medication: avoid one explanation for an entire category

Medications differ in their indications, expected benefits, and precautions. Neither “medication wakes up mitochondria” nor “long-term medication damages mitochondria” adequately describes the category. Even when a study concerns a particular medication’s metabolic effects, its experimental conditions, dose, population, and clinical outcomes need separate examination.

An observed change in weight or a laboratory result is also distinct from an explanation of its cause. Such a change does not prove a specific cellular mechanism, and this article cannot establish an individual medication’s benefit–risk balance. Discuss personal treatment decisions with the treating clinician.

## CBT: understand the therapy before adding a biological story

The US National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) describes psychotherapy as helping people identify and change troubling emotions, thoughts, and behaviors. Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) examines inaccurate or harmful automatic thoughts and addresses connections between thoughts, emotions, and behavior. Treatment approaches are selected for the person’s needs and difficulties. [NIMH: Psychotherapies](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/topics/psychotherapies)

That description should not become a proven chain of “counseling → lower cortisol → normalized mitochondria → recovery.” Evidence measuring and connecting each step would be needed separately. When reading about psychotherapy outcomes, consider practical goals such as daily functioning and quality of life alongside symptoms.

## ECT and rTMS are not the same “reboot”

Both are brain stimulation treatments, but their procedures differ. NIMH explains that ECT uses an electrical current to induce a seizure under general anesthesia with a muscle relaxant. Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) uses a magnetic field to induce current and stimulate particular brain areas, without requiring anesthesia.

| Treatment | Procedure and cautions |
| --- | --- |
| Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) | Clinicians may consider it for severe illness or when other treatments have not helped, among other circumstances. Risks such as confusion and memory problems also require consideration. |
| Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) | Stimulation is delivered with a magnetic coil. Headache and discomfort at the stimulation site can occur; seizures are a rare risk. |

This is not a treatment-selection table. Suitability requires professional assessment of diagnosis, treatment history, and individual risks. “Removing old mitochondria and making new ones to reboot the brain” should not be presented as an established explanation of either treatment’s clinical benefits. [NIMH: Brain Stimulation Therapies](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/topics/brain-stimulation-therapies/brain-stimulation-therapies)

## Do not turn lifestyle and treatment into competitors

Avoid the assertion that every treatment is a temporary fix until diet, sleep, and exercise problems are resolved. Considering difficulties in daily life is different from discounting existing care. If treatment is not helping enough, review changes in symptoms and functioning, treatment burdens, and follow-up plans with the clinician rather than fitting everything into a single “energy shortage” explanation.

<summary>Fictional example: do better sleep and mood prove a metabolic mechanism?</summary>

Suppose someone sleeps better and feels better after starting treatment. Their workload also decreases during the same period. This record alone cannot separate the contributions of treatment, work changes, and sleep changes. If mitochondrial function was not measured, there is even less basis for saying that mitochondrial recovery caused the improvement.

That does not mean dismissing the improvement or concluding that treatment was ineffective. Record what changed while leaving an untested mechanism unconfirmed.

## Ask about concrete treatment goals

You can ask which symptoms and daily functions the treatment targets, what benefits and risks to expect, how progress will be assessed, and when to discuss insufficient improvement. NIMH’s psychotherapy guide also recommends questions about goals and progress evaluation.

This article is not individualized treatment advice or a guide to stopping treatment. Do not stop or change current treatment on your own. A metabolic perspective can generate research questions, but it cannot replace the evidence and assessment needed for personal care.
