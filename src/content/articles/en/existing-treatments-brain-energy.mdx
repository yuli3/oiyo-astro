### Introduction: One Hypothesis That Tries to Connect Many Treatments

Psychiatry has many treatments, ranging from medication like antidepressants to psychological therapies like Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT), and brain stimulation treatments like Electroconvulsive Therapy (ECT) or Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation (TMS).

Many of these treatments have been shown to help in clinical trials, yet exactly how each one works is still not fully understood. In **Brain Energy** (2022), psychiatrist Christopher Palmer proposes that mental disorders may be, at least in part, disorders of brain metabolism, and that existing treatments may work partly by affecting cellular metabolism and mitochondria. This is a **hypothesis**. It is not the established consensus in psychiatry and is still being tested. This article explains how Palmer interprets each treatment, and separates that interpretation from what research has actually shown.

---

### 1. Medication: What Is Established and What Is Speculative

**What is established:** Large analyses of clinical trials show that antidepressants, on average, work better than placebo for adults with acute major depression (Cipriani et al., 2018). Their usual explanation involves neurotransmitter systems, though the full picture is still being studied.

**Palmer's interpretation:** Some laboratory studies have looked at whether psychiatric drugs also affect mitochondria. Palmer reads this as a sign that part of their effect could be metabolic. Whether this explains how they help patients has not been shown.

**Side effects:** Some psychiatric medications, especially certain antipsychotics, are known to be associated with weight gain and changes in blood sugar and cholesterol, which is why doctors monitor weight and blood tests. Palmer sees this as consistent with his framework, but the causes are not settled. If side effects worry you, raise them with your prescriber rather than stopping on your own; there are often options.

### 2. Talk Therapy and CBT: Effective, With an Untested Metabolic Explanation

**What is established:** CBT and other psychotherapies are effective treatments for depression, anxiety, and other conditions in many trials.

**Palmer's interpretation:** He suggests that by helping people manage chronic stress, therapy may ease the strain that stress hormones such as cortisol place on the body's metabolism. Chronic stress and metabolism are linked in research, but there is no direct evidence that CBT works by restoring mitochondrial function. It is best read as one possible explanation, not a proven mechanism.

### 3. ECT and TMS: Strong Evidence of Benefit, Mechanism Still Unclear

**What is established:** ECT is one of the most effective treatments for severe depression (UK ECT Review Group, 2003). It is given under general anesthesia by specialist teams, and decisions about it are made with a psychiatrist. TMS is approved in many countries for depression that has not responded to other treatments.

**Palmer's interpretation:** He speculates that these stimulation treatments might encourage the production of new mitochondria or the clearing of damaged ones, comparing it to "rebooting" brain cells. This idea draws mainly on animal and laboratory research and has not been confirmed in people. How ECT and TMS work remains an open research question.

### 4. Lifestyle Factors: A Complement, Not a Replacement

Why do many people still struggle despite treatment? Palmer argues that **unaddressed metabolic factors such as diet, sleep, and physical activity may limit how well other treatments work**. This is his hypothesis. The benefits of medication, therapy, and ECT were established in clinical trials regardless of the metabolic view, and it has not been shown that they are only "temporary fixes."

There is broader research support for the idea that exercise, sleep, diet, and not smoking play a role in mental health (Firth et al., 2020), although the strength of the evidence varies by factor and condition. Lifestyle changes can sit alongside your treatment, not replace it. Specific metabolic interventions proposed for mental illness, such as a ketogenic diet, are still in early research and should only be tried under medical supervision, particularly if you take medication.

---

### Wrapping Up: A Hypothesis Worth Following, Not a New Rulebook

The Brain Energy hypothesis offers an interesting attempt to connect many treatments under one idea, and researchers are actively testing parts of it. Until that work is done, it is one perspective among several, not a proven explanation, and it does not promise recovery.

**If this framework interests you, bring your questions to your doctor or care team.** Keep your current treatment as prescribed, and if you want to add supportive habits such as regular sleep, physical activity, or balanced meals, talk them through with the people who know your health.
