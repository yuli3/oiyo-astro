## 1. Origins: Sentinels of the Tribe?

Why don't we all sleep at the same time? One hypothesis is that in small groups, staggered sleep meant someone was usually awake. A field study of Hadza hunter-gatherers in Tanzania found that, over about three weeks of observation, it was rare for all members of a group to be asleep at the same moment (Samson et al., 2017). This is an evolutionary hypothesis, not a settled fact, but it is a kinder way to look at night owls: variation in sleep timing may be part of how humans were built.

## 2. The 4 Animal Types (Dr. Breus's Popular Model)

<Callout>
  The Lion, Bear, Wolf and Dolphin types come from Michael Breus's popular book
  *The Power of When* (2016). They are a friendly shorthand, not a validated
  scientific classification. Researchers usually measure chronotype on a
  continuous scale, for example with the Morningness–Eveningness Questionnaire
  (MEQ; Horne & Östberg, 1976) or the Munich Chronotype Questionnaire (MCTQ;
  Roenneberg et al., 2003), which estimates the midpoint of your sleep on free
  days. Most people fall somewhere in the middle rather than at an extreme.
</Callout>

### ① The Bear

- **Aura:** Solar. Rises with the light and winds down with the dark. Steady, friendly energy.
- **Rhythm Breus suggests:** Wake 7 AM – focused work 10 AM to 2 PM – sleep 11 PM.
- **Truth:** The typical 9-to-5 workday suits you fairly well. Lucky you!

### ② The Lion

- **Aura:** Dawn hunter. Up early and sharpest in the morning; by evening the battery runs low.
- **Rhythm Breus suggests:** Wake 5:30 AM – focused work 8 AM to 12 PM – sleep 10 PM.
- **Truth:** Use your mornings for your hardest problems and decisions. Try not to leave important decisions for late evening.

### ③ The Wolf

- **Aura:** Creature of the moon. Mornings feel heavy; evenings are when ideas flow.
- **Rhythm Breus suggests:** Wake 7:30–9 AM – focused work late afternoon into the evening – sleep after midnight.
- **Truth:** A late rhythm is not the same as laziness. Where you can, look for some flexibility in your schedule.

### ④ The Dolphin

- **Aura:** The light sleeper. Easily woken, a busy and sensitive mind. The name borrows from real dolphins, which rest one half of the brain at a time; in humans this is only a metaphor.
- **Rhythm Breus suggests:** Wake 6:30 AM – focused work 10 AM to 1 PM – aim for sleep around 11:30 PM.
- **Truth:** Chasing a 「perfect」 night can itself make sleep harder. If you regularly struggle to fall or stay asleep and it affects your days, that is worth discussing with a doctor; it may be insomnia, which is a treatable condition, not a personality type.

## 3. Social Jetlag

When your body clock and your social clock (work, school, alarms) disagree, the gap is called 「social jetlag」, a term introduced by Wittmann, Dinich, Merrow and Roenneberg (2006). It is usually estimated as the difference between your sleep midpoint on free days and on workdays. Observational studies have linked larger social jetlag with outcomes such as higher body weight and lower well-being, but these are associations, not proof that one causes the other.
What can help: **work with light.** Light is the strongest signal for the body clock. Late types often benefit from bright light soon after waking and dimmer light in the late evening. Early types who want to stay up a little later can do the opposite and get some light in the evening.

## 4. In short: Rest Without Guilt

Sleep is not wasted time; it is when the body and mind recover.
Whether you lean toward the Lion or the Wolf, it helps to know your rhythm and plan around it where you can. On a free day, try noticing when you would naturally fall asleep and wake up without an alarm.

  please talk to a doctor or a sleep specialist.

### FAQ

**Q: Does it change with age?**
A: Yes. Large surveys show that children tend to be earlier types, people shift later through adolescence, and then gradually earlier again in adulthood and later life (Roenneberg et al., 2004). A teenager who can't get up may be dealing with biology, not just rebellion.

**Q: Can I change my type?**
A: Chronotype is partly heritable, so you probably can't turn a strong night owl into a natural early riser. Consistent light exposure, regular sleep times and meal timing can shift your rhythm somewhat, but how much varies from person to person.
