## 1. Origins: The Gift of Odin's Sacrifice

**Odin**, father of the gods, longed for wisdom above all else. To uncover the secrets of the universe, he hung on **Yggdrasil**, the World Tree, for nine days and nine nights, wounded by a spear and given as a sacrifice to himself.

According to the Eddic poem *Hávamál* (stanzas 138–139), at the edge of death he peered down and, screaming, took up the **runes**. The runes therefore symbolize insight that comes after an extreme ordeal: knowledge paid for with suffering.

## 2. Core Idea: The Web of Wyrd

We often think of fate as a straight line, but the image associated with the old North is a living web called **Wyrd**. (*Wyrd* is an Old English word; its Old Norse counterpart is *urðr*, also the name of one of the Norns. Reading it as past, present and future, and picturing it as a web, are largely modern interpretations.)

- **The past:** The web already woven (unchangeable).
- **The present:** The thread we are weaving now (our choice).
- **The future:** The pattern that emerges from those choices.

In this reading, runes do not announce a fixed fate; they say, "If you keep weaving this way, this is the pattern that will appear." They become tools for **taking charge of your own path**.

<HighlightBox type="info" title="Historical note">
  Ancient sources do not clearly show that the Norse drew runes to predict the
  future. Tacitus (*Germania*, 10) describes casting lots with strips of wood
  marked with signs, but does not say those signs were runes. Rune divination
  as practiced today is largely modern, popularized above all by Ralph Blum's
  *The Book of Runes* (1982). There is no scientific evidence that it predicts
  the future, so please use it as a tool for personal reflection.
</HighlightBox>

## 3. Key Runes

Of the 24 runes of the Elder Futhark, here are three that offer a meaningful perspective. The names and meanings of the runes come to us mainly from medieval rune poems (Old English, Norwegian and Icelandic); the "messages" below are modern interpretations.

### ① Fehu - Abundance

- **Symbol:** Cattle (wealth).
- **Message:** "You already have what you need." It speaks of material wealth, but above all of inner fullness. A good moment for a fresh start.

### ② Uruz - Wild Strength

- **Symbol:** The aurochs (wild ox), according to the Old English rune poem.
- **Message:** "Trust your untamed strength." Sometimes politeness is not enough, and you need to wake your raw nature to get past an obstacle. A sign of vitality.

### ③ Raido - The Journey

- **Symbol:** Riding, the journey.
- **Message:** "The path matters more than the destination." If you feel stuck, Raido whispers that it is time to move. Movement itself can be healing.

<HighlightBox type="insight" title="Insight">
  **The Blank Rune:** It did not exist in antiquity; the empty rune is a modern
  addition (introduced by Ralph Blum in 1982) that stands for the unknown or
  the will of the divine. Sometimes not knowing is a blessing. Embrace the
  mystery.
</HighlightBox>

## 4. Why It Still Matters: An Inner Compass

In a world full of noise, the runes cut through confusion with the simplicity of stone. "Stop (Isa)," "Fight (Tiwaz)," "Love (Gebo)." They teach us the **beauty of the essential**.

## 5. In Short: Finding Your Rune

If the world feels chaotic today, close your eyes and draw a stone from your rune bag. Let the cool pebble hand you an ancient word. Like Odin, you may find that real wisdom is not only in books but in the deep silence within yourself.

### FAQ

**Q: How are runes different from tarot?**
A: Tarot is like an illustrated novel: it tells a complex story. Runes are more like a haiku: they strike with a single, direct truth. Many people find them more instinctive.

**Q: Can I use runes as a talisman?**
A: You can, as a symbol. Runic inscriptions do appear on ancient weapons and jewelry, and some scholars think formulas such as *alu* had a magical or protective function, though this is debated. Linking the rune Algiz to protection comes mostly from modern esoteric practice.
