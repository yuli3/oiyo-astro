## We all see the world from where we stand

We know our own minds in detail but see other people only through what they do. That asymmetry lets different standards slip in when we judge ourselves and when we judge others. Social psychology has named these mismatches many times over. This article sorts seven of them into **illusions that make us loom large** and **illusions that make others look simple**.

## Illusions that make us loom large

### Egocentric bias: my share looks bigger

Ross and Sicoly (1979) asked married partners, separately, what percentage of various tasks — chores, causes of arguments — each was responsible for. Added together, the two answers usually **came to more than 100%**. We remember each thing we did, while what the other person did is less visible.

**Check question**: "How much would my partner say they did?" Especially useful when a team project leaves you feeling unappreciated.

### The spotlight effect: others notice us less than we think

Gilovich, Medvec and Savitsky (2000) had students walk into a classroom wearing a T-shirt with an embarrassing singer's face on it. The wearers expected about half the room to notice; in fact, **only about half that many** could recall it. Our slips and outfits are spotlit mainly for ourselves.

**Check question**: "How busy is everyone with their own concerns right now?" Helpful before a nerve-racking presentation.

### The false consensus effect: my view feels common

Ross, Greene and House (1977) asked students to walk around campus wearing a sandwich board reading "Eat at Joe's." Those who agreed thought most others would agree too; those who refused thought most others would refuse. **Each side assumed it was the majority.**

**Check question**: "What reasons might someone have for choosing differently?"

### Self-serving bias: success is my doing, failure is the situation's

This is the tendency to credit our skill when things go well and blame luck or others when they do not (Miller & Ross, 1975). A meta-analysis of 266 studies (Mezulis et al., 2004) found the tendency clear across cultures but weaker in East Asian samples. It protects self-esteem, but it can also block learning from failure.

**Check question**: "If this had happened to someone else, what would I say caused it?"

### The Dunning-Kruger effect: the less skilled overrate themselves?

Kruger and Dunning (1999) reported that on logic, grammar and humour tasks, **participants in the bottom quartile rated themselves above average**. Their interpretation: lacking a skill also means lacking the ability to see what is missing.

Two things are worth knowing before using it:

- The popular "Mount Stupid → valley of despair" curve **does not appear in the original paper**.
- Later work pointed out that much of the pattern can arise as a **statistical artefact** — regression to the mean plus everyone's tendency to rate themselves a little above average (Gignac & Zajenkowski, 2020). "Unskilled people are always overconfident" is a stronger claim than the evidence supports.

**Check question**: "Is there an outside benchmark — a test, expert feedback — that can check my judgement?"

## Illusions that make others look simple

### The fundamental attribution error: blaming others' character

Jones and Harris (1967) had participants read an essay supporting Castro, telling them the writer had been **assigned** that position. Participants still concluded the writer truly supported Castro. Ross (1977) named this tendency to explain others' behaviour by character rather than situation the **fundamental attribution error**.

When a car cuts in front of you, the driver seems rude; when you cut in, you remember the urgent reason. It is the same asymmetry.

**Check question**: "In their situation, what would I have done?"

### The just-world belief: looking for a reason in the victim

In Lerner and Simmons's (1966) experiment, participants watched someone receive what appeared to be unfair electric shocks (it was acted). When there was no way to help, participants actually **rated the victim less favourably**. Lerner (1980) explained this as a wish to believe that the world is fair and people get what they deserve — the **belief in a just world**.

The belief offers hope that effort is rewarded, but it slides easily into blaming the victims of accidents, crime or poverty.

**Check question**: "What factors were outside this person's control?"

## Checklist at a glance

| Illusion | Check question |
|---|---|
| Egocentric bias | How much would the other person say they did? |
| Spotlight effect | How busy is everyone with their own concerns? |
| False consensus | What reasons might someone have for choosing differently? |
| Self-serving bias | If it happened to someone else, what would I say caused it? |
| Dunning-Kruger | Is there an outside benchmark to check my judgement? |
| Fundamental attribution error | In their situation, what would I have done? |
| Just-world belief | What was outside this person's control? |

These illusions work not only when we judge others but also when we reassure or berate ourselves. Slipping in one question can reduce both misunderstandings between people and needless self-blame.
