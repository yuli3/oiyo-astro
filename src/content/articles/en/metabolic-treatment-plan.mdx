> This article is general information for understanding a research idea. It is not medical advice and does not replace diagnosis or treatment. **Never stop or change psychiatric medication without your doctor.** Talk to your doctor before starting a ketogenic diet, fasting, or any major diet change, especially if you take lithium or diabetes medication, are pregnant or breastfeeding, or have a history of an eating disorder. If you are in crisis, contact local emergency services right away.

### Introduction: An Interesting Hypothesis, Not a Do-It-Yourself Plan

If Dr. Christopher Palmer's **Brain Energy** idea resonated with you, a natural next question is: "So what should I do?"

The honest answer is that this is still a **hypothesis**. Palmer (2022) proposes that many mental disorders may involve problems with energy metabolism in the brain. Research is testing that idea, but it has not been established, and metabolic approaches are not a proven replacement for standard psychiatric treatment.

So this article is not a treatment plan to run on your own. Instead, it lays out **five topics you can bring to your doctor** if you are curious about metabolic approaches.

---

### Where the Evidence Stands

- **Lifestyle in general:** The evidence that exercise, sleep, diet, and not smoking support mental health is relatively solid (Firth et al., 2020).
- **Ketogenic diets for serious mental illness:** Studies are at an early stage. For schizophrenia and bipolar disorder, the evidence comes mainly from small pilot trials, such as a single-arm study without a control group (Sethi et al., 2024). Results like these suggest the idea is worth testing in larger, controlled trials; they do not show that it works for everyone, or that it is safe without supervision.
- **The mitochondria explanation:** Linking all of this to mitochondrial function is Palmer's proposed mechanism. It has not been confirmed in people.

---

### Topic 1: Your Current Metabolic Health

It is reasonable to ask your doctor about your metabolic health, both because it matters for overall health and because some psychiatric medications can affect weight, blood sugar, and blood fats.

- **Measures to ask about:** Weight, waist circumference, blood pressure, blood sugar, and blood lipids such as triglycerides
- **Habits worth noting:** Your usual diet (sugar and processed food intake), sleep, physical activity, and stress
- **How you have been feeling:** Mood changes, anxiety, concentration, and fatigue

A simple record like this makes the conversation with your doctor more concrete. It is a starting point for discussion, not a self-diagnosis.

### Topic 2: Medication Safety

**Caution: If you take psychiatric medication, never stop or adjust it on your own.** Stopping suddenly can cause relapse or withdrawal effects.

Diet changes can also change how medication works in your body. A ketogenic diet in particular is a significant medical intervention and can be risky with some medications and conditions, for example:

- **Lithium:** Shifts in fluid and salt balance can change lithium levels in the blood.
- **Diabetes medication:** Insulin and some tablets can cause low blood sugar, and some (such as SGLT2 inhibitors) carry a risk of ketoacidosis.
- **Pregnancy or breastfeeding**
- **A history of an eating disorder:** Restrictive diets can trigger relapse.
- **Kidney, liver, pancreas, or gallbladder conditions**

If you are interested, tell your doctor something like, "I've been reading about the Brain Energy hypothesis and metabolic approaches. Is anything like this appropriate or safe for me?" If a ketogenic diet is ever tried, it should be under medical supervision, ideally with a dietitian and regular blood tests.

### Topic 3: What Kind of Diet Change, If Any

There is no single diet that suits everyone, and how restrictive to go is not something to decide by yourself based on how severe your symptoms feel.

- **General healthy eating:** Eating fewer processed foods and sugary drinks is low-risk for most people and fits general health advice.
- **More restrictive approaches:** Low-carbohydrate diets, intermittent fasting, and ketogenic diets are bigger changes. Ask your doctor whether they are appropriate for you, what the risks are, and whether there is a clinical trial or specialist service you could be referred to.

### Topic 4: Everyday Habits Around Diet

Diet is only one part of the picture. These habits are worth discussing too, and they have support for mental health regardless of the Brain Energy hypothesis:

- **Sleep:** Regular, sufficient sleep is closely tied to mood and concentration.
- **Physical activity:** Regular exercise is linked to better mood and lower risk of depression.
- **Stress management:** Methods such as relaxation, meditation, or yoga can help some people manage stress.
- **Alcohol and tobacco:** Cutting down helps both physical and mental health; ask for support if quitting is hard.

### Topic 5: What to Monitor, and When to Get Help

If you and your doctor agree to make changes, decide together what to keep track of and how often to check in.

- Energy, sleep, and mood over time
- Physical changes such as dizziness, weakness, or unusual thirst
- **Warning signs** such as worsening mood, thoughts of self-harm, or signs of mania: contact your doctor promptly, or emergency services if it is urgent

---

### Wrapping Up: Curiosity Is Fine, Just Bring It to Your Doctor

The Brain Energy hypothesis is an interesting attempt to connect mental health with metabolism, and research on it is ongoing. But it is not yet a proven treatment, and no diet can promise recovery.

**Small steps can still be a good start.** For most people, drinking one fewer sugary drink or walking 10 more minutes a day is a reasonable change. For anything bigger, especially a ketogenic diet, start with a conversation with your doctor.
