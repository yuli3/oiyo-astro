## Where did attachment theory begin?

The British psychiatrist John Bowlby (1969), observing war orphans and hospitalised children, proposed that children have an **instinctive system for staying close to a protective person** when they feel threatened. This is **attachment**. A child uses a reliable person as a "secure base" to explore the world and returns to them when frightened.

Mary Ainsworth (Ainsworth et al., 1978) observed this with the **Strange Situation procedure**. Watching how babies aged 12–18 months reacted to brief separations from and reunions with their mothers, she identified three patterns.

- **Secure**: upset at separation, but quickly comforted on reunion and back to play.
- **Anxious-ambivalent**: very distressed at separation and hard to soothe even after reunion.
- **Avoidant**: seems barely to react to separation or reunion, though the body's stress response still appears.

Later researchers added **disorganised attachment**, marked by confused and inconsistent responses, as a fourth pattern.

## Is adult love attachment too?

Using a newspaper survey of adult romance, Hazan and Shaver (1987) showed similar patterns in romantic relationships. About 56% of respondents described themselves as secure, 25% as avoidant and 19% as anxious.

Current research looks at people not as types but as **scores on two dimensions** (Brennan, Clark & Shaver, 1998).

- **Attachment anxiety**: how much you worry about being abandoned and how sensitive you are to your partner's responses.
- **Attachment avoidance**: how uncomfortable closeness feels and how much you avoid depending on others.

Those who **cling** when contact drops often score high on anxiety, while those who **go quiet** as things get closer often score high on avoidance. When these two meet, a vicious cycle easily forms: the more one approaches, the more the other retreats. ## Can attachment change?

The claim that childhood attachment stays the same for life is an exaggeration. In Fraley's (2002) meta-analysis, the link between infant attachment and later attachment was **moderate** — meaning there is continuity, but many people change.

- **Earned security**: some people with difficult childhoods develop secure ways of relating by reflecting on and making sense of those experiences (Roisman et al., 2002).
- **The power of relationships**: reliable partners, friends or therapeutic relationships can help reduce attachment anxiety and avoidance.

Attachment styles work best as **a map for understanding yourself**. Used as labels that box you or others in — "I'm avoidant, so I can't help it" — they narrow relationships instead.

## Summary at a glance

| Topic | What research says | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Bowlby's attachment | A system for seeking a protector under threat | Not about whether you deserve love |
| Strange Situation | Secure, anxious, avoidant (+ disorganised) | A classification from observing infants |
| Adult attachment | Scores on anxiety and avoidance | Look at scores rather than type labels |
| Change in attachment | Moderate stability; change is possible | "Fixed for life" is an exaggeration |
