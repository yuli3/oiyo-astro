## Three questions developmental psychology asks

Developmental psychology studies **how and why people change** from birth to death. The field has long revolved around three questions.

- **Nature and nurture**: which matters more, genes or environment? A meta-analysis of some 2,700 twin studies spanning 50 years found an average heritability of about 49% across human traits (Polderman et al., 2015). Genes and environment contribute roughly equally, and they influence each other.
- **Continuity and discontinuity**: does development accumulate gradually, or jump in stages like a staircase?
- **Critical and sensitive periods**: are there abilities that can never be learned if a certain window is missed? Today researchers more often speak of **sensitive periods**, windows when learning comes most easily, rather than strict "critical periods."

## How development is studied

To see change with age, research design matters.

| Design | Method | Strength | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cross-sectional | Compare several age groups at once | Fast and cheap | Age effects mix with **cohort effects** (generational differences) |
| Longitudinal | Follow the same people over time | Shows individual change directly | Cost, dropout, practice effects |
| Sequential | Follow several cohorts at once | Separates age and cohort effects | Most complex and expensive |

For example, if you compare intelligence test scores of people in their 20s and 70s at a single point in time, the older group tends to score lower. But part of that gap may come not from age but from **generational differences in schooling**. Schaie's Seattle Longitudinal Study used a sequential design to separate the two and showed that many intellectual abilities hold up relatively well into the 60s (Schaie, 2005).

## Cognitive development: Piaget and beyond

### Piaget's four stages

Piaget held that children grow by building **schemas** through interaction with the world, fitting new experiences into existing schemas (**assimilation**) and changing schemas to fit experience (**accommodation**).

| Stage | Approx. age | Key achievements |
|---|---|---|
| Sensorimotor | 0–2 | Object permanence; the beginnings of representation |
| Preoperational | 2–7 | Explosion of symbols and language; egocentrism; lack of conservation |
| Concrete operational | 7–11 | Conservation, classification, logic about concrete objects |
| Formal operational | 11+ | Hypothetico-deductive reasoning, abstract thought |

### Critiques of Piaget

- **He underestimated infants**: using the **violation-of-expectation paradigm** — infants look longer at surprising events — Baillargeon (1987) reported that 3- to 4-month-olds seem to expect hidden objects to continue to exist. Whether looking time can be read as "understanding" remains debated.
- **Stages are less clear-cut than proposed**: the same child can behave as if in different stages depending on the task, so modern research tends to see development as **domain-specific and gradual**.
- **The role of culture and schooling**: formal operations are not a stage every adult reaches automatically; they depend heavily on educational experience.

### Vygotsky: thinking that grows in society

Vygotsky argued that cognitive development begins in **social interaction**. He called the range of tasks a child cannot do alone but can do with help the **zone of proximal development (ZPD)**. Wood, Bruner and Ross (1976) named the process of adults fitting their help to the child's level and gradually withdrawing it **scaffolding**.

### Theory of mind: others think differently from me

**Theory of mind** is the understanding that others can hold beliefs, desires and intentions different from one's own. In Wimmer and Perner's (1983) **false-belief task**, many children around ages 4–5 became able to answer, "They don't know, so they'll look in the wrong place." Baron-Cohen, Leslie and Frith (1985) used the "Sally-Anne task" to study theory of mind in autistic children.

## Socioemotional development: attachment and temperament

In Harlow's (1958) experiments with infant monkeys, the monkeys clung far longer to a **soft cloth mother** than to a wire mother that provided milk — evidence that attachment is not explained by feeding alone. Bowlby's and Ainsworth's attachment theory and research on adult attachment are covered in detail in the [article on attachment theory](/en/attachment-theory-research-and-adult-love/).

Babies differ in **temperament** from birth. In the New York Longitudinal Study, Thomas and Chess (1977) grouped infants as "easy," "difficult" and "slow-to-warm-up." Kagan showed that **behavioural inhibition** — shrinking back from the unfamiliar — persists to a degree but changes with parenting and experience. What matters is the **goodness of fit** between temperament and environment.

## Language development

Babies progress from babbling (around 6 months) to first words (around 12 months) and two-word combinations (18–24 months), after which vocabulary grows explosively.

- **Nativism**: seeing children master complex grammar from limited input, Chomsky argued that humans have an innate capacity for language.
- **Usage-based theory**: Tomasello (2003) explained that children build grammar by reading others' intentions and extracting patterns from expressions they hear often.
- **Evidence for sensitive periods**: Johnson and Newport (1989) found that the younger immigrants were when they arrived in the US, the higher their English grammar judgement scores; those who arrived after puberty scored lower and varied more.

## Adolescence: identity and the changing brain

Erikson saw the central task of adolescence as **identity versus role confusion**. Marcia (1966) classified identity status into four types depending on the presence of **exploration** and **commitment**.

| | Committed | Not committed |
|---|---|---|
| **Explored** | Identity achievement | Moratorium |
| **Not explored** | Foreclosure (a path set by others) | Identity diffusion |

The **dual systems model** is often invoked to explain why adolescents take more risks: a reward-sensitive system becomes highly active around puberty, while the prefrontal system that regulates impulses matures slowly into the mid-20s (Steinberg, 2008). Risky choices rise especially **when peers are watching**. The model has been criticised as a simplification, though, so interpretations that treat teenagers as "helpless because their brains aren't finished" call for caution.

## Adulthood and old age

### Does intelligence decline with age?

Horn and Cattell (1967) divided intelligence into two kinds.

- **Fluid intelligence**: the ability to solve novel problems quickly. It peaks in the early-to-mid 20s and declines gradually.
- **Crystallised intelligence**: accumulated knowledge and vocabulary. It is maintained or even grows into later middle age.

### Why people get happier with age

According to Carstensen's (Carstensen et al., 1999) **socioemotional selectivity theory**, the shorter people feel their remaining time is, the more they focus on **emotionally meaningful relationships** rather than gathering new information. Older adults may therefore show a **positivity effect**, attending to and remembering positive more than negative information (Mather & Carstensen, 2005). This is considered one reason emotional well-being in later life is higher than many expect.

## Check questions

**Question 1.** In 2026, a researcher gathers 20-, 50- and 80-year-olds and compares their digital skills; the older the group, the lower the score. What is wrong with concluding that "digital skills decline with age"?

**Answer.** This is a cross-sectional study, so **cohort effects** are mixed in. The 80-year-olds grew up without digital devices, so the gap may reflect differences in experience rather than ageing. A longitudinal or sequential design is needed.

**Question 2.** Four-year-old Minsu watched his mother move chocolate from a drawer to a box. Asked where his younger brother, who didn't see this, will look for the chocolate, Minsu answers "the drawer." What ability has he shown?

**Answer.** He understood that his brother's belief can differ from reality. This **false-belief understanding** is a key marker of theory of mind.

**Question 3.** Which kind of task is a translator in their 70s more likely to do better than someone in their 20s: one relying on fluid or crystallised intelligence?

**Answer.** A task drawing on vocabulary and background knowledge. That depends on **crystallised intelligence**, which is maintained or can grow with age.
