Color reaches you before a sentence does. A red price tag, a blue logo, a green package: each one borrows a familiar association. The phrase color psychology covers two things at once. One is research that keeps the situation specific. The other is a much larger set of marketing charts. They are not the same size.

<Callout type="info">
This page is not medical advice. A wall color does not treat blood pressure, mood, appetite, or eye strain. Where an effect shows up in a study, it is usually small, tied to that task, and not always replicated.
</Callout>

## Basic principles

Research talks about two ways a color picks up meaning.

**Meaning in context.** Elliot and Maier (2014) describe color as something whose meaning changes with the situation. Red before a test is not the same signal as red on a card. When an experiment finds a difference, it is often a small one, bound to that task. Color is not a switch.

**Learned custom.** In many weddings in Europe and the Americas, white has been the color of the ceremony. In many traditional funerals in Korea, China, and Japan, mourners have worn white. It is not accurate to say, in one line, that white means death in East Asia and purity in the West. In East Asia today, white is also snow, a clinic corridor, and empty space in a product. Meaning is a custom, not a fixed translation.

Popular charts add claims that the research does not support.

- A common line says red raises heart rate and blood pressure, and blue lowers them, as if color were a switch. Kaiser (1984) reviewed measures that include heart rate and blood pressure. He concluded that some bodily responses to color can be recorded, and that some of those responses may come indirectly, through what the color means to the person, rather than as a simple reflex. That is not the chart's on-off rule, and it is not a reason to treat blood pressure with paint.
- Some charts say yellow raises serotonin. Looking at a yellow surface has not been shown to do that. Calling serotonin a happiness hormone is also too simple.
- The idea that green is the easiest color for the eye mixes in a different fact. In daylight, the eye's sensitivity to brightness peaks near yellow-green light, around 555 nm. That is sensitivity. It does not mean a green wall rests tired eyes.
- Appetite claims conflict with each other. It is true that many fast-food logos use red and yellow. It is not an established fact that they do so because red creates hunger. In one experiment, red reduced snack and soft-drink intake, which fits a stop signal better than a hunger switch (Genschow, Reutner, and Wänke, 2012). Eating less under blue light is a diet-blog claim, not a settled result.

## The colors people ask about

The associations below are impressions that branding and design often hope for. Wearing or painting a color does not change your personality or your body into that impression.

### Red

**Common associations**: passion, energy, danger, love, urgency.

**What the research can actually say**:
- In achievement settings, seeing red before a cognitive task has been linked with lower scores, read alongside avoidance motivation (Elliot et al., 2007). That is not a finding that red produces aggression.
- In one experiment, red raised the attractiveness ratings that heterosexual men gave to photographs of women (Elliot and Niesta, 2008). Those are lab ratings, not a script for a first date.
- Hill and Barton (2005) reported that athletes in red won more often in some Olympic combat sports. Their note does not separate better performance from referees or opponents reacting to the color. Do not read it as evidence that red gear improves athletic ability.

**A common design impression**: a large field of red feels intense to many people. It is not a treatment for stress or aggression.

**Brands that use it**: Coca-Cola, YouTube, Netflix, H&M. McDonald's and KFC also use red in the logo. A logo color is a choice about being seen. It is not evidence from an appetite experiment.

---

### Blue

**Common associations**: trust, steadiness, calm, competence. Banks and technology companies often pick blue for that reason. The choice is an industry habit.

**What the research can actually say**: In Mehta and Zhu (2009), blue beat red on a creative task, and red beat blue on a detail task. That runs against the one-page chart that says blue means focus and yellow means creativity. One pair of tasks is not a rule for office walls. Effects like these are small, and they do not always replicate.

This article does not list lower blood pressure, or less appetite under blue light, as research findings.

**A common design impression**: a large blue field is often called calm, and sometimes cold. That is not a reading of someone's emotions.

**Brands that use it**: Facebook, Samsung, LinkedIn, Dell. The BMW roundel is blue and white.

---

### Yellow

**Common associations**: warmth, optimism, caution, being easy to see.

**What the research can actually say**: chart language about yellow improving memory, improving concentration, or producing serotonin is unsupported. Warning signs use yellow because it stays visible at a distance. Visibility is not a hormone effect.

**A common design impression**: some people find a large area of bright yellow restless. That does not make yellow a cause of an anxiety disorder.

**Brands that use it**: IKEA uses blue and yellow. McDonald's uses red and yellow. Snapchat's ghost and the National Geographic border are yellow.

---

### Green

**Common associations**: plants, growth, safety, and the go signal on a traffic light. Finance sometimes uses it as a metaphor for growth. Brands also borrow it to suggest care for the environment. A green logo does not prove that care.

**What the research can actually say**: Lichtenfeld, Elliot, Maier, and Pekrun (2012) reported better creative performance when people saw a brief green cue just before a creativity task, compared with some other colors. The researchers were then working in Germany and the United States. The result is not that a green room makes a person creative. It is a short-cue experiment.

The eye-strain correction is in the principles above. A walk among trees and a can of green paint are different topics. This article does not say that green paint treats stress.

**Brands that use it**: Whole Foods, Starbucks, John Deere. The BP mark is green and yellow.

---

### Purple

**Common associations**: royalty, mystery, luxury, creativity. These are cultural associations.

**What history can actually say**: Tyrian purple, made from murex shells in the ancient Mediterranean, was extremely expensive. Roman sumptuary custom tied the fullest purple to high rank and, later, the emperor. That is a history of a Mediterranean dye, not a law of every ancient culture, and not an experiment showing that purple causes creativity.

**A common design impression**: some designers say a lot of purple looks artificial. That is a matter of taste, not a measured side effect.

**Brands that use it**: Cadbury, Milka, and the Yahoo wordmark. Hallmark's familiar mark is red and gold, so it is not on this list.

---

### Black

**Common associations**: formality, luxury, mourning in much of Europe, authority.

**A common design impression**: black clothing is often said to look slimming. That is a styling habit about outline and contrast, not a change in the body. A black field can also feel heavy or solemn. Same kind of impression.

**Brands that use it**: Chanel uses a great deal of black, and the Nike swoosh is often black. Apple has used black, white, and gray. Louis Vuitton's well-known canvas is brown and gold, so it is not listed here as a black brand.

---

### White

**Common associations**: they depend on the place. White shows up in weddings and hospitals in many regions, in traditional East Asian mourning dress, and in product design that wants empty space.

**A common design impression**: people often choose white walls when they want a room to feel larger, and when they want a clean look. Those are interior habits. White does not heal illness.

**Where you see it**: Apple's product design has used white for a long time. Many hospitals choose white for a clean impression. That is an institutional habit, not evidence of healing.

---

### Orange

**Common associations**: energy, warmth, approachability. Restaurant branding sometimes ties it to appetite. That tie is a marketing habit, not a demonstrated hunger mechanism.

**Brands that use it**: Fanta, and Harley-Davidson alongside black. Burger King's familiar mark is warm red and yellow. Amazon's letters are black; only the smile arrow is orange. It is hard to call the whole of Amazon an orange brand.

---

### Pink

**Common associations**: sweetness, care, and a code of femininity built by twentieth-century marketing. That gender code is fashion history, not a property of the color.

**What the research can actually say**: In 1979 Alexander Schauss proposed that one bright pink, Baker-Miller pink, briefly lowered aggression and physical strength. Some detention rooms were painted with it. In a short 1987 comparison, people in a pink room reported lower state anxiety than people in a red room, but grip strength and motor precision did not differ (Profusek and Rainey, 1987). That support is narrow and minimal. It is not a treatment, and this article does not recommend the color.

**Brands that use it**: Barbie, Victoria's Secret, and T-Mobile's magenta. PINK, the Victoria's Secret line, is the same family, so it is not listed separately.

## Color in ordinary use

The three tables below are colors you often see in interiors, clothes, and brands. They are not results tables from psychology experiments. The impression in each row is what a designer hopes for, not a measured effect.

### Interiors

| Place | Colors people often choose | Impression they are aiming for |
|------|---------|------|
| Bedroom | Blue, lavender, low-saturation green | Calm, the way people want a place for sleep to feel |
| Study or office | Blue, green | Quiet. Not a treatment for concentration |
| Living room | Warm neutrals, green | Easy to stay in, easy to talk in |
| Kitchen | Yellow, orange | Warmth. A rise in appetite is not an established effect |
| Bathroom | Blue, white, green | Clean and cool |

Blue in the study row is a habit of treating blue as a quiet color. In Mehta and Zhu (2009), blue sat with the creative task instead. A wall color cannot produce concentration.

### Clothes

| Situation | Colors people often choose | Impression they are aiming for |
|------|---------|------|
| Job interview | Navy, gray, white | A tidy, steady business code |
| First meeting | Red, pink | A popular styling habit |
| A presentation | Blue, black | Formality |
| Creative work | Yellow, purple | A common studio choice |

Wear red on a first date is styling talk, and only that. The attractiveness ratings in Elliot and Niesta (2008) are scores from a specific experiment, not the outcome of a date. Creativity does not switch on with a shirt. The short laboratory cues that do exist are a glimpse of green before a creativity task (Lichtenfeld et al., 2012) and a creative task that went better in blue than in red (Mehta and Zhu, 2009). Do not rebuild a wardrobe from either result.

### Branding and marketing

| Field | Colors you often see | Impression they are aiming for |
|------|---------|------|
| Finance and law | Blue, black | An industry code for trust and authority |
| Health and medicine | Green, blue | A code that looks safe. Not a clinical effect |
| Food | Red, yellow, orange | Being seen, plus a category habit. Appetite research is mixed |
| Environment and organic goods | Green, brown | A category code. The color is not proof of the practice |
| Luxury | Black, gold, purple | A code that aims at exclusivity |

<Callout type="tip">
**60-30-10 is an interior-design convention.** The split of about 60 percent dominant color, 30 percent secondary color, and 10 percent accent is a proportion often taught in decorating classes. It is not a result from a psychology experiment. It can still be a useful habit when you want a room not to look scattered.
</Callout>
