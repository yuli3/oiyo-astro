## Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT): the loop of thoughts, feelings and actions

Aaron Beck (Beck et al., 1979) observed that depressed people were caught in **automatic thoughts** that interpreted themselves, the world and the future negatively, and developed **cognitive therapy**. Adding behavioural techniques produced today's **cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT)**.

The core of CBT is finding the links you can change in the loop of **situation → thought → feeling → action**, and practising.

- **Thought records**: write down the situation, the thought that came up and how intense the feeling was when your emotions swung strongly.
- **Spotting cognitive distortions**: notice common thinking traps such as "I made one mistake, so I always fail" (overgeneralisation) or "Everyone must hate me" (mind reading) (Burns, 1980).
- **Alternative thoughts**: weigh the evidence and rewrite the thought as a more balanced statement.
- **Behavioural experiments**: test in small ways whether a feared prediction actually comes true.

When Hofmann et al. (2012) reviewed 269 meta-analyses, CBT had especially strong evidence for **anxiety disorders, somatic symptoms, bulimia, anger and stress**. It is not equally effective for every problem, however, and some studies find it roughly on par with other treatments for depression.

## Radical acceptance: the skill of no longer fighting

"Radical acceptance" is a core skill in Marsha Linehan's **dialectical behaviour therapy (DBT)** (Linehan, 1993). It means **fully acknowledging "this is how it is"** about a reality you cannot change, even if you don't like or agree with it, to reduce the suffering that comes from fighting reality. The meditation teacher Tara Brach linked it to Buddhist meditation for a wide audience in *Radical Acceptance* (2003).

**Acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT)**, in a similar vein, helps people accept uncomfortable thoughts and feelings rather than struggle to eliminate them, and act according to what they value (Hayes, Strosahl & Wilson, 1999). A meta-analysis of randomised trials found ACT effective for anxiety, depression, chronic pain and more (A-Tjak et al., 2015).

Acceptance is **not the same as giving up**. It means changing what you can and spending less energy on what you can't. If you're curious how you treat yourself, OIYO's [self-compassion test](/en/self-compassion-test/) may help.

## *The Body Keeps the Score* and the evidence for trauma treatment

The psychiatrist van der Kolk's *The Body Keeps the Score* (2014) resonated widely with its message that trauma lives not only in thoughts but in **bodily reactions and sensations**. It introduced approaches such as yoga, **somatic** methods that attend to bodily sensation, and EMDR.

From the research, it can be summarised like this:

- **Treatments with the strongest evidence**: for post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), **prolonged exposure, cognitive processing therapy, trauma-focused CBT and EMDR** have been confirmed effective in multiple meta-analyses (Cusack et al., 2016).
- **Somatic Experiencing (SE)**: a small randomised trial found reduced PTSD symptoms (Brom et al., 2017), but there are still few studies.
- **The body's role**: it is widely accepted that trauma is entangled with bodily responses such as arousal, sleep and pain. Some claims in the book, though, have been criticised as running ahead of the research, so when choosing treatment, discuss **evidence-based methods** with a professional first.

## Summary at a glance

| Treatment | Core idea | Level of evidence |
|---|---|---|
| CBT | Practise changing the loop of thought, feeling and action | Strong for anxiety, stress and more |
| Radical acceptance (DBT) | Acknowledge unchangeable reality to reduce suffering | Evidence as a skill within DBT |
| ACT | Accept and act on values | Moderate effects across several problems |
| PTSD treatments | Exposure, cognitive processing, EMDR and others | Confirmed in multiple meta-analyses |
| Somatic Experiencing | Working through bodily sensation | Early stage; more research needed |
