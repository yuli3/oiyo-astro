## Check the conditions before applying a label

Cognitive biases describe systematic tendencies in judgment on particular tasks. They are not labels for people whose choices we dislike, or diagnoses based on one mistake. Heuristics can be useful shortcuts and can produce errors when they do not fit the situation.

These 20 entries include judgment biases and related phenomena such as changing attitudes, conformity and discomfort. They are not all the same kind of error. Unless identified as research, everyday examples and review questions are written by the editors.

[Kahneman's Nobel lecture page](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2002/kahneman/lecture/) and [the lecture material held by a university](https://www.alice.id.tue.nl/references/kahnemann-2003.pdf) distinguish quick intuition from effortful checking while discussing skilled intuition and errors in deliberation.

## Distinguish 20 concepts in context

### 1. Confirmation bias

A tendency to seek supporting evidence or give insufficient attention to contrary evidence. [Wason's rule-discovery study abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1080/17470216008416717) describes a classic task. Seek cases that distinguish competing explanations, not just cases consistent with yours. This does not mean accepting poor evidence simply because it opposes your view.

### 2. Availability heuristic and bias

Using readily recalled examples to judge frequency or likelihood. A recent accident coming easily to mind does not establish that it is the most common accident. Comparisons need data for comparable populations, periods and denominators. Separate ease of recall from observed frequency. [Source for this definition](https://www.alice.id.tue.nl/references/kahnemann-2003.pdf).

### 3. Anchoring

An initial value or reference point influences later estimates. In an editorial example, “Original price 100 → sale 50” may make 50 look cheap, but comparable products and quality need separate checking. Whether 100 was a real selling price matters too. Having an initial figure is not, by itself, evidence of an error. [Source for this definition](https://www.alice.id.tue.nl/references/kahnemann-2003.pdf).

### 4. Loss aversion

Weighting losses more heavily than equivalent gains relative to a reference point. It is not a law that everyone's pain is always exactly twice their pleasure. [Research on its boundaries](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1509/jmkr.42.2.119.62292) distinguishes purposes of money and transaction contexts. Continuing because of an unrecoverable past cost is a different definition: the sunk-cost fallacy.

### 5. Status quo bias

The existing choice receives extra weight because it is existing. [Samuelson and Zeckhauser's paper](https://rzeckhauser.scholars.harvard.edu/sites/g/files/omnuum4441/files/rzeckhauser/files/status_quo_bias_in_decision_making.pdf) also discusses rational reasons to stay, including switching costs and uncertainty. Not changing is not sufficient evidence of bias. Compare the actual benefits and costs of changing.

### 6. Dunning–Kruger effect

[The original abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10626367/) reports overestimation among lower-performing groups on humor, grammar and logic tasks. It does not classify a person's knowledge or self-awareness in every domain. Check task-specific standards and feedback rather than confidence alone. Do not present a fixed internet confidence curve as a graph measured in that study.

### 7. Hindsight bias

After learning an outcome, treating it as more foreseeable at the time. [AHRQ's glossary](https://psnet.ahrq.gov/glossary/hindsight-bias) discusses problems this can cause in incident review. Compare an expectation recorded before the result with the explanation afterward. Do not require people to have known information unavailable at the time.

### 8. Groupthink

A group-decision problem in which preserving agreement suppresses dissent and scrutiny. [OpenStax's account of group influence](https://openstax.org/books/psychology-2e/pages/12-4-conformity-compliance-and-obedience) explains the concept. Agreement and good teamwork are not identical to groupthink. An editorial option is to record individual judgments and reasons before a leader announces a preference.

### 9. Fundamental attribution error

Overweighting personal characteristics and underweighting situational influences when explaining others' behavior. Before concluding that a late colleague is irresponsible, check the circumstances. [OpenStax's attribution discussion](https://openstax.org/books/psychology-2e/pages/12-1-what-is-social-psychology) distinguishes this from actor–observer differences. “My situation versus their personality” is not the whole definition.

### 10. Self-serving bias

Here we consider its attribution form: explaining success through one's abilities and failure through external factors. This does not imply that the true causes are always the opposite. Check whether the standards of explanation changed with the outcome, and whether the same evidence was considered for success and failure. [Source for this definition](https://openstax.org/books/psychology-2e/pages/12-1-what-is-social-psychology).

### 11. Hot-hand judgments

Believing that recent successes have increased the chance of another success. Independent chance and actual performance must be distinguished. [Miller and Sanjurjo's 2018 abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.3982/ECTA14943) reports bias in statistics selecting outcomes after streaks and a reinterpretation of classic findings. Neither universal independence nor a guarantee that any streak will continue follows.

### 12. Gambler's fallacy

Expecting the next outcome of an independent trial to compensate for earlier outcomes. If a coin remains fair on the next toss and that result is independent of the entire earlier sequence, three heads still leave a 50% chance of the next heads. Do not apply that number to every real process without checking its conditions.

### 13. Conformity and following the crowd

Adjusting behavior to others' judgments or group norms. People may seek acceptance or believe others have useful information. Conformity is not always an error. Distinguish multiple repetitions of the same claim from independent evidence. [Source for this definition](https://openstax.org/books/psychology-2e/pages/12-4-conformity-compliance-and-obedience).

### 14. Halo effect

An overall impression or evaluation on one dimension spills into an evaluation on another. A polished presentation does not, by itself, establish the accuracy of its data. The concept is not limited to appearance or celebrities. Seek direct evidence for the dimension being assessed. [Source for this definition](https://openstax.org/books/psychology-2e/pages/12-1-what-is-social-psychology).

### 15. Framing effect

[The classic abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7455683/) describes preferences changing with how a problem is presented. In a fictional editorial example, “90% pass” and “10% fail” describe the same proportion only for the same group and period. Different populations or times mean different information. Rephrase and compare the options' consequences on a common basis.

### 16. Present bias

Giving additional weight to immediate utility when the present is involved. [O'Donoghue and Rabin's paper](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/blogs.cornell.edu/dist/b/5495/files/2015/10/OD-Rabin-AERpnp-2015-x02qub.pdf) distinguishes ordinary discounting, uncertainty about future payment and constraints. Choosing one smaller immediate reward does not establish present bias.

### 17. Mere-exposure effect

Repeated exposure can affect liking. [Zajonc's 1968 published paper](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/biases/9_J_Personality_Social_Psychology_1_(Zajonc).pdf) discusses the need to examine conditions such as frequency, spacing and stimuli. Repetition does not guarantee liking. Distinguish familiarity from evidence of quality.

### 18. Cognitive dissonance

Discomfort arising from inconsistent attitudes, behavior or cognitions, and motivation to reduce it. [OpenStax's explanation](https://openstax.org/books/psychology-2e/pages/12-3-attitudes-and-persuasion) describes changing behavior or cognitions among several responses. Dissonance itself is not a lie or judgment error. Review the process separately if reducing discomfort involves ignoring contrary evidence.

### 19. Endowment effect

A tendency to value an object more highly when owning it than when not owning it. For example, the amount demanded to give it up may exceed the amount one would pay to acquire it. An existing object may have real use value and replacement costs, so unwillingness to sell does not establish an error. Consider how you would value obtaining the same object now. That question is not guaranteed to eliminate an endowment effect. [Source for this definition](https://benny.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jep.5.1.193).

### 20. Planning fallacy

Optimistic completion estimates focused on the current plan while underusing relevant past experience. [Buehler, Griffin and Ross's paper](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/biases/67_J_Personality_and_Social_Psychology_366,_1994.pdf) examines this. Not every delay is a bias. Compare actual times on similar tasks and differences in scope and resources. Past records do not guarantee accurate forecasts.

## Independence is an assumption the conclusion needs

If the next toss gives heads and tails equal probability and is independent of the entire earlier sequence, the next heads has probability 50%. It does not become more likely because heads is “overdue.” Removing an assumption does not supply enough information to calculate a different value. ## Review decisions through records and comparisons

In an editorial example, having spent many hours on a new task does not stop you reviewing the effort and benefit still ahead. Past hours cannot be recovered, but learning and reusable material may retain value. “Ignore past inputs” should not erase real remaining value.

## Distinguish what the sources establish

Papers and author abstracts support findings within their tasks, samples and conditions. OpenStax and AHRQ explain concepts; they did not experimentally test every example in this article. We do not present detailed methods or numbers from abstract-only papers as if their full texts were read. Each link supports a particular point, not all 20 concepts or the effectiveness of the review process.
