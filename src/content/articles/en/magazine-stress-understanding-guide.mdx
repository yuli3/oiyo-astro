## Stress is not the enemy

"Don't stress" is an impossible instruction. Stress is the response your body and mind switch on to adapt to change — without it you couldn't sense danger, meet deadlines, or feel the thrill of a new challenge.

The endocrinologist **Hans Selye**, often called the father of stress research, divided stress into two kinds:

- **Eustress** — good tension that lifts motivation and focus: the excitement of a new project, the pre-game jitters, the concentration a deadline brings.
- **Distress** — bad stress that exceeds your coping resources and leads to burnout: a load that feels uncontrollable, endless, and without recovery time.

The same presentation is eustress if you have time and resources to prepare, and distress if it overwhelms you. What often splits the two is **not the event itself but the ratio of event to resources**.

## Not what happened — what might happen

Interestingly, stress often grows less from what has already happened than from **anticipation of what might happen**. That's why the night before an exam is worse than the exam itself. Knowing this gives you room to notice — and break — the mental loop that keeps replaying an unarrived future.

## Yerkes and Dodson, 1908 — why zero stimulation is a hard goal

In 1908, Yerkes and Dodson reported that, in an animal learning task, a **middle stimulus strength** was tied to faster habit formation than a very weak or very strong one. The paper is about habit formation. It is not a law that explains human work stress in every job.

A later popular reading turns that result into an inverted-U for human performance.

- **Too little stimulation** can come with boredom and apathy.
- **A middle level of tension** is where some people feel focus come more easily.
- **Too much stimulation** can come with anxiety, freezing, and clouded judgment.

So wiping out stimulation is a hard goal to hold. The curve is not the same for every task or every person. In practice, the nearer aim is to **cut load that exceeds your resources and keep tension that still has recovery**.

## Burnout — stress that has piled up at work

In ICD-11, the WHO describes burnout as a syndrome conceptualized as resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed. It is an occupational phenomenon, not a medical diagnosis, and the term is not meant for life outside work. Three features are listed:

1. **Energy depletion** — fatigue that rest does not restore
2. **Mental distance from the job** — cynicism or detachment
3. **Reduced professional efficacy**

**Boreout** is a popular label for apathy when stimulation is almost gone. It is not a diagnosis. ([About burnout →](https://oiyo.net/en/burnout/about/))

## Put a number on your stress load

To move beyond a vague "I'm exhausted lately," measurement helps. The **life-events stress scale** developed by Holmes and Rahe in 1967 sums the changes you've experienced over the past year (moving, a new job, marriage — good events count too) to estimate your stress load.

👉 [Try the Holmes-Rahe stress test](https://oiyo.net/en/stress-events/test/)

If your score is high, read it not as a fault but as **a signal to secure more recovery time**. The scale is not a diagnosis. Strain in relationships can add to the load as well.
