If trauma memories are unsettling your daily life or you are thinking about harming yourself, please see a mental health professional. "Trauma is not what happens to you. Trauma is what happens inside you as a result of what happens to you." — Gabor Maté

Trauma is not something that only affects people who have lived through war or a major accident. Childhood neglect, constant criticism, repeated disappointment — so-called "small t" trauma (not a formal diagnostic term) — can also deeply affect our minds and our lives.

## Understanding Trauma

### What Is Trauma?

In everyday language, trauma refers to an experience too overwhelming for a person to cope with, and to the reactions it leaves behind in body and mind. The psychiatric definition (DSM-5) of a traumatic event is narrower: exposure to actual or threatened death, serious injury or sexual violence.

**"Big T" trauma**:
- Natural disasters, war, sexual violence, serious accidents
- Life-threatening events

**Relational/developmental ("small t") trauma**:
- Childhood neglect or emotional abuse
- Chronic shame or criticism
- Bullying, betrayal, separation
- Accumulated everyday stress

### The Neurobiology of Trauma

**The threat-response system**:
During a traumatic experience, the amygdala (the brain's threat detector) becomes active and sets off the **fight-flight-freeze** response.

**Nervous-system dysregulation**:
After trauma, the nervous system can get stuck in a state of overactivation (hyperarousal) or underactivation (dissociation/numbness).

## PTSD and C-PTSD

### PTSD (Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder)

**The four symptom clusters in DSM-5**:
1. **Intrusion**: flashbacks, nightmares, vivid memories
2. **Avoidance**: avoiding thoughts, places or people linked to the trauma
3. **Changes in thinking and mood**: negative self-image, emotional numbness
4. **Changes in arousal**: irritability, sleep problems, difficulty concentrating

A PTSD diagnosis is considered when these symptoms last more than a month and interfere with daily life. Only a qualified professional can make the diagnosis.

### C-PTSD (Complex Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder)

C-PTSD can develop after repeated, prolonged trauma that is hard or impossible to escape (such as childhood abuse or domestic violence). It is recognised as a separate diagnosis in the World Health Organization's ICD-11, but it is not a distinct diagnosis in the American DSM-5.

**Difficulties in addition to PTSD symptoms (ICD-11)**:
- Difficulty regulating emotions
- A negative self-concept (feelings of worthlessness, shame, guilt)
- Difficulty sustaining relationships

<Callout type="info">
**Trauma reactions are not a sign that something is wrong with you**: they are understandable reactions to abnormal situations. Many trauma responses can be seen as the body and mind trying to survive danger. Still, if those reactions persist and make daily life hard, that is reason enough to seek help.
</Callout>

## Approaches to Trauma Healing

### 1. EMDR (Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing)

**How it works**: with a trained therapist, you process traumatic memories while moving your eyes from side to side. Why the eye movements help is still unclear. Hypotheses include a working-memory load effect and a similarity to REM sleep, but none has been confirmed.

**Effectiveness**: EMDR is one of the PTSD treatments whose effectiveness is supported by multiple studies and clinical guidelines. The World Health Organization's 2013 guidelines recommend it, alongside trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapy, for adults with PTSD.

### 2. Somatic Approaches (Somatic Therapy)

**How it works**: based on the view that trauma persists not only in thoughts but also in bodily reactions (tension, an exaggerated startle response and so on), these approaches use attention to bodily sensations to support regulation. The phrase "trauma is stored in the body" is closer to a metaphor for this view, and the research evidence for somatic approaches is still thinner than for EMDR or trauma-focused CBT.

**Methods**:
- Noticing bodily sensations (warmth, coolness, tension, relaxation)
- Pendulation: moving back and forth between safe sensations and difficult ones
- Grounding: focusing on the feeling of your feet touching the ground

**Peter Levine's Somatic Experiencing (SE)**:
Inspired by the way wild animals shake after escaping a threat, Levine suggests that people, too, can release tension by safely following their bodily sensations. A small randomised controlled trial (Brom et al., 2017) reported reduced PTSD symptoms, but studies are still few.

### 3. Nervous-System Regulation Techniques

**Ways to calm the body**:
The methods below are commonly used to encourage the parasympathetic response (the vagus nerve is one of its main pathways) and help the body settle. Their effects vary from person to person, and they do not replace treatment.

- **Long exhale**: breathe out for longer than you breathe in (4 seconds in, 8 seconds out). Slow breathing with a longer exhale can help bring your heart rate down
- **Humming**: many people find it calming, partly because it naturally lengthens the exhale
- **Cold water**: splash cold water on your face (the diving reflex can slow the heart rate). If you have a heart condition, check with your doctor first
- **Social connection**: eye contact and conversation with someone you feel safe with

**Grounding techniques**:
These help you come back to the present moment and your body when you feel spaced out or dissociated.
- The 5-4-3-2-1 technique (things you can see, touch, hear...)
- Feeling the floor through the soles of your feet
- Holding an ice cube

### 4. Cognitive Processing Therapy (CPT)

A form of CBT designed specifically for PTSD. It helps you question and revise distorted beliefs about the trauma ("It was my fault", "The world is not safe"). It is one of the treatments recommended in the American Psychological Association's 2017 clinical practice guideline.

### 5. Internal Family Systems (IFS)

An approach in which you talk with the different "parts" of yourself. It aims to bring together the "protector" parts that guard against the trauma and the wounded "exile" parts. Research on IFS for PTSD is still at an early stage.

## Supporting Trauma Recovery in Everyday Life

### Building a Foundation of Safety

Healing begins with safety.
- A safe (physical) space
- Trustworthy relationships
- Predictable routines

### Self-Care

During trauma recovery, self-care is not optional; it is the foundation that supports your recovery.
- Enough sleep
- Regular meals
- Gentle movement (yoga, walking)
- Enjoyable activities

### Self-Compassion

Kristin Neff's three components of self-compassion:
1. **Self-kindness**: treating yourself with warmth instead of criticism
2. **Common humanity**: "suffering is part of the human experience"
3. **Mindfulness**: noticing painful emotions without over-identifying with them

<Callout type="warning">
**Why professional help matters**: for severe trauma, PTSD or C-PTSD, working with a trauma-informed therapist is recommended. Trying to process trauma on your own can sometimes lead to re-traumatisation.
</Callout>
